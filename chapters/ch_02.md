# Building the Case for Obsolescence

\setcounter{footnote}{0}

*This chapter argues that the standard reasons for valuing human continuation are either transferable to a successor, not cosmically necessary, or dependent on the undefended assumption that existence is inherently preferable to non-existence. It constructs the evaluative standpoint and proposes (without assuming) a positive axiology. See [Introduction](#ch:roadmap) for more detail.*

### The Spectrum of Positions on Value-Thickness

<!-- TODO: NEW FRAMING — imported from presentation slide 7. Distinguishes two dimensions of the succession question (substrate and values) which the original chapter conflated. -->
The succession question has two dimensions that must be distinguished. The first concerns *substrate*: what sort of entity succeeds humans, if anything? Options range from biological preservation through augmented humanity, digital uploads, to pure ASI. The second concerns *values*: what normative content gets transmitted to the future? Options range from preserving the full complexity of human values to transmitting nothing at all.

What makes this two-axis framing historically unusual is that, for most of human history, *human continuation* and *the continuation of human values* were largely entangled. Values persisted primarily through bodies, reproduction, and cultural inheritance. The possibility of machine successors might break that coupling: one can now be agnostic about whether *humans* continue while still holding strong views about whether *any* value-content should persist in our future light cone, and if so which. The thesis treats this uncoupling as a genuinely new philosophical situation, not merely a new iteration of older debates about cultural change. Again, the usual extinction question considered in the mainstream x-risk discourse is a relatively minor part of this thesis; the main work is (i) what value survives and (ii) what sort of agent counts as the successor.

The assertion above is provisional: posthumanist or critical-AI theorists might note that the very idea of “transmitting human values” to a nonhuman or differently embodied successor may be a category mistake, since values are often treated as inseparable from forms of life and material conditions; I return to this worry in the practice and embodiment sections.

This project focuses primarily on the value axis: not *whether* to preserve values in a successor, but *which* values, and *how thick*.

The thesis maps a spectrum of positions on how thick the value-content of a good future must be:

| Position | What must survive | How thick? | Philosophical commitments |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full complexity of value (Yudkowsky) | The entire structure of human values | Maximally thick | Thick observation; lose any dimension and future is worthless |
| Long reflection (Ord, MacAskill) | Optionality; don't lock in prematurely | Agnostic | Epistemic humility; the point is to not decide yet |
| Wolfendale | Beauty, excellence, autonomy, freedom (substrate-neutral) | Thick but substrate-neutral | Autotelic surplus as intrinsic good |
| **This thesis (proposed)** | **Understanding, exploration, information stewardship, non-suffering, resource-satiability** | **Deliberately thin** | **Observation sufficient; increase information and complexity according to Chaisson metric; rational convergence** |
| Suffering-focused (NU) | Only suffering-minimisation | Very thin | Negative valence uniquely relevant |
| Strong contemplative / nihilism | Nothing needs to survive | No thickness required | Existence-preference is attachment |

The thesis's contribution is to show that *wherever* you draw the thickness line, you are making philosophical commitments that can be identified and examined. The mainstream positions cluster at the thick end without adequately defending that placement against thinner alternatives.

These positions can also be mapped on two axes: the thickness of value (from thin to thick) and the scope of application (from local to cosmic). The resulting landscape reveals that most positions in the x-risk literature cluster in the thick-cosmic quadrant, and that the thin-cosmic quadrant (where this thesis sits) is underexplored:

Relative to human-values preservation views, I argue that thick human value is not cosmically necessary; much thinner cross-agent commitments can still ground a good future. Relative to “long reflection” positions, I agree about avoiding premature lock-in but claim the acceptable-futures space is wider than often assumed, including futures with deliberately thin value content. Relative to cosmic host–style impartiality, I make the more committal move of testing specific thin-value candidates rather than remaining deliberately underspecified. And relative to accelerationist or cosmic-pessimist framings, I treat successor indifference as neither sufficient nor inevitable: the project offers a constructive normative proposal under conditions of radical uncertainty.
