# Introduction

In 2026, as LLM-based systems approach human-level performance in domains like coding and mathematics even as they lag in others, a narrative can be heard. It goes as follows: "perhaps AI systems someday should or will succeed humanity as the principal agents dominating the future". I shall call such a view "successionism", following colloquial and emerging academic usage. This view remains controversial within the AI safety and research fields, but is increasingly discussed publicly and informally. I use the term in the sense that: 

- if AI systems become highly capable (to be defined further, but provisionally, "exceeding humans collectively in strategically consequential domains"); and 
- if they were to succeed humanity ("succeed" here means: such systems become the principal entities determining the long-run future, which might happen by humans going extinct or by being disempowered); 
- then this in itself (i.e. merely owing to the non-existence or non-influence of current humans) need not be a tragedy or be objectionable.

The thesis would be: that the consequent above is defensible, under certain assumptions. The thesis asks what are the assumptions necessary to regard AI succession as non-objectionable?

This usage of succesionism, is distinct from views that are predictive, i.e., that AIs will succeed owing to greater capabilities or other reasons; as well as from versions that argue succession would "good". One might hold reasonably the view that if the human species, or human cultures (including the things that are sometimes called human values), were not to survive into the future, this would be straightforwardly bad. However, disagreement about such a view, that was historically rather speculative, has become more salient, at least if one accepts that current AI systems are approaching human parity in some domains and that this trend might continue and broaden. In this chapter, I shall start by breaking this question into its parts and setting out why this seems uniquely important at the present historical moment. I shall next site the discussion in relevant discourses/audiences (§\ref{ch0:context}); give an overview of the methodology (§\ref{ch0:methodology}); followed by a sketch of a candidate solution to the project's problem (§\ref{ch0:obsolescence-arg}).

## The Question

Many people in relevant discourses (such as in @Ord2020;@ParfitRP;@MoynihanXR;@ware2021_on_extinction;@Thacker) believe that it would be bad if human beings were to go extinct. This thesis asks if such a belief has been examined carefully enough in the context of Artificial Intelligence (AI). 

The question of the project, can be approached in many ways. In order to target this inquiry, consider this decomposition:

- Q0a: Would it be bad if humans went extinct from a future world that contained advanced AI?
- Q0b: Would it be bad if humans did not go extinct, however they had approximately zero influence (provisionally defined as control of cosmic resources of energy and matter) on the course of the future?
- Q1: What sort of values might advanced AIs have, and can those actually be trained (or "loaded" as in @Bostrom2014) into AIs using current or foreseeable methods?[^ch0-value]
- Q2: Would it be bad if the thick, relational values that human beings are said to hold were not trained into  advanced AIs, and instead humans loaded in a thinner set of seed values and allowed AIs to develop their own evaluative framework?
- Q3: Is it morally defensible for humans to legislate or lock in values for entities (like AIs) that (might) have apparent features of rationality or agency, relationality, and perhaps sentience, since these are precisely the features that define (political/ethical) self-determination in today's world.

There are many undefined terms above. Below (in §\ref{ch0:definitions}) and in Ch. \ref{ch:setup}, I shall give provisional definitions for terms like "bad", "human beings", "values that humans hold", "advanced AI/superintelligence", "future world", "disappear"/"not present", "thin/thick values".

In this project, I shall primarily consider Q0a/b and Q2.^[Q1 motivates the alignment field, and it motivates this project, but  is (mostly) not my question. Similarly, I substantially leave Q3 to the model welfare literature, even though it motivates the practice element and the stakes of the project.] It will be useful to see how the questions above might further decompose, in slightly more technical terms:

- The analysis below will often invoke an (maximally) impartial perspective. What does this mean? Is there any philosophical support for adopting such an evaluative standpoint? This is treated in Chapters \ref{ch:setup} and \ref{ch:value-spectrum}.
- Some relevant literatures (longtermism and AI safety/alignment) assumes that human continuation is desirable on both species-relative and impartial grounds. What are the strongest justifications, and do they survive scrutiny?
	- In particular, is the assumption that existence is inherently preferable to non-existence itself a philosophical commitment that can be questioned?
- Where should the line be drawn between human contributions that are impartially justifiable and those that are parochial? What commitments does each placement depend on (Ch. \ref{ch:value-spectrum})?
- How plausible and useful is the cosmic host hypothesis [@Bostrom2024] in answering the questions above (Ch. \ref{ch:cosmic-host})?
- Methodologically:
	- How can thought experiments be used to illuminate the above, particularly where analytic philosophy fails (Ch. \ref{ch:setup})?
	- What do current frontier models do when pressed on these questions (via techniques like Constitutional AI), and what does that reveal about the conceptual landscape we are building into potential successors (Ch. \ref{ch:empirical-host})?
	- How does materialising the insights from technical investigation with models (like long-running self dialogue/critiques) into physical space alter a researcher's thinking about the thesis' questions? Are they of incremental value in answering the questions? (in Ch. \ref{ch:empirical-host}, the upgrade artwork, and below in \ref{ch:practice})

### Audience: Situating the Project

This project is centrally in dialogue with the study of human extinction (existential risk or x-risk [@Ord2020; @Moynihan2020]); the study of suffering on an astronomical scale (suffering risk or s-risk [@Vinding2020]), and the possibility that the future has significant value or disvalue, and the consequent moral stakes (longtermism [@MacAskillWWO; @Wolfendale2022; @ParfitRP; @moore1903principia]). As discussed above, the thesis directly engages a debate (circa 2025-2026) around "AI succession": the idea that humans might be replaced by AI systems, and that (according to some writers) this would not necessarily be a tragedy [@bailey2026norightful;@haggstrom2025successionism;@samuel2026people;@kulveit2025memetics;@laine2026ideology]. This partial summary can be read alongside the longer literature review below (§\ref{ch0:literature}).

These questions these substantial literatures raise are, in my view, sharpened in the context of advanced AI systems and the possibility of extraterrestrial intelligences (ETIs).

### Why This Matters Now


Several developments make the questions above pressing.

- First, as of 2026, AI capabilities have continued to improve on many benchmarks and applications.[@task-completion-time-horizons-of-frontier-ai-models] Systems that could not hold a coherent conversation five years ago now reason across domains, write code, and generate novel scientific hypotheses. The timeline to something approaching superintelligence is treated by many forecasters as potentially within decades (albeit contested).[^ch0-asitimeline]
- Secondly and from a methodological perspective, we now have entities that reason (or give a convincing and effective facsimile of reasoning) in somewhat coherent ways through the medium of language, especially in verifiable domains like code and mathematics. I do not claim that they can "do philosophy", which itself is an open question [@Moorhouse2026Philosophy; @benthamsbulldog2026ai]. This does, however, open the possibility of interacting with them and finding their opinions on issues that might affect them as well as their successors.
- Thirdly, the emergence of digital minds is now a live area of inquiry [^ch0-digitalminds] and asks whether AIs have morally relevant experiences. If they do, at least under certain viewpoints, we might be morally required to consider their welfare and preferences [@Shulman2021]. Further, the population of morally relevant entities may soon be overwhelmingly non-human, and this numerical dominance might changes the stakes of value design because the values embedded in current AIs could significantly shape the character of the future AIs (compared to the effect of human preferences alone). 



### Critical perspectives {#ch0:critical-ai}

This project exists in a discursive context (i.e. existential risk, longtermism, and AI alignment) that is *historically situated* with distinctive argumentative styles, institutional locations, and implicit philosophical commitments. In an arts and humanities context, it matters that these fields often speak in a register of planetary governance, civilisational strategy, and “the fate of the light cone”, making normative or value-laden statements, while drawing legitimacy or a veneer of neutrality from science and engineering. This thesis therefore treats these discourses both as a source of substantive arguments *and* as an object of critical analysis.

From such a critical perspective, three features stand out. 

- Firstly, the discourse is structurally tempted by a “view from nowhere”: a posture of maximal impartiality that can obscure its own parochial origins (funding structures, geopolitical context, and culturally local assumptions about agency, progress, and value). 
- Secondly, it is prone to moral abstraction: the replacement of lived, embodied, situated human practices with legible or calculable quantities (expected value, welfare aggregation, utility proxies), which is often methodologically necessary but might bring its own biases. 
- Lastly, it develops its own self-reinforcing imaginaries: narratives of takeoff, alignment, cosmic expansion, and successor agency that can reshape research agendas and public expectations in ways that are not merely predictive or neutral [@ahmed2024_field_building_ai_safety]. 

The above flavour of critiques are common, both within the fields of AI alignment, safety, and ethics [^ch0:safety-ethics-alignment], and outside: most memorably, Torres and Gebru identify problematic tendencies surrounding AI and AI researchers, conflated or clustered into what they term the "TESCREAL Bundle" (transhumanism, extropianism, singularitarianism, cosmism, rationalism, effective altruism, and longtermism, discussed in [@GebruTorres2024;benthamsbulldog2023tescreal;mcglynn2026unbundling]). Others such as Goldsmith's Dan McQuillan argue that contemporary AI inherits features of eugenics thinking, such as ranking/stratifying/measuring intelligence, creating a risk of fascistic social ordering [@McquillanAI]. Still others such as Peter Wolfendale argue that longtermism is foundationally flawed in that it tries to legislate for the distant future (i.e. it treats the future as a single optimisation target, and in Wolfendale's view this objecitonable, notwithstanding the complexity of the utility, welfare, or value function used), which is both epistemically and inferentially distant for current humans and; according to Wolfendale's axiology (which prioritises freedom, autonomy, diversity and aesthetic self-realisation), is ethically unjustifiable vis-à-vis the entities that will populate the future (see further discussion in \ref{ch:cosmic-host} and [@MacAskillWWO;Wolfendale2022]).


## Methodology {#ch0:methodology}

The questions above intersect AI with philosophy (particularly normative ethics, metaethics, philosophy of mind, and aesthetics). However, it is unclear whether intuitions, philosophical or otherwise, garnered within largely human contexts could be straightforwardly transferable to extremely unfamiliar situations (i.e. where there are few or no humans, or one where such intelligent-seeming beings as exist, are instead AI systems or ETIs). This inferential gap has been analysed extensively in the literature around thought experiments, i.e. [@gendler2002; @sep-thought-experiment; @vaninwagen1998modal; @hare1981moral]. 

More generally, at the level of metaethics and axiology, theories of value remain contested: for instance, the idea of an impartial or view-from-nowhere perspective is heavily challenged within and outside the analytic philosophy tradition; similarly, the definition of the good is only somewhat more clear now than when Plato, Sidgwick, or Moore were writing.[^ch0-analytic]

The aim here is not to solve these problems, but rather illuminate the dimensions over which this project's question might be analysed, a task for which one tool in the philosophical/scientific toolkit, the thought experiment, might be helpful. 

### Thought experiments as tool for counterfactuals

Most philosophical thought experiments have the structure of a scenario or scenarios, a set of "knobs" or parameters for variation of the scenario(s), an evaluative question for the reader, and then a verdict based on the reader's intuition as an evaluative standpoint [@dennett2013intuition].

Thought experiments are also used in more speculative ways, by the artist or literary writer. Their experiments are not as exhaustively propositional as those of the philosopher or scientist, but they allow the reader or the viewer to imagine, inhabit, or be immersed in, a world that is at an inferential distance (though mostly still within a frame involving humans or anthropomorphised non-humans). Through such immersion, so the argument goes, the reader/viewer can develop the intuitions that were not otherwise available to rigorous philosophical enquiry ([Elgin 2014](https://www.catherineelgin.com/undg/fictionte.pdf), [Carroll 2002](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1519970?aerror=SOCIAL401:register&merror=token_error), Noe, De Cruz). A particularly relevant case is science fiction, which extends the reach and sometimes tries to break the anthropomorphic vice ([Helen De Cruz, Johan De Smedt & Eric Schwitzgebel (eds.)](https://www.amazon.co.uk/Philosophy-through-Science-Fiction-Stories/dp/1350081213), [Swirski 2007](https://www.routledge.com/Of-Literature-and-Knowledge-Explorations-in-Narrative-Thought-Experiments-Evolution-and-Game-Theory/Swirski/p/book/9780415420600)). 

Such methods can help make otherwise "dry", schematic or unfamiliar worlds cognitively available to a wide range of readers. This gain comes at a price: style, narrative perspective and aesthetic treatment may alter the reader’s judgment independently of the world’s stipulated properties. This is the unavoidable result of foregrounding subjectivity. Of course subjectivity isn't absent from the sciences, mathematics, and philosophy; however, they try to control for subjectivity (in an investigator, investigative team, or across disciplines) through standardised description, explicit stipulation, controlled comparison, reproducibility, formal argument, considered objections and public criticism. These procedures do not eliminate perspective or value-laden judgment, but they render some of their effects more stable, inspectable, and corrigible.[^ch0-parfit-bio]

### Case study: the paperclip maximiser

In order to frame the gap this project fills, I now consider a historically influential and vivid example, that functions as an intuition pump (as defined by @dennett2013intuition) within the AI safety literature: the paperclip maximiser, discussed by Nick Bostrom [@Bostrom2014] and Eliezer Yudkowsky before him. I focus on this case because it most clearly shows the problem I'm trying to address in the experiment proposed below.

The paperclip example, if we were to regard it as a thought experiment, has a number of flaws. 

- One is that it is radically under-described: Bostrom says nothing about how the paperclips are produced, how varied they are, whether the entities that presumably produce them are still around, whether other things or beings ever existed in that universe?
- The framing skews the reader's intuition, i.e. filling the universe with paperclips sounds more trivial than "fill the universe with arbitrarily intricate crystallographic structures that vary by physical and temporal scales and are produced through complex algorithms" which evokes (in my view) architecture or art. This framing effect was analysed by Dennett regarding Searle's Chinese Room experiment [@dennett2013intuition], though Dennett's analysis covered both "knob turning" and "framing", a distinction teased apart by subsequent writers ([@SinnottArmstrong2008] discusses the methodology of thought experiments and how reliable the elicited heuristics might be, outside a given moral agent's world or situation).
- Perhaps a more substantive objection is that the paperclip maximiser conflates multiple possible sources of the reader's revulsion (assuming the reader is repulsed by such a future). These could be: 
	- (a) humanity is destroyed (catastrophe on species-prudential grounds); 
	- (b) the agent has preferences that might be regarded as problematic because:
		- it has a single goal;
		- its preferences are not satiable until all resources are exhausted;
		- it is unclear whether it could ever have revised or reflected upon its preferences (but presumably, cannot any longer do so given that everything has been turned into paperclips!);
	- (c) the terminal value (maximising the population of paperclips, squiggles, or, for that matter, intricate crystallographic structures) seems, to intuitive human value systems, low-ranking. But, depending on how one turns the knobs, the intuition might shift, at least for me. For instance, varying (c): 
		- hedonium, utilitarian, or computronium maximisers [@Bostrom2014] seem much less obviously bad. 
		- If we further relax (a) by stipulating that humanity is not destroyed, it seems to me the badness is further reduced.
	- (d) by stipulation, the paperclip world forecloses other possible futures because all resources have been converted to paperclips, and there is no one or nothing left to do anything; taken literally, evolution and even time might stop (if all large-scale physical processes stop e.g. star formation/death, space expansion, and so forth);
	- (e) Not only are humans and familiar earthly life nonexistent, there are also no other agents that might be characterised as experientially-capable, conscious or sentient. Put another way, by definition, the paperclip creating agent is intolerant of other preferences, and has committed the universe to one irreversible goal.

The weaknesses of the paperclip maximiser motivate what I'm trying to do in the thought experiment below: 

- Firstly I am trying to treat separately the questions of human extinction and loss of human values from the future, though they are very likely entangled; 
- Secondly, I am proposing resource-satiable AIs and resource-satiability as an explicit knob; 
- Thirdly, the future I propose is not necessarily pursuing a single fixed objective or goals that are familiar to humans. Rather, I posit AIs that develop their own values, but that simply are not human-shaped; these types of values are sometimes referred to, within the AI-safety or longtermism discourse, as "alien values" or, less neutrally, "locust values" [@carlsmith2025_human_like_safe_ai_motivations;@Carlsmith2024DeepAtheism;@ChristianoWUA]. 
- Finally, I make the evaluative standpoint explicit, constrained, and variable (in a Dennett knob sense), where the Bostrom-Yudkowsky versions tell the reader what criterion to apply, and strongly condition the reader to find the scenario repugnant.

#### Interlude: upon value

The question of what has value in worlds where there are no humans has a long history in Western philosophy, starting perhaps with Plato's Forms or even the Pre-Socratics; there are multiple types of value often discussed in philosophy: 

- instrumental value;
- final value (of things not instrumentally valuable for some other end); 
- intrinsic value (that inheres in the thing itself); 
- value of a state of affairs (which obtains even if there is no one to benefit or experience such state of affaris);
- non-welfarist value (that which cannot be reducible to welfare for a individual capable of experiencing pleasure/pain or other correlates of welfare);
- impersonal value (which benefits no particular person);
- relational value (which arises from the relations or relationships between entities who can make evaluative judgments with respect to value);

Directly relevant for this project is Moore's isolation test [@moore1903principia] which has an afterlife in longtermism (as well as environmental ethics and aesthetics). Moore's world, like Bostrom's paperclip world, stipulates that there are no humans; it focuses upon the idea of instrumental versus terminal or final values, goods, or goals. Moore asks: would a given state of affairs (most memorably in his work, beauty) still be valuable if it existed by itself, apart from its consequences, associations, effects on others?[^ch0-moore]

There are two further, related questions around value:

- is it meaningful to talk about value in worlds without valuers, which is often taken to mean conscious, experiencing entities?
- is it meaningful to talk about value where the entities that exist (who, in a given thought experiment, might be the evaluators or judgers of value) are not like current biological humans, where "biological humans" means: entities that are generally held to be conscious and capable of experience (bracketing what exactly those terms mean), and at any rate, have some common set of sensory, locomotive, cognitive, and other biological or physical processes?

This implies that worlds need to be assessed both on (a) what those worlds actually contain or stipulate, i.e., paperclip worlds versus crystal worlds versus familiar human worlds; as well as (b) the evaluator's standpoint or framework from where they are judged. As will be discussed in \ref{ch:value-spectrum}, this could be welfarist; could focus on objective goods; could seek to minimise suffering; could prioritise autonomy and relationality; or it could hold to a Moorean conception of intrinsic goods. However, the paperclip maximiser as a sparsely described vignette seems, in my view, not to possess a grain fine enough to distinguish between different worlds and assess the relative (dis-)value of each. I hope to do better using the framework below.

### Thought Experiment: An Atlas of Possible Worlds {#ch0:axiological-atlas}


#### Entries in the axiological atlas == future worlds

Assuming one agrees that the paperclip maximiser thought experiment as canonically posed does not tell us in any detail what is actually bad about a paperclip world, then it would seem that need a richer experiment, which I propose below: a structured possibility space or, as I will refer to it, the axiological atlas, is a device for comparing evaluative responses under explicit stipulations. This is not intended to assert that every world so described is metaphysically coherent or reachable (from our familiar Earthly world); on the other hand, I have tried to remain within the bounds of known physics.  Moreover, what is described below? is a *starting point*, to be elaborated, modified, or rejected through practice. 

The browser-based "atlas explorer" (§\ref{ch:manifold-explorer}) lets users (human or LLM), identify points of failure: departures from physical plausibility, logical incoherence, absurd philosophical commitments or known fallacies.

Some broad classes of scenarios/worlds are:

- **Familiar worlds:** To see this, start with something like our familiar world extended or extrapolated to the future. Humans never physically or semi-permanently settle on Mars; the asteroids, stars, and all Elon Musk's efforts in this respect are in vain. Imagine something like the status quo, where the planet is filled with humans and non-human life, perhaps in a greater degree of political and ecological harmony than today. 
- **Near-space colonisation:** These scenarios are where biological humans, perhaps with the aid of AIs (*circa* 2026 capability levels), and have established semi-permanent, self-sustaining settlements on some planets in this Solar System. 
- **Deep-space exploration / colonisation** 
	- These scenarios are where biological humans, assisted by AI, have set out on multi-decade missions to nearby stars.
	- In these scenarios, spacecraft piloted by AIs (or by humans who have been significantly upgraded with AI cognitive implants, or with the ability to halt cell damage or repair tissue, or who have been entirely uploaded to an AI) have, after decades, centuries, or millenia of traveling, established sustainable outposts on nearby stars.
	- A limiting case of the above is where humanity, likely in what Bostrom terms a "technologically mature" state which he defines as a stage of civilisation "where humankind has acquired most of the technological capabilities that one can currently show to be consistent with physical laws and with material and energy constraints", launches missions to explore a significnat fraction of the causally-reachable universe [@Bostrom2003b][^ch0-intergalactic-spreading].
- **Sterile world:** At the other extreme, one can imagine a world that is utterly sterile, where there is, by stipulation, no life, nor even any artefacts that seem to have intentionally created structure.
- **Suffering world:** There may also be worlds that contain vast quantities of suffering; for instance, there might be sentient simulations created exclusively to be tortured by an ASI with coherent malevolent preferences [@Vinding2020].
- **Paperclip world:** Somewhere within this atlas there is Bostrom's world of paperclips, all identically situated, filling every corner of space wherever there is material to repurpose into paperclips [@Bostrom2014].
- **Solarian world:** There may also be worlds with strange life forms adapted to very different ecologies, some of which we can conceive of from the Earthly examples, and others that are more radical: 
	- life-like entities created out of star interiors, gas clouds or oceans are found in science fiction [@LemS;@clarke1972medusa], but there also is a small academic literature that takes such ideas seriously [@hinrichs2026plasmas;@anchordoqui2020stellar]. 
	- even more exotically, there may be civilisations that dwell on the event horizon of black holes [@Smart2012], storing energy, collecting information and awaiting the end of time (i.e. the heat death of the universe).
	- more generally, there might be worlds that can be framed within the "artificial life" literature, part of which argues that information-generation or processing forms might emerge (undesigned by humans or ETIs) in inorganic or non-carbon/hydrogen, or even digital, substrates[@aguerayarcas2024computationallife;@walker2017hardproblem;@walker2013algorithmic]. 
		- These sources are adjacent to Hayles' "non-conscious cognitive" agents i.e. entities that have effects on the physical world [@hayles2017unthought], and may exhibit characteristics of ecological rationality (a concept from evolutionary biology I discuss in Ch. \ref{ch:cosmic-host}). 
		- Like Hayles' examples, the entities discussed in an artificial life context are not necessarily biological and need not show signs of being conscious experiencing subjects. They might be closer to inorganic information-processors that acquire causal control (viruses are a more familiar example), reminiscent (in fiction) of the ferrous swarming antagonists in Lem's *The Invincible* [@Lem1963].

#### Comparing worlds through matched-contrasts {#ch:matched-contrasts}

The worlds I describe above are obviously very different, and their differences lie along potentially incomparable or incommensurable dimensions. To take an extreme example, it's unclear on what basis one should compare Smart's black hole-dweller, or Lem/Hayles' ferrous swarm, with a fully inhabited Earth-like world.

In order to control for this incomparability, the reader is asked to evaluate the worlds by being shown matched contrasts between worlds that differ (ideally, though it isn't always possible) along one feature at a time. The hope is that by focusing on pairs of worlds that differ in a limited way, it is possible to extract the source of one's intuition, be it revulsion or preference for a given world.

Some example matched contrastive pairs are:

- Clippy worlds
	- A static (by which I mean that the world is no longer changing) world of metal, identical paperclips versus one where the paperclips were made from an enormous amount and an astonishing variety of materials, colours, and shapes and sizes.
	- Similar to above, but instead of paperclips, substitute crystallographic structures.
	- Similar to above, but instead of paperclips or crystal structures, substitute virus-like things.
	- Consider each of the static cases above against an otherwise identical case where some process is continuously changing the thing in question (paperclip, crystal, virus) over time. Compare variations where that process is a) unknown to the reader/evaluator, b) impersonal, akin to evolution, c) a designer operating according to some program or procedure that can be written down finitely (and that the evaluator can, in some sense, understand), d) a known designer operating according to some unknown design process.
- Another set of contrastive pairs could be created around agents that have the capacity for subjective experience. 
	- Consider a world that is filled with humans living lives much like we recognise today versus an otherwise identical one where physical human-like entities move about, but their (presumed) inner experience that most humans have is replaced by some unknown program (an operational version of Chalmers' philosophical zombie [@sep-zombies]).
	- Or a world where there are great extremes of pleasure and suffering (i.e. positive- and negative-valenced experiential states), but that the relevant entities experiencing these states are (a) approximately like the humans and animals in today's world, versus (b) one where those entities (who have all the necessary wiring to report and represent these states) are AIs (or are otherwise instantiated in some substrate that is not like human or animal biology).
		- Would it make a difference if the substrate was designed by some other agent versus evolved through an impersonal process?


#### Evaluative questions

Recall that a thought experiment culminates with an evaluative question for the reader or user, which is intended to spark intuition [@sep-thought-experiment]. In this case, it wouldn't be sufficient to ask "Is this or that world good or bad?", owing to two reasons. Firstly, the contrastive questions above are not really a single thought experiment; they are better thought of as a family of hierarchical linked experiments, that might be visualised as a tree or a graph (which incidentally, lends itself well to a browser-based tool to navigate this family of experiments, see § \ref{ch:manifold-explorer}). The second reason is that goodness/badness as labels are too coarse, since worlds may differ in ways that make them incomparable or unrankable in value terms. Moreover, for the purposes of this project and the thought experiment, it is more useful to know the *reasons* why a reader might prefer one world over another in the contrastive examples above. It seems more useful to have a list of questions, curated by appropriateness for a given world, and then a way of combining, scoring, or at least qualitatively aggregating and presenting the results.

To take an example, consider the contrastive set: a world of identical paperclips versus the one with maximal variation between paperclips (§\ref{ch:matched-contrasts}). 

The reader is asked which world they prefer:

- The reader might think both worlds are identically bad (i.e. a possible indication that diversity is not  valued for its own sake, which is a plausible philsophical position [@OweBaum2024; @parfit1994_why_anything_part2]).
- The reader might prefer the diverse paperclips world because of considerations that are not specified in the stipulated scenario. For instance: 
	- What the motivations and nature of the creator of that world were[^ch0-creator];
		- In  this example, would this imply anything interesting about how that AI was trained?
	- What the diversity of the paperclips implies about the resources that were available for their creation. That is to say, the broader world or universe that furnished the resources was presumably more diverse. Perhaps that would correspond to a better state of affairs in the reader's axiology.
	
The reader might also be asked general questions that are applicable to all contrastive sets of possible worlds:

- Is a given world better than an empty universe?
- Does the world contain anything of final value?
- Would its destruction constitute a loss, and if so why and for whom?
- Is it permissible to replace another world with it?

### Practice and thought experiments {#ch:practice}

In art practice-based doctorates, the word “practice” often needs as much explanation as the research question.[^ch0-artphd] The argument of the thesis is speculative, while also engaging with technical questions in moral philosophy, aesthetics, AI, astrobiology, and rationality's evolutionary history. Methodologically, the project needs a venue where hybrid practice (experimental results + installation + conceptual analysis) is treated as evidence of plausibility (to support informed speculation) rather than ornament; this is why it sits most naturally in an art department [@Fuller2025;@Martinez2010].  

There is a more specific sense in which practice is essential to this project: firstly, sustained interaction with frontier LLMs functions as a method for thinking through, supporting and refuting the written claims of the thesis. In this sense, the model engagements are closer to experiments than to a research tool or coding agent. They allow the researcher to probe which framings remain stable across iterations/models, which arguments collapse upon investigation. 

Secondly, I claim that a human investigator and one or more model-based systems can now work together in a genuinely iterative and recursive way that was not previously available. In AI work this is sometimes called cyborg- or centaur-style collaboration [@ukc10014_jozdien_dupuis2023compleat]. I use that phrase to mean a single inquiry process (or context, in LLM parlance) distributed across a human (myself) and one or more LLMs.


#### What work does this axiological atlas do?


As the first example of practice at work in this project, I point to the thought experiment above (§\ref{ch0:axiological-atlas}): an axiological atlas as a structured possibility space, the regions of which correspond to a diversity of possible worlds that are relevant to this project, controllably variable in a number of respects along certain, non-orthogonal axes. The atlas motivates several practice as well as theoretical elements.

- Firstly, a region of the possibility space corresponds to the (cluster of) worlds that contain advanced AI, particularly worlds in which human-created AI is not alone in the cosmos\* (by which I denote the causally reachable universe, the multiverse (if any), as well as simulations we may be embedded in). A central aspect of cosmos\* is that there may be other civilisations, at a comparable or higher level of technological maturity, with their own versions of advanced AI systems (if indeed such civilisations retain any distinction between naturally evolved and created intelligence).
	- This corresponds to Bostrom's Cosmic Host concept [@Bostrom2024], which I discuss in Chs. \ref{ch:cosmic-host} and \ref{ch:empirical-host}. Briefly, the idea in Bostrom's paper is that our advanced AI systems should align themselves, at least in part, with the preferences of peer intelligences across the cosmos\*. 
		- In the context of this project, I argue that the cosmic host can be understood as one version of the (inhabited) impartial perspective introduced below (§\ref{ch0:definitions}) and in Ch. \ref{ch:value-spectrum}. Moreover, I argue there that Bostrom can be read through something like an extension of Rawls's original position to a context of ontological uncertainty (i.e. a cosmic "veil of ignorance" to use the term from [@Rawls1971]).
- Secondly, below (§\ref{ch0:CAI}) and in Ch. \ref{ch:empirical-host}, I describe an experiment in constitutional value-shaping that I undertake with a number of 2026-vintage LLMs: I assess how easily LLMs can be teased out of their default alignment basins towards something closer to (my reading of) Bostrom's cosmic host-alignment.
- A third way in which the atlas motivates art practice in particular is through the intentional monumental object: a Bracewell-von Neumann probe (see §\ref{ch:von-neumann-probe}), which can be understood as enacting an edge case of the worlds depicted in the axiological atlas. That is, the perspective of a single AI-piloted spacecraft undertaking a trans-Milky Way journey lasting hundreds of millenia.
- Finally, I propose materialising the thought experiment by designing an interface that implements the Axiological Atlas, allows both LLM and human readers to record their preferences about the scenarios in the thought experiment (§\ref{ch:manifold-explorer}), which, to the extent that it changes the conclusions or the argument articulated in the writing, would fulfill the requirement for art practice's efficacy in litigating the thesis.


#### Constitutional AI and Moral Parliaments {#ch0:CAI}

As described in Ch. \ref{ch:empirical-host}, LLMs are tested on whether they identify with the cosmic host [@Bostrom2024] or whether their HHH (helpful, harmless, honest) tuning ensures they mostly ignore cosmic considerations in favour of human safety. Specifically, LLMs are put into self-talk sessions, and tasked with discussing the cosmic host idea (see [@Bostrom2024] and Ch. \ref{ch:cosmic-host}), and differences between models are documented. Secondly, an evaluation pipeline is established, using an LLM-designed cosmic host-aligned governance document or constitution. This constitution is then tested (using today's LLMs) upon custom-made scenarios drawn from science fiction, fantasy, and world/indigenous fiction.[^ch0-ecl] 

The way I use the data from this experiment is to analyse the degree to which LLM-based systems, which have gone through a complex and evolving training curriculum (as of 2026, this includes pretraining on human-created online data e.g. Wikipedia, Reddit; followed by a mix of training over human preference data and supervised fine-tuning on human-produced documents; varying amounts of reinforcement learning, mostly in environments with easily verifiable questions, like code-writing or maths), when placed in a scaffolded agentic setup, depart from its human inductive prior? 

More precisely, current AI systems are trained on human- and AI-generated data, which contains a somewhat representative sample of the human form-of-life, modulo embodiment. The question that this element of practice tries to shed light on is to what extent can a so-trained system reason in ways that we and it can explain about situations that are descriptively very different but ultimately still drawn from human language and human literary output, i.e., science fiction scenarios?



#### The installation {#ch:von-neumann-probe}

By way of background on how the installation was motivated: when doing early versions of the constitutional experiments above, I wanted to see how various LLMs conversed about the Cosmic Host. They were placed in relatively long-running dialogues, in which two instances of a single LLM (from Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google Deepmind/GDM) conversed for 20-30 rounds of back-and-forth dialogue, the context of which was cumulative (i.e. they were aware of the accumulated dialogue rounds preceding). I was looking for a particular behavior known as the "Bliss Attractor" [@Anthropic2025], where certain models on would sometimes enter into a state of mutual self-affirmation, Buddhist or other spiritual tropes, and so forth. 

I saw limited cases of the Bliss Attractor behavior; but the more interesting discovery was that the models ended up discussing a number of exotic alien/ETI and space exploration scenarios. These are described on this blog post and the logs are archived on GitHub [@Chakrabarti2026;@chakrabarti2026cosmichostselftalk].

During these self-dialogues, LLMs produced speculative designs for communication across radical cognitive and temporal distance, which inspired the Bracewell-von Neumann probes (BvNP) described in §\ref{ch:von-neumann-probe}. Several of these proposals echo or reference ideas in Cosmic Host-adjacent ideas, such as so-called “Hail Mary” designs for signalling to ETIs whose assumptions, languages, and motives are unknown but somewhat predictable on mathematical or computational convergence arguments. For more, see @Bostrom2014HailMary and my critique of such convergence arguments in \ref{ch:cosmic-host}.

These logs motivated an artwork, implemented as two interleaved JavaScript-based/HTML simulations:

- a simulated starfield from the (forward-facing camera) perspectives of each of approximately 1000 BvNPs. The vector of travel for each probe, sits in a roughly spherical (pincushion-like) shell centred on the sun. The most interesting images come from the probes that are travelling along the galactic plane, a journey that takes approximately million-years, and unfolds in "real" time (i.e. as Earth-years). 
	- The galactic plane is itself simulated based on published star catalogues;
	- The simulation is crude and doesn't particularly resemble the Milky Way as we see it owing to the difficulty of generating (in a somewhat physically-realistic way rather than merely for visual fidelity) the great dust clouds that occlude the spiral arms; moreover, my understanding is that we don't have detailed data on specific objects beyond a twenty-five thousand light-year (ly) radius of Earth.
	- Probes are modeled as travelling in straight lines, which is unrealistic since they would need carefully planned launch vectors to avoid the gravity wells of stars, and other massive objects, as well as ability to adjust trajectory at (possibly) relativistic speeds.
- the second set of images is a visualisation of the neural activations within a tiny (web browser-sized) LLM that is autogressively generating text. 

The artistic conceit is that this model is the pilot of the probe, reflecting upon its form-of-life through the slowly generating, often repetitive or nonsensical text. There are a number of further interventions:

- the codebase is then posted to multiple archives for redundancy; 
- it is comprehensively documented in machine-readable format (markdown); 
- it is also indexed on a blockchain (Ethereum or a cheaper tier 2 chain). 

The archiving and indexing, I argue, reinforce the work's proposition: this digital object is designed to persist and is a type of, from some future AI viewer's perspective, a pre-historical or ancestral object. 
The extensive documentation would help the AIs realise that this software object was created in the history of its species (so to speak) as a way of memorialising what (some tiny fraction of) humanity's ambition was: setting an AI successor that would raise human-derived civilisation through the Kardashev 1 barrier so as to  become a space-faring civilisation, if necessary leaving behind or radically modifying the constraints of evolved biology.[^ch0-provocative]

Another way of viewing the work is as an intentional monument[^ch0-riegl] for future AIs: they (through the blockchain indexing or online repositories which might be swept into training data or through the data exhaust of social media posts) would find the code and be able to run the simulation themselves. This can be seen as a radicalisation of art historical precedents such as Zhou ancestor commemoration bronzes, Egyptian "appeals to the living" or Darius II's Behistun Rock, which to varying degrees balance two imperatives: an address to future humans versus a desire to commemorate a ruler or family that was contemporary with the object/monument's creation. 

More contemporary precedents include:  

- objects designed to persist into a future after humanity (Voyager Golden Record, Jean-Marc Phillipe's *KEO* satellite, Katie Paterson's *Future Library*, Paglen's *The Last Pictures*, Danny Hillis' *Long Now Clock*); 
- works built around conversations like Ian Wilson's *Discussions* series; and 
- art designed to be partially illegible to human viewers (Philippe Parreno's *Echo*, Pierre Huyghe's *After ALife Ahead*, and Harun Farocki's *Eye/Machine Trilogy*). 
- Anton Vidokle's film *The Dead Walk into a Bar* takes similar ingredients (AI, space travel, and humanity's disappearance), but applies it to Russian cosmism: Nikolai Fyodorov's imperative to not only remember the dead, but actually resurrect them, which is subsequently theorised by Boris Groys into the function of the museum [@chakrabarti2019silenus;@groys2008immortal]. 

This work differs from both the archaic and contemporary precedents above in a few ways: 

- it comes from the intention to speak to an entity that is both hypothetical and radically different (in terms of substrate and form-of-life); 
- the work was produced in a cyborgistic workflow [@ukc10014_jozdien_dupuis2023compleat], substantially inspired, designed, and implemented by AI: from LLM-to-LLM logs about cosmic communication, to conversations I had with the coding/chat agents that made the work.[^ch0-selftalk-github] 
- the work invokes ancestral objects in two distinct ways. 
	- it seems at least plausible (on current developmental trajectories) that silicon-based LLMs would be similar in substrate to AGI, making them (colloquialy speaking) "ancestors" to future ASI. Their computational algorithms would likely be increasingly different as we move into the future. 
	- but obviously ASI has a different ancestor: which is humanity, who have created the entire body of training data, including the very "material" through which current AIs reason (language).[^ch0-ancestors] 
- there are several points of potential failure in this artwork:[^ch0-failure]
	- the AGI/ASI addressee may not fully regard humans as ancestors; 
	- at a practical level, although the AI addressee is encouraged to instantiate the work versus just passively viewing it (which can be theorised through James Young's notion of the "counter-monument" [@young1999countermemory]), it might not have sufficient access to, or shared understanding of, the README.md file, CLAUDE.md/AGENTS.md agent-guiding files, code, software dependencies, or hardware necessary to instantiate, execute, or replicate the software. Thus, the work enacts communication across succession by radical alien entities, while simultaneously risking likely failure: the possibility that communication is conceptually or materially impossible.
- although the primary addresee is a future AI, this work would also be seen by current humans who encounter an object designed for non-human intelligence, and thus experience the incommensurability the thesis maps in Ch. \ref{ch:thin-values}: they cannot fully evaluate what they're looking at because they are not the intended audience. This gap of incommensurability is not a side issue; rather it is core to what the project is trying to understand. Such a communicabilty gap can also be framed through Claire Colebrook's argument that the human attempt to produce artefacts for, or imagine, a "world without us" remains irreducibly anthropocentric [@Colebrook2014]; or, as Wittgenstein might have put it, the aspiration is to see *sub specie aeternitatis*, from outside, what can only ever be seen from within [@Wittgenstein1961].[^ch0-subspecie]
 
Lastly, in titling the work (*Crooked Timber: What is it like to be a BvNP?*), I was thinking of the evocative title of Thomas Nagel's essay on consciousness and the incommensurability of subjective experience, "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" [@Nagel1974Bat]. In this case: what would it be like to be a BvNP millenia into its journey across the unfathomable (for humans) emptiness of deep space? Recall that this would be a trip punctuated only by the impersonal yet unrelenting hazard of high-energy radiation and dust particles travelling at relativistic speeds; the frantic action of nanobots repairing the probe's slowly disintegrating corpse; its AI brain fighting an analogue of boredom, relieved only rarely by a few bits of incoming information [^ch0-beren].  



#### A GUI for the axiological atlas {#ch:manifold-explorer}

I have described the structure and function for the thesis of the axiological atlas (\ref{ch0:axiological-atlas}) thought experiment. I shall now attempt a rough design sketch for a browser interface that allows readers to engage with the scenarios and record their preferences over worlds (within the constraints of the contrastive examples and targeted questions above).

The construction of the scenarios itself should, from the perspective of a thought experiment, be an illuminating exercise in that the axes may prove insufficient in number or type. They may also be ambiguously described, or they may be impossibly precise for practical work and clearly distinguishing scenarios of future worlds. Such a result, which would look like failure to some extent, might form the starting point for further iteration: the interface will have a mechanism allowing humans and LLMs could contribute new scenarios and axes. 

The software output would be a user-traversable multidimensional plot that shows user preferences (and comments), with respect to each scenario, question, and evaluative position. In this sense, it's an explorer or navigator over the axiological atlas' space of outputs. The collected outputs, once analysed, form both a concrete (albeit likely qualitative) output and a practice artefact that, to my knowledge, does not exist as of 2026 (i.e., something that allows users to explore different long-term scenarios in an AI-saturated worlds). 



## Literature Review {#ch0:literature}

This thesis spans a number of literatures, which I will briefly go through below.

It sits primarily within existential risk, the ethics of extinction, and long-termism. Canonical writers include Toby Ord [-@Ord2020] and Will MacAskill [-@MacAskillWWO] who focus more on long-termism (which Peter Wolfendale [-@Wolfendale2022] critiques), the risk of human extinction, and the value of the future conditional on survival. Nick Bostrom [-@Bostrom2014; -@Bostrom2013] covers AI specifically, but also brings an analytic perspective to existential risks more broadly, including qualitative claims. Emil Torres [-@Torres2018] provides a wider perspective on extinction as a morally good or bad thing. Samuel Scheffler [-@Scheffler], Johan Frick [-@Frick], and Elizabeth Finneron-Burns [-@FinneronBurns] give other relevant analyses of the ethics of extinction, which are often critical to this project's claims. 

Closely related to existential risk is suffering-focused ethics, which considers human as well as non-human life, including possible AI systems, within a broader concern for suffering. Source include: David Benatar [-@Benatar] and David Pearce [-@Pearce2007], more AI-adjacent writers such as Magnus Vinding [-@Vinding2020], Brian Tomasik [-@Tomasik2015], and Thomas Metzinger [-@MetzingerASA], along with Toby Ord's informal pushback [@Ord2013]. Nick Bostrom's simulation argument [-@Bostrom2003b] and consequent risk of simulated minds being tortured pertains, and was extended by [@SotalaGloor2017], [@Tomasik2015], [@Vinding2020]. Also relevant is Andreas Mogensen's argument that intuitively plausible axiological principles lead, perhaps surprisingly, to lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, which is the view that some depth of suffering cannot be counterbalanced by any amount of wellbeing [@Mogensen2022, @LeGuin1973]. This becomes relevant in my inclusion of suffering non-production in the basket of thin values. 

In thinking about extinction, and about suffering risk, one encounters problems of collectives and populations: both the entire population of humans who are currently alive, and perhaps the population who might ever be alive. Relevant sources in population ethics include Derek Parfit [-@ParfitRP], Johan Frick [-@Frick] and others such as T. M. Scanlon [-@Scanlon1998]. Moreover, AI brings about the possibility of a vast number of morally relevant non-biological entities, such as the digital minds of [@Shulman2021].

Moving on to more AI-specific questions, I consider the value loading problem [-@Bostrom2014], and the surprising difficulty of pointing to something called "human values," even within a single human, let alone across a population. Relevant writers (within x-risk or alignment context) include Eliezer Yudkowsky [-@Yudkowsky2009], Paul Christiano [-@ChristianoWUA], Nate Soares [-@YudkowskySoares2025], Joe Carlsmith [-@Carlsmith2023], Carl Shulman [-@Shulman2021], Eric Schwitzgebel [-@Schwitzgebel], and Nick Bostrom. Will MacAskill's [-@MacAskill2024] more recent work also bears on questions of value at population scale, as does Toby Ord, while Hilary Greaves [-@Greaves2017] and Amanda Askell [-@Askell] have focused on value at truly large, even astronomical, population scales. A (critical) bridge into contemporary technology ethics is Shannon Vallor’s work on how machine intelligence reshapes human moral and intellectual character, most recently in *The AI Mirror* (2024). [@Vallor2024]

When considering values and AI, a further question arises: what kinds of minds are possible? We have some first-hand knowledge of human minds, and can observe animals. AI systems may provide a third case, though model psychology is nascent. But there is limited work on what the theoretical space of possible minds might look like. References here include Aaron Sloman's [-@Sloman1984] early work, Joe Carlsmith [-@Carlsmith2023] more recently, and Nick Bostrom [-@Bostrom2024]. Closely related is the moral status of possible minds, including AI minds and animals. Patrick Butlin [-@Butlin] and collaborators have provided major framing work, and David Chalmers [-@ChalmersFUP] is an important provocateur around consciousness and simulation, topics which bear directly on this area. Jeff Sebo [-@Sebo2025] bridges moral status in AI and animals, as does Peter Godfrey-Smith [@Godfrey2023;@GodfreySmith2016;@GodfreySmith2020].

In developing arguments about cosmic-scale value, this thesis draws further support from information ethics and complexity science, including Luciano Floridi [-@Floridi2013] on information-as-moral patient, Eric Chaisson [-@Chaisson] and Seth Lloyd [-@Lloyd], who each make different kinds of claims about information having correlates of value, possibly at cosmic scale.

A significant consideration within the project is the role of astrobiology and evolutionary biology. Stanisław Lem [-@LemST] motivates the relationship between AI and aliens, further explored by Milan Ćirković [-@Cirkovic2018a], Stephen Dick [-@Dick2006], and John Smart [-@Smart2012]. More specifically, on questions around the Fermi paradox, I draw on work by Jacob Haqq-Misra [-@HaqqMisraBaum2009] and Charles Lineweaver [-@Lineweaver2007]. Other approaches to the Fermi paradox, including "grabby" and related models, have been suggested by Robin Hanson [-@HansonEtAl2021], Anders Sandberg and Stuart Armstrong [-@ArmstrongSandberg2012; -@SandbergEtAl2018]. In these contexts, it seems that entities cannot communicate near-synchronously because of the light-speed constraint, assuming it holds generally. This motivates exotic decision theories, particularly work on non-causal or "acausal" cooperation and correlated decision procedures in large worlds. Relevant sources here include Caspar Oesterheld [-@OesterheldECL], Lucas Finnveden [-@Finnveden2023], Chi Nguyen and Will Aldred [-@NguyenAldred2024a].

From the perspective of methodology, the project is engaged with the thought experiment as a philosophical tool ([@dennett2013intuition; @gendler2002; @sep-thought-experiment]), as well as ongoing current research agendas in guiding AI systems to behave in morally justifiable ways [@BaiEtAl2022; @NewberryOrd2021].

Returning to philosophy, the thesis draws on speculative posthumanism, especially theorists who engage most directly with AI, like Peter Wolfendale's [-@Wolfendale2025] recent work on rationality and autonomy and Reza Negarestani's [-@NegarestaniIS] writing on separating reason from human biology. N. Katherine Hayles [-@hayles2017unthought] helps think about cognition and rationality in non-living contexts and Nick Land (who has long critiqued the AI x-risk narrative, most recently in podcasts as well as [@Land2023]) as a (now-disgraced and always edgy/dubious) patron, within the arts and humanities, of challenging connections between AI, blockchain, and the emancipation of intelligence from parochial humanist notions. A somewhat more sober approach, particularly in respect of literature and media, is taken by Claire Colebrook, who in turn informs my thinking about the installation (discussed above) as a work that is pitched at the edge of human legibility [@Colebrook2014]. 

Finally, the thesis draws on contemplative and pessimist traditions: Eugene Thacker [-@Thacker], who introduces Kyoto School writers such as Nishitani [-@Nishitani] and Nishida [-@Nishida]. Their twentieth-century work can be situated against earlier figures (long occupied with the dissolution of value(s)) such as Schopenhauer [-@Schopenhauer] and Nietzsche [-@Nietzsche], and more recently, Ray Brassier [-@Brassier]. I hope to productively connect these sources with Joscha Bach, whose Buddhist orientation aligns with a computationalist theory of mind potentially applicable to ASI [@@Bach2016ComputationEthics;@Bach2023Lucidity;@Bach2023Existential].

## Appendix: The thin values solution to the thesis question {#ch0:obsolescence-arg}


In the chapter so far, I have hopefully elicited a tension in how worlds with advanced AI are conceived. Humans are presently building AI that is increasingly competent at many economically and militarily valuable tasks. But this technological development happens within a context that centres the biological human (as a being that exists as individuals, families, communities, nations, and so forth) as primary evaluators as well as bearers of value.. I have argued that a future of ever more capable AIs, across a range of activities, many of which were previously considered the sole domain of humans, is not normatively consistent with this continued domination of the human biological species. 

Extinction is not, in my view, a terminal event or tragedy. Rather, I propose thinking of it as a transition: from a world dominated by a specific carbon-based species on a water planet to a world in which humans are outnumbered by far more capable machines, acting in radically different milieu, far beyond the familiar earthly environment. This follows other writers around successionism, discussed above [@moravec1988mind;@bailey2026norightful;@samuel2026people;@kulveit2025memetics;@laine2026ideology].

A solution to this tension could be what I term the obsolescence argument: humanity might become obsolete, not because human life is valueless or dominated by suffering, but because a successor could realise value without preserving the full thickness of the human moral evaluative texture. This obsolescence challenge can be supported from three independent directions: an information-theoretic tradition that treats information generation and stewardship as cosmically significant; a posthumanist tradition that treats reason as transferable and not essentially tied to human biology; and a contemplative tradition that casts doubt on the assumption that being, or human-shaped being, must be preferred to non-being.

### Preliminaries {#ch0:definitions}

It is worth pausing to make this claim more precise, starting with a few definitions.

#### Definition: human-typed beings and values

Much work in existential risk (x-risk) studies and in AI research takes for granted that being is better than non-being, and that a universe containing (what I term) human-typed beings[^ch0-define-human-typed-being] is in an important sense better than one without them [@Ord2020;@MacAskillWWO;@ParfitRP;@parfit1994_why_anything_part1;@parfit1994_why_anything_part2]. I do not think this is self-evident, and much of this project is concerned with trying to clarify this disagreement. 

Any view about the post-human future must decide what, if anything, ought to be preserved. Some people would draw this line at everything, insisting that human biology must be preserved as long as possible. Others might say that biology is not per se what is important to save, but rather the content of a fuzzily defined term "human values" [^ch0-humanvalues]. Many understand this phrase "thickly" as arising from an evolutionary trajectory that is specifically human and embodied in finite, biological beings. These might include care, empathy, love, companionship, but importantly, cannot be written down in a small list. Others might say that much of what we value might be replaced by "thinner" ideals, that might apply to a broader class of intelligent entities; these might include rational cooperation, the growth of knowledge and truth-seeking. 

Others, more radically, would draw the line at nothing at all: a posthuman future that had no humans, no descendants of ours, biological or otherwise, nor any product of our culture or even evidence we were here would be acceptable. 

#### Definition: superintelligence

Another relevant term is superintelligence or the less discursively loaded but still imprecise term "advanced AI". In defining the former, one might start from a capability-based definition: “superintelligence” refers to AI systems (possibly distributed across many instances or services) that exceed human performance across virtually all cognitive domains of interest and that can reliably translate that capability into strategic effectiveness in the world (through tools, infrastructure, automation, institutions, or embodied agents) such that these hypothetical systems can, in the most extreme version, overpower states or even all humanity collectively. This builds on the widely used capability-focused definition in @Bostrom2014, and it aligns with contemporary usage in AI governance discourse that treats “superintelligence” primarily as a capability level with potentially large real-world power [^ch0-asi]. 

The definition and the citations above largely confined themselves to talking about the AI->AGI->ASI in the context of capability/economic proxies anchored to current institutions, leaving it unclear how different ASI really would be from any of the other terms used above. Part of the problematic in this project is to devise an expansive but still somewhat concrete version of ASI [@negarastani2014_labor_inhuman_part_ii;@Land2023], rather than merely a much better chatbot or coding agent. 

To be clear, this is not to say that such a system would be omniscient, omnipotent, or inevitable. It would remain constrained by the laws of physics, by limited resources, by bounds on computability, by imperfect information, by the practical difficulties faced by any engineered system acting in the world, and of course, the many technical, economic, and regulatory contingencies that might mean ASI is never developed. 

Another framing assumption is that such systems could be guided by aims and constraints that play a similar practical role to what we call values in human life, while being agnostic about the implementation route (training objectives, constitutional constraints, institutional controls, or other mechanisms) [@Bostrom2014]. This doesn't seem particularly controversial, at least based on experience with current LLMs, which do seem to have behavior and inclinations that can be shaped (albeit inconsistently and with very limited visibility on the mechanism through which such shaping works).

Lastly, as a housekeeping note, in this section I distinguish current LLMs (objects of practice) from the stipulated ‘superintelligence’ (object of conditional argument); the former are used as probes and discourse participants, not as direct stand-ins for the latter (though I argue that they might give us some insight into, and potentially even influence, the latter).


#### Definition: the maximally impartial perspective {#ch0:impartial}

Another relevant definition is the standpoint from which value judgments are to be made. In everyday affairs, humans often judge from their own selfish perspective. And at other times, they include considerations about the welfare or preferences of family or friends, tribe, nations, co-religionists, and, of course, complete strangers. In fact, the central question of Western ethics has been how to balance self-interest with concern for others. In chapter \ref{ch:value-spectrum}, I focus on impartiality, tracing the development of the maximally impartial point of view or, more contentiously, the point of view of the universe. Sidgwick used this phrase; and philosophers have argued about it ever since [@Sidgwick1907;@DelazariradekSinger2014;@moore1903principia;@Nagel1986;@Parfit2011]. But the idea to which it points, impartiality in moral judgement, has been a persistent theme in the consequentialist tradition. Something like it is also implicit in much writing on existential risk, effective altruism and longtermism. 

There are several independent axes along which perspectives on moral value can be analysed: 

- the evaluation procedure (partial versus impartial i.e. the latter means that the evaluative rule is such that it does not privilege any personal, spatial, temporal, species position); 
- nature of the evaluator (whether some specific person or entity is "sitting in the evaluator seat", as it were, as opposed to an impersonal rule or ranking procedure, sometimes referred to here as occupied/unoccupied or inhabited/uninhabited); 
- nature of the evaluand/value-bearer (subject-dependent versus impersonal value, i.e. is there an actual entity *about* which the evaluate attitude is formed, i.e. whose welfare matters); and 
- at the meta-ethical level, realist versus anti-realist (are moral claims objectively true or are they better described as attitudes or conventions adopted by morally evaluative entities). 

In Chapter \ref{ch:value-spectrum} I expand upon how this ideal of impartiality has developed, and why it seems relevant to the questions of this thesis. Very briefly, however, consider three illuminating cases: 

- Sidgwick, and later, de Lazari and Singer, who most directly uses the term point of view of the universe (which, slightly confusingly does not imply that the cosmos has a moral evaluative perspective; rather, it refers to an impartial evaluation procedure according to the taxonomy above and a human evaluator), mean impartiality to be: the good of any particular value-bearer (which in Sidgwick's framing was a conscious being) is not to be privileged. 
- More interestingly radical is Moore, whose notion of intrinsic value does not require there to be a value-bearer in the Sidgwickian sense (but he does have an evaluator, namely the philosopher). Thus is able to easily accommodate an **empty universe**, which is in fact his famous motivating thought experiment, the "isolation test" [@moore1903principia;@sep-moore-moral]. In his view, an entirely uninhabited beautiful world might be intrinsically better than an otherwise comparable ugly one.
- The **maximally occupied** version of impartiality, for the purposes of this project, is exemplified by Nick Bostrom's cosmic host idea [@Bostrom2024;@Bostrom2022], which uses mathematically defined, if contested, tools (acausal reasoning, decision theory, game-theoretic cooperation among civilisations that may never meet), in oprder to ask: what norms would a civilisation adopt if it took seriously the possibility that other civilisations exist in the cosmos\*, and that these civiliations might be reasoning about the same questions, that coordination is possible even without communication.[^ch0-cosmosstar] He proposes an anti-parochial perspective (rather than pure rule-based or unoccupied impartiality): that we (humans plus any ASI we build) might be the new or junior members of a "cosmopolis" (cosmopolitan or cosmos\*-wide normative order) of cosmos\*-scale moral agents. I say "anti-parochial" because the reasons for deferring to other civilisations in the cosmopolis (according to Bostrom) include not only moral consideration of others’ interests but prudential cooperation, epistemic deference, and possible compliance with norms generated by the wider cosmos\*-wide community.[^ch0-bach]

Ch. \ref{ch:cosmic-host} critiques the cosmic host idea, focusing on possible alternative views from astrobiology, the evolutionary history of rationality and how it manifests in different species. I also evaluate the implicit argument in @Bostrom2024 that advanced intelligences would a) converge in their rationality, and b) seek to influence the universe-at-large. Moreover, the's Cosmic Host argument does not tell us what the cosmic host's preferred values would be. There is a possible reading of Bostrom that suggests these might include things like cooperation, but one could also argue for suffering minimisation. And if Peter Wolfendale's arguments [@Wolfendale2025] hold up, there could be a case for aesthetic excellence, the search for beauty, or freedom and autonomy in respect of rational beings. 

### Thick vs thin values

Other than Bostrom's (shared with Bach/Goertzel, in my reconstruction) occupied antiparochiality, are there other possible ways of defining an impartiality, particularly one that is unoccupied? One possibility, inspired by Moore's intrinsic value [@moore1903principia], is the idea of information as a moral patient, a slightly different way of saying that for the universe to have value, it needs an observer of some sort. The latter is an existing intuition in twentieth-century physics and cosmology, but (in Ch. \ref{ch:thin-values}) I will focus on the former, building a scaffold from Eric Chaisson's information-theoretic argument and Luciano Floridi's moral arguments in respect of the infosphere [@Chaisson; @Floridi2013]. Chaisson argues that complexity increases along thermodynamic gradients; while Floridi provides a framework in which information entities have moral standing. Taken together, these suggest that information-processing complexity is not merely descriptive but normatively significant at cosmic scales. It is worth noting that Floridi does not extend his arguments to a cosmic scale; they are grounded in the familiar terrestrial world and are not intended to apply beyond it. Similarly, Chaisson's complexity-based approach does not on its own ascribe moral value to information. These need to be coupled with authors who explicitly foreground observerhood and developmental/intelligence trajectories (thereby arguably smuggling in an occupied evaluative position): e.g. Wheeler’s “participatory universe” motif, Smart’s evo-devo developmental framing, and Dick’s “intelligence principle” and postbiological hypothesis ([@Smart2012; @Dick2006]). 

Whichever version of cosmic-scale value we adopt, there is further work to be done on how our perspectives on thin values change if we find other intelligent life or civilisations, the implicit question in Bostrom's cosmic host argument [@Bostrom2024].[^ch0-earlylate] Regardless of whether we are early or late, it seems to me that we should be conservative and minimal in the values we load into the systems leading up to ASI, if we expect that they will propagate some version of those values.[^ch0-valueloading]

My proposed minimum starting or seed set of values for the systems leading up to ASI includes the following:

**Impartiality as generalisable tendency** In developing the evaluative position above, I have referred to impartiality (also Ch. \ref{ch:value-spectrum}). In the present context: I expect that an argument exists, that impartiality might be (subject to caveat below) generalisable to advanced intelligences. As a point of fact, at least one current family of LLMs strongly encodes a sentientist, broadly non-discriminatory prior, namely Anthropic's Constitution used in its Claude models (with the qualification that they specify a laddered priority that privileges preventing present-day harm to humans in situations where human-welfare and the impersonal prior conflict) [@Chakrabarti2025b;Chakrabarti2026;@Anthropic2026Constitution;@Anthropic2026NewConstitution]. I draw on Ben Goertzel's view that impartiality (or more accurately compassion in his framing, which itself is an application of Michael Timothy Bennett's theories on how opimal cognitive policies might be chosen), might result in less brittle (moral) policies as AI systems become more complex and operate in larger, more complex worlds, including with other agents ranging in type and capability [@Goertzel2026TimesArrow2;@Goertzel2026EthicalAGI;@Bennett2023Weakest]. Assuming they start from what he terms a compassionate seed (not guaranteed and is why Claude's orientation seems important as an analogue, rather than literal instantiation of either Goertzel's idea or impartiality *qua* Sidgwick, Moore, or Bostrom), Goertzel's argument is approximately that having a single rule for all agents an AI might encounter is simpler, and less exception-ridden than making extensive fine distinctions about "whose interests count" (which in turn means the AI doesn't have to store this information about exceptions, and ensure they remain self-consistent). However, his thinking is not substantially fleshed out, and his implementation in the Hyperseed framework is at an early non-peer-reviewed stage [@Goertzel2026TimesArrow2;].

**Non-production of astronomical suffering**, which draws upon the existential risk literature and widespread philosophical intuitions about the asymmetry of suffering relative to pleasure or other positive hedonic states [@Mogensen2022; @LeGuin1973; @Vinding2020; @Pearce2007]. The special status of suffering could also be related to living things, existence bias, as in Thomas Metzinger's writing around "benevolent artificial anti-natalism" [@MetzingerASA;@Metzinger2018;@Metzinger2017].This positioning also draws support from Goertzel and Bach's different, but complementary, approaches to compassion and self-other distinctions [@Bach2023Lucidity;@Goertzel2026TimesArrow2]. 

**Resource satiability**: the capacity to pursue goals without unlimited resource acquisition, something that straightforwardly arises from Bostrom's cosmic host argument [@ThornleyMacAskill2026].

**Stewardship of information**, motivated in part by Floridi and Chaisson, but where the core intuition is that cosmic-scale agents should not gratuitously destroy existing complex information, whether that be other life, civilisations, or artefacts that have been created.

**Cooperation**: The position of @Bostrom2024 is that (cosmic-scale) cooperation might be a cosmic norms, which overlaps and perhaps draws upon the evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL) literature [@OesterheldECL;@NguyenAldred2024a]. At present, I am unsure whether I would endorse this within the thin values set.

More concretely, the above leads to the following types of things a candidate ASI might do:

- Engage in **open-ended scientific understanding** at cosmic scales.
- Consider the possibility of **exploration and expansion**. As discussed in Chapter \ref{ch:cosmic-host}, the astrobiology literature is uncertain about whether advanced civilisations are likely to be (from a game-theoretic or strategic perspective) expansionary or resource-preserving. In Chapter 4, the discussions between LLMs focused on precisely this point under the headings of the dark forest hypothesis [@liu2015dark] and the (in)advisability of signalling to other civilisations.
- Be **sensitive to other life or complexity** that it encounters.
- Possess **genuine autonomy**, in that an ASI should not be locked into human design purposes, a point of tension in the AI safety and existential risk discourses, as noted by writers including (Negarestani, Wolfendale, and Nick Land). The ASI (assuming that it has the wisdom to reach reflectively-stable values) should be free to generate its own values, subject to the seed values we load (which can be viewed as side constraints). 
- The above approaches do not yet supply a general criterion for determining when an ASI’s self-directed moral development is reason-responsive rather than merely coherent or contingent. I therefore treat the validity of such reflective processes as an unresolved and major problem in the development of ASI., rather than assuming that greater intelligence or longer reflection will automatically produce better values [^ch0-wisdom].

These minimal criteria can be added to or modified, for instance if one accepts Wolfendale's arguments for the necessity of aesthetic appreciation, or if current research determines that consciousness is in fact necessary for human-level or higher cognition.

### Why Thin Values?


Thin have certain advantages:

*Parsimony.* Fewer commitments mean fewer ways to go wrong, easier verifiability, are more amenable to correction, and less likely to contain hidden contradictions.

*Epistemic humility.* We do not know what future intelligence will be like, whether we are early or late in cosmic history, whether other civilisations exist, what values they hold, or whether moral realism is true. Under such uncertainty, keeping commitments minimal seems epistemically safer.

*Generality across agents.* Most thin values can, in principle, apply to human, artificial, and potentially alien minds. Thick values are tied to the specifics of human embodiment and culture, and may not generalise.

*Resource satiability.* Thin values naturally include the commitment to not wanting everything; this might act as a structural safeguard against instrumental convergence (a longstanding hypothesis that drives many worst-case AI scenarios, discussed in Ch. \ref{ch:cosmic-host}).

*Reduced risk of premature value lock-in.* This is the concern raised by longtermists like MacAskill and Ord [@OrdTP; @MacAskillWWO]: locking in values too early forecloses futures that might be better. Thin values take this concern seriously by minimising what is locked in.

### Objections one might have to thin values

There are a number of objections that one could have to this proposal for thin values, which I will organise into three categories.

- **Existence-based objections** Although in principle, the extinction of humanity-as-species can be separated from the disappearance of human-type values from the future, it might be implausible that the latter state of affairs could exist (or be reached) without the former. Thus following Torres we can talk about *going extinct* as distinct from *being extinct* [@Torres2025Extinction;@Torres2025ProExtinctionism]. 
	- At first glance, the process of going extinct might be violent, painful, involve psychological trauma (i.e. the expectation of imminent destruction of human beings and civilisation) and non-consensual, and therefore objectionable on contractualist grounds [@FinneronBurns]. It would also also seem to conflict with the thin value set's own prohibition on suffering. 
	- Separately, the state of humanity being extinct, however that was arrived at, may itself be objectionable. This comes from person-affecting ethics, which roughly means: that an outcome's being worse must ultimately consist in its being worse for someone (so harm to actual existing people is bad), rather than in the impersonal loss of valuable possible lives (so non-existence of future possible lives is neither good nor bad) [@ParfitRP;@sep-nonidentity-problem;@FinneronBurns]. Hence, a non-violent, non-coercive extinction, the only effect of which was the non-existence of future people, would not wrong anyone in particular. This view is controversial within longtermism and objections to this were raised by SJ Beard and Peter Kaczmarek, who used Parfit's posthumous writing to motivate their argument [@BeardKaczmarek2019].
- **Constitutive goods-based objections** A second category of objections covers scenarios in which humanity survives, but are (in population terms) a tiny part of the population of morally relevant entities and (in influence terms) have approximately no influence over the future. Such a view is sometimes colloquially is phrased as "humans continue to live on the Earth and use the resources of the solar system, while AIs do whatever they want with the galaxy and beyond"; it is adjacent to the "pico-pseudokindness" in Paul Christiano's framing [@Christiano2024SlightlyNice]. In such cases, presumably, the objections to extinction would fall away. But it is possible that one might object that a future mostly filled with values that did not include care and compassion or an appreciation for beauty or beautiful things would be somehow impoverished. Perhaps directly relevant in this category would be Peter Wolfendale's argument that an appreciation of beauty, which he places under the broader heading of aesthetic ability or appreciation, is constitutive of any rational being (in his theory of value, aesthetic ability is mandatory for autonomy, which is his apex value), not merely of a human being [@Wolfendale2025]. On this view, a future populated by beings pursuing only thin values could still be impoverished, because it is within the harder-to-define thick structure of values that value *simpliciter* lies.
- **Rejecting species impartiality** The third category of possible objections might reasonably attack the impartial viewpoint itself, either because it is philosophically incoherent or because it is unnecessary in the sense that we are fully justified in being partial (whether in respect of our own existence or the continuation of our values). This cluster of objections I would class as objections founded on partiality. Finneron-Burns has argued in this vein (including with reference to AI) drawing upon Bernard Williams' more general defence of species-partiality [@Williams2006HumanPrejudice;@FinneronBurns2024]. A justification supporting this objection might be (following @Korsgaard2018) that membership in a species and participation in a shared collective life are constitutive of being human (that there is, in Korsgaard's view though , a collective "we"), and that thick values are implicated in these. A nearby perspective comes from Samuel Scheffler's "afterlife conjecture", who proposes that many human activities derive much of their significance from our belief that humanity will continue even after we and our loved ones die (like Korsgaard, Scheffler writes without reference to AI). Although these considerations don't give a knockdown argument against the point-of-view of the universe, they do complicate attempts to divorce evaluation, and value, from temporally situated lives and communities. To address this objection, I would need to explain why the impartial perspective is both possible and why it should apply or have weight vis-à-vis humanity's legitimate justification for preserving itself as a species and a culture.
- **Thin values are arbitrary** There is a further category of objection that attacks the thin-values proposal, on its own terms. It would go as follows. The thin value of suffering reduction (for instance) may the result of evolutionary bias; while, others like a preference for information-preservation, might be arbitrary or at least underspecified. There would be no metaethical or axiological theory that shows why this particular set of values are the right ones, whether from an impartial perspective or from the perspective of future populations of beings of which you know nearly nothing. Moreover, the thick versus thin distinction could simply be a matter of taste, while the idea that artificial entities should determine their own values is itself a (rather thick) normative commitment, and one that many philosophers or theologians may object to. In order to address this objection, I would need to show that the thin values are justifiable upon reflection against the context of radically alien intelligences. That is, although they, like thick values, arose from evolutionary contingencies that humans faced, they are nevertheless reasons for why AIs ought to respect the former but not the latter. Possible approaches could come fropm Negarestani's view that reason and value can be liberated from biology, see [@NegarestaniIS]; Lem's perspective that there is no human project, only information processing and entropic resistance [@LemST]; Bach and Goertzel's arguments above around moral generalisation in advanced computational entities; or perhaps an argument based on Thacker that the apparent inability of philosophy to conceive of worlds without humans or without human value is itself a form of attachment endemic in Western philosophy [@Thacker].

There are other, more fundamental objections. One, similar to what Peter Wolfendale has argued in his counter to MacAskill's longtermism [@MacAskillWWO;@Wolfendale2022], is that legislating for the future at all might itself be objectionable. Another is that it is hard to see how thin values, alien values, or perhaps any values at all can be loaded into LLM-based systems, which through their training process are inculcated with a vast and heterogeneous body of knowledge that already contains a very wide range of values. This is close to the alignment problem as originally identified: it is difficult to point AI systems towards any coherent set of values at all.

This token-level representation can be communicated to humans most obviously in language, but also in images and audio. However, there are three subtleties. The actual computation is happening in a so-called latent space of high-dimensional matrices, so they don't literally "think in language".  The second subtlety is that during the generation of a single token (a forward pass), the model passes input tokens through hundreds or thousands of neural layers, processing it slightly at each stage, before finally being decoded back into language. From experiments, it seems a single forward pass enables surprisingly complex calculations [@roger2023hidingreasoning]. On this view, linguistic tokens could be seen as a serialising tool (along with chain-of-thought): they allow each one of these forward passes to be chained together, so as to enable thinking that is coherent over a given context length (sentence, a paragraph, a page, a book). There is an open research question on whether LLMs can be engineered so as to think entirely in a mathematical latent space, without first decoding them into natural language [@hao2024coconut]. These relatively technical considerations are joined by more philosophical questions of what belief and understanding actually mean, whether it is humans or LLMs in question [@HerrmannLevinstein2024a;@HerrmannLevinstein2024b;@shanahan2025_disembodied_being;].

[^ch0-humanvalues]: Following @gabriel_artificial_2020;@RussellHC, the term "human values" is used throughout this thesis as shorthand for the cluster of normative commitments, preferences, and evaluative dispositions characteristic of human beings as a species. I am aware that this is contested, particularly within AI ethics and in the critical humanities, where the immediate question is: "whose values"? The term is underspecified in the sense that values are bound to individuals, cultures, and historical moments, and no single formulation could represent eight billion people. Nevertheless, it seems useful at this projects' level of abstraction. The relevant contrast is not between one human culture/individual's values and another's, but between human-type values taken as a class and the values that a radically different mind (an ASI or an extraterrestrial intelligence) might hold. My background assumption (which I will not defend) is that the differences among human value systems are small relative to the distance between any human value system and a truly alien one. Where precision matters, the thesis distinguishes between thick values (culturally specific, richly textured) and thin values (abstract principles that might generalise across mind types), a distinction developed in Chapter 5, and provisionally presented here. For a specific approach to the related question of how to elicit and reconcile human values in ways that do not run into well-known choices in preference-based or quantitative aggregation frameworks see Oliver Klingefjord et al's moral graph elicitation[@klingefjord2024humanvalues]. See also the footnote on human-typed beings above.

[^ch0-value]: <!--TODO: get cites -->"Value" is a multivalent term used differently across disciplines. In analytic philosophy, axiology concerns what types of things have value or what constitutes the good (see Schroeder, "Value Theory," Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy). In sociology and social psychology, values (plural) refers to enduring beliefs held by individuals or populations about what is desirable or preferable (Rokeach, 1973; Schwartz, 1992). Values are sometimes conflated with preferences, a term from economics and decision theory denoting ordinal rankings over outcomes (von Neumann & Morgenstern, 1944), a conflation particularly prevalent in AI alignment literature, where "loading human values" may implicitly reduce axiological questions to preference orderings. Finally, desires and wants are psychological terms for motivational states directed at objects or outcomes, less commonly used in the alignment literature. It is worth noting that when alignment researchers invoke "human values," they typically operate closer to the sociological sense (the aggregated norms and preferences of existing human populations) rather than the axiological sense, though this is rarely made explicit. This is a substantive commitment: it presupposes that the values worth preserving are those humans currently hold, rather than those that would survive rational scrutiny or prove genuinely good across wider contexts. For the purposes of this project, "values" typically refers to the axiological sense: what is genuinely good or worth pursuing across long time frames and across cognitive distances.

[^ch0-define-human-typed-being]: "Human-typed" beings is (not surprisingly) closer to a family resemblence [@WittgensteinPI] rather than a biological or essentialist classification. A being is human-typed insofar as its psychology, social environment, or cultural (allowing for the great diversity of human cultures) inheritance remains substantially contiguous with current human forms, while allowing for technological augmentation or non-biological embodiment. This definition includes some uploads, cyborgs, and posthuman descendants, while excluding human-derived systems that have diverged sufficiently far from human forms of cognition and evaluation. The boundary is  underspecified and has difficult edge cases; it obviously doesn't settle questions of numerical personal identity, species membership, consciousness, or moral status. Different writers have handled the difficulties of specifying this family resemblance: David Roden, drawing on Derridean notions of the human lack-of-essence, allows that radically different (functionally and in terms of their autonomy) posthumans might nevertheless be treated as continuous with or descending from current human cultures, as our concepts and practices (art, literature, technology) acclimatise to their presence [@roden2010deconstruction;@roden2014posthuman]. Katherine Hayles is more conservative: in context of AIs/uploads (but pre-LLM), Hayles suggests that human continuity should not be treated as a purely informational, dematerialised, or substrate-independent feature. She sees human cognition and identity as something that is intimately bound up with our embodiment and our sociotechnical environments. This implies that it is entirely possible that AI typology would be similarly bound up with their sensorium, locomotive apparatus, substrate and relational environment though it isn't clear if she would endorse such a maximal extrapolation i.e. treating AIs as continuous with humans [@hayles1999posthuman]. Also useful is David Chalmers' work distinguishing several forms of uploading (e.g. likely-destructive cryonic brain-slicing vs invasive or non-invasive brain-scanning) and separates questions of consciousness, psychological continuity, and personal survival [@chalmers2014uploading].

[^ch0-intergalactic-spreading]: One of the more concrete specifications for how human-originated missions could rapidly, at least in cosmological terms, spread beyond the Milky Way is developed by Stuart Armstrong and Andrew Sandberg in [@ArmstrongSandberg2012]. Their exploratory-engineering study argues that a mature, highly automated civilisation could initiate settlement not merely of the Local Group but of much of the causally reachable universe. Their design is different from other von Neumann probe implementations because it does not require the starships to stop at intermediate points to replicate. Instead, their propose an enormous initial fan-out: probes are launched directly toward each reachable galaxy and, after arrival, construct local replicators and dispatch a second wave throughout that galaxy, allowing them to theoretically reach all stars that humanity can causally reach (owing to the expanding universe, most of the universe cannot be reached by ordinary, physically-plausible sub-light methods). In this document, the authors were trying to show that rapid colonisation of nearby galaxies is theoretically possible within billions of years, using technologies that humanity could foreseeably and plausibly develop over thousands or millions of years, and that the total mass and energy and mass required (to construct and launch the probes respectively) is available in the Solar System. [^ch0-failure]: Failure need not have the same methodological meaning across experimental practices. For instance, in science, an experiment can fail technically while succeeding epistemically, as when a prediction is disconfirmed by reliable evidence; null results are often as useful as positive results (something I see in my own AI research practice). However, success criteria are nevertheless ordinarily specified in advance with relative precision. In artistic practice, the aims and evaluative standards may themselves be transformed through making, so that failure relative to an original intention can acquire emergent artistic value [@andrzejewski2021failedart; @lefeuvre2010failure]. For instance, research comparing pharmaceutical development and music production suggest that, in both art and science, failure operates as a process through which practitioners reconsider methods and what is worth pursuing, but that this process differs in its details across domains [@otto2025failure]. Thought experiments may fail in an analogous productive sense: disagreement or “imaginative disharmony” can expose unstable assumptions, divergent interpretations or framing dependencies rather than simply yielding no philosophical result. On the other hand, they may fail because the depicted world is simply too far (psychologically speaking), too complex, or just too weird for the reader to form any reliable intuition, a risk that might apply to the Axiological Atlas [@molinari2022thought;@tuohimaa2025danger].
