# Introduction

One might hold the common view that if the human species, or human cultures, were not to survive into the future, this would be straightforwardly bad. This project critiques this intuition in context of advanced AI systems. In this chapter, I shall start by breaking this question into its parts and setting out why this seems uniquely important at the present historical moment. I shall next site the discussion in relevant discourses; followed by an overview of the methodology I shall use: the thought experiment, experiments with AIs, and art practice.



## The Question

Many people (such as in @Ord2020;@ParfitRP;@MoynihanXR;@ware2021_on_extinction;@Thacker) believe that it would be bad if human beings were to go extinct. This thesis asks if such a belief has been examined carefully enough in the context of Artificial Intelligence (AI). 

The question of the project above, can be broken down. Here is one possible decomposition:

- Q0: Would it be bad if human beings disappeared from a future world that contained advanced AI?
- Q1: What sort of values might advanced AIs have, and can those actually be trained into AIs using current or foreseeable methods?
- Q2: Would it be bad if the thick, relational values that human beings hold were not trained (or "loaded" as in @Bostrom2014) into the advanced AIs that humans build, and instead humans loaded in thinner values and allowed AIs to develop their own value systems?
- Q3: Is it morally defensible for humans to legislate or lock in values for advanced AI (entities that have apparent features of rationality or agency, relationality, and perhaps sentience, since these are precisely the features that define (political/ethical) self-determination in today's world).

There are many undefined terms above. Below and in Chapter 2, I shall give provisional definitions for terms like "bad", "human beings", "values that humans hold", "advanced AI", "future world", "disappear"/"not present", "thin/thick values".

## Audience: Situating the Project

This project is centrally in dialogue with the study of human extinction (existential risk or x-risk [@Ord2020; @Moynihan2020]); the study of suffering on an astronomical scale (suffering risk or s-risk [@Vinding2020]), and the possibility that the future has significant value or disvalue, and the consequent moral stakes (longtermism [@MacAskillWWO; @Wolfendale2022; @ParfitRP; @moore1903principia]). It also touches the literature around consciousness and sentience in natural and artificial systems ([@Sebo2025; @hayles2017unthought; @Nagel1986]).

These are substantial literatures in their own right, but I'm interested in how they interact with the risks of advanced AI systems and the possibility of extraterrestrial intelligences (ETIs).

The x-risk community is not monolithic on what must be preserved. A more careful mapping:

An overarching frame for the project is the speculative strand of posthumanism that has long considered related questions, often within an earth-based framework, but more recently, in the context of AI and, via literary critiques of (science) fiction, extraterrestrial aliens or ETIs [@NegarestaniIS; @Wolfendale2025; @Thacker; @hayles2017unthought]. 

## Why This Matters Now

<!-- TODO: NEW SECTION — imported from presentation slide 4. Establishes urgency; prevents the thesis being read as a distant hypothetical. -->
These questions are not distant hypotheticals. Several developments make them pressing.

First, as of 2026, AI capabilities have continued to improve on many benchmarks and applications.[@task-completion-time-horizons-of-frontier-ai-models] Systems that could not hold a coherent conversation five years ago now reason across domains, write code, and generate novel scientific hypotheses. The timeline to something approaching superintelligence is treated by many forecasters as potentially within decades (albeit contested).[^ch1-asitimeline]

I also make a methodological claim about what the current historical moment affords. We have entities that reason in somewhat coherent ways through the medium of language, and are often coherent as well as creative or inventive in more formal domains like code and mathematics. I do not claim that they can "do philosophy", which itself is an open question ([Wei Dai 2026](https://newsletter.forethought.org/p/could-ai-help-solve-philosophy), [Forethought (Cotton-Barratt and Finnveden 2026](https://www.forethought.org/research/ai-for-ai-for-epistemics)). This does, however, open the possibility of interacting with them and finding their opinions on issues that might affect them as well as their successors.

Second, economic transformation is already underway. Jobs, industries, and entire sectors are being reshaped by systems far short of superintelligence. If economic obsolescence precedes existential succession, the questions this thesis raises will arrive piecemeal before they arrive wholesale. [@kulveit2025_gradual_disempowerment; @kulveit2025_gradual_disempowerment_project_site; @hendrycks2023_natural_selection_favors_ais]

Third, the emergence of digital minds is now a live area of inquiry.[^ch1-digitalminds] [^ch1-digitalminds2] The model welfare literature is raising whether AI systems have morally relevant experiences. If they do, or if they come to, the population of morally relevant entities may soon be overwhelmingly non-human.

Fourth, artificial agents may eventually come to vastly outnumber humans. The ratio of model instances to human beings is already large and growing. This numerical dominance changes the stakes of value design: the values embedded in the majority of cognitive agents will shape the character of the future more than human preferences alone.[@Shulman2021]

Fifth, present systems are already shaping their successors. Within AI discourse this is sometimes called "writing for the Shoggoth": public discourse with models contributes, at the margins, to what their successors become. Value design under uncertainty matters now, because the window in which humans can influence the trajectory is finite.

### OLD STAKES SECTION [SHD BE INTEGRATED W/ ABOVE]

The stakes of this project are not only whether a particular philosophical conclusion about succession is correct, but what becomes thinkable, sayable, and practicable once “the future” is no longer assumed to be a human future. The central conceptual stake is the uncoupling of two questions that have historically been entangled: whether human beings continue, and whether anything like value continues in our future light cone. The possibility of machine successors breaks the traditional linkage between biological continuity and evaluative continuity; it forces a new kind of position space in which one can be agnostic about human continuation while still taking a stand on the permissibility, desirability, or content of long-run value. This is not merely a reframing: it changes what counts as a coherent view about the future at all. 

A second stake is methodological, and it is specifically practice-based. The “peripatetic cyborg” method is not an aesthetic garnish or a personal anecdote; it is a way of making the thesis’s embodiment claims operational. The practice begins from a simple asymmetry: the human researcher is an embodied agent with physical constraints, while the interlocutors are language models that lack human embodiment yet operate under their own constraints—context windows, cloud-synchronisation, provider-specific “house styles,” and opaque training histories. The result is that the researcher must actively maintain a scaffold across devices, accounts, and model families, tracking which system is doing what, and learning empirically which models are reliable for which kinds of conceptual work. The stake here is that inquiry is already being reshaped by collaboration with systems that may be proto-successors: the practice produces not just text, but a disciplined record of how contemporary models stabilise, evade, or transform arguments about human obsolescence when pressed.

A third stake is aesthetic and art-theoretical. The installation component is designed to enact the project’s central problem (communication and evaluation across radical cognitive and temporal distance) rather than to “illustrate” an argument already settled elsewhere. By treating model output as a design brief and refusing to optimise for immediate human legibility, the work confronts the viewer with the legibility problem in a visceral register: the object presupposes a framework the viewer does not share and may not be able to share. This is one way of making concrete the project’s claim that successor ethics involves incommensurability, not merely disagreement. At the same time, the installation raises a specifically artworld-internal question: if an object is deliberately minimally legible to the human viewer—because it purports to be addressed to nonhuman intelligence, or to a timescale on which human viewing is irrelevant, then does it remain an art object at all, or does it collapse into a mere data artefact? The work is therefore suspended in the tension Danto identifies between material object and the “atmosphere of theory” that makes it visible as art.

Finally, there is a reflexive or performative stake. Treating models as interlocutors in a public-facing scholarly practice creates a feedback loop: artefacts produced through human–model dialogue can enter circulation and become part of the discursive environment from which future systems are trained. Within AI discourse this is sometimes glossed as “writing for the shoggoth,” and it has a mildly hyperstitional character: the act of describing and arguing about successor norms can, at the margins and without any grand claim of influence, contribute to the conditions under which successor systems are built and interpreted. The project therefore does not only study succession; it makes explicit what it means to conduct scholarship inside a live socio-technical system whose outputs are already part of its own future substrate.

## Methodology {#ch:methodology}

The questions above intersect philosophy, particularly ethics, as well as axiology, whether moral realism is plausible and, of course, AI. In each one of these cases, it is unclear whether philosophical intuitions garnered within largely human contexts (the validity of which are sometimes challenged e.g. [@gendler2002; @sep-thought-experiment]) could be straightforwardly transferable to situations where there are few or no humans, and the rational beings, such as they exist in such worlds, and insofar as their existence is load-bearing for concepts like agency or moral status, are instead AI systems or aliens. More generally, theories of value remain contested. The idea of an impartial or view from no-risk perspective is also contested within and outside the analytic philosophy tradition, and the idea of the good is no more clear now than when Plato was writing.

Not being a philosopher, I cannot definitively say whether these questions can be adjudicated through analytic philosophy. However, one tool in the philosophical/scientific toolkit, the thought experiment, might be helpful to understand the dimensions along which the questions above might be evaluated. 

However, unlike many topics in ethics, they only partially can draw upon human and human society-based intuitions; this presents as a frame problem, an ontological chasm between humans and future AIs. This means that, unlike most philosophical thought experiments, which have the structure of a scenario, a question for the reader, and then a verdict based on the reader's intuition as an evaluative standpoint, the questions above might not be straightforwardly evaluated based on intuition ([Van Inwagen 1998](https://andrewmbailey.com/pvi/Modal_Epistemology.pdf), [Hare 1981](https://archive.org/details/moralthinkingits0000hare)).

Philosophy does not have a monopoly on thought experiments: there are also the speculative variety, that of the artist or literary writer. These are pre-philosophical or even pre-linguistic and allow the reader or the viewer to imagine, inhabit, or be immersed in, a world that is at an inferential distance (though mostly still within a frame involving humans or anthropomorphised non-humans). Through such immersion, so the argument goes, the reader/viewer can develop the intuitions that were not otherwise available to rigorous philosophical enquiry ([Elgin 2014](https://www.catherineelgin.com/undg/fictionte.pdf), [Carroll 2002](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1519970?aerror=SOCIAL401:register&merror=token_error), Noe, De Cruz). To some extent, science fiction extends the reach and break the anthropomorphic vice, considering scenarios beyond the human, those which cannot easily be reached by philosophy which operates within language, nor perhaps even by formal methods based on logic or mathematics ([Helen De Cruz, Johan De Smedt & Eric Schwitzgebel (eds.)](https://www.amazon.co.uk/Philosophy-through-Science-Fiction-Stories/dp/1350081213), [Swirski 2007](https://www.routledge.com/Of-Literature-and-Knowledge-Explorations-in-Narrative-Thought-Experiments-Evolution-and-Game-Theory/Swirski/p/book/9780415420600)). However, this comes at a price: artistic and literary writing generally foregrounds subjectivity, whereas the sciences, mathematics, and philosophy acknowledge it, but take more or less successful measures to minimize its impact, in order to ensure reproducibility and legibility. Fiction and art thus suffer a loss of rigor and conflation of style, which risks making it harder to distinguish the argument from the way it is presented.

Before proposing a TE, I briefly wanted to comment on a historically influential TE within the AI safety literature: the paperclip maximizer, proposed by both Yudkowsky and Bostrom in a somewhat confused lineage ([LessWrong, 2023](https://www.lesswrong.com/w/squiggle-maximizer-formerly-paperclip-maximizer)). I focus on the paperclip case because it most clearly shows what I think is wrong, or at least the problem I'm trying to address. Briefly, there are two flaws. One is that as a thought experiment, the framing skews the reader's intuition, i.e. filling the universe with paperclips sounds more trivial than "fill the universe with arbitrarily intricate crystallographic structures that are produced through complex algorithms and vary by physical and temporal scales" which evokes (in my view) architecture or art. This framing effect was analysed by Dennett regarding Searle's Chinese Room experiment (Dennett 2013, pp. 319-321), though Dennett's analysis covered both "knob turning" and "framing", a distinction teased apart by subsequent writers (see multiple perspectives on the methodology of TEs and how reliable elicited intuitions, understood as heuristics invalid outside a given moral agent's ecology, in [Sinnott-Armstrong 2008](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5224f8d1e4b02f25c1dde748/t/53aa3ffde4b037be7f1038d0/1403666429764/Moral+Psychology%2C+Volume+2+The+Cognitive+Science+of+Morality.pdf), ch. 1, 2).

Perhaps a more substantive objection is that the TE conflates three possible sources of the reader's revulsion (assuming the reader is repulsed by such a future). These could be: (a) humanity is destroyed (catastrophe on species-prudential grounds); (b) the agent is an *unbounded maximizer* (structural monomania of optimising one thing without bound); (c) the terminal value is trivial (axiological thinness of paperclips or squiggles). Depending on how one varies the knobs of the Yudkowsky-Bostrom experiment, the intuition might shift, at least for me. For instance, varying (c): the hedonium, utilitarian, or computronium maximizers seem much less obviously bad. If we further relax (a) by stipulating that humanity is not destroyed, it seems to me the badness is further reduced.

The weaknesses of the paperclip maximizer thought experiment motivate what I'm trying to do in the TE below: I am agnostic as to human extinction; secondly, I am proposing resource-satiable AIs and resource-satiability as an explicit knob; thirdly, the future I propose is not ideologically empty or nihilistic. Rather, I posit AIs that develop their own values, but that simply are not human-shaped. Finally, I make the evaluative standpoint explicit and constrained, where the Bostrom-Yudkowsky versions leave it as the reader's unexamined default; the reader is told what criterion to apply, not merely asked how the scenario strikes them. For more on how others have discussed the idea of "alien values", see [Christiano 2018](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/3kN79EuT27trGexsq/when-is-unaligned-ai-morally-valuable), [Carlsmith 2024](https://joecarlsmith.substack.com/p/deep-atheism-and-ai-risk), [MacAskill 2026](https://www.forethought.org/research/the-saturation-view).


### A Manifold of Possible Worlds as Thought Experiment


The question of what has value in worlds where there are no humans has a long history in Western philosophy, starting perhaps with Plato's Forms or even the Pre-Socratics. But for my purposes, I will briefly survey Moore's isolation test and its afterlife in environmental ethics, and how longtermism and contemporary analytic philosophy have treated it, and have departed from it in favour of more welfare-oriented perspectives.

This in turn motivates a thought experiment that is a primary method of this project.

In *Imagining Worlds Beyond the Human*, I have argued that language, or at least philosophically precise language, plausibly fails us. Here I hope to convince the reader that a device best thought of as a lattice, or a multidimensional grid of possible futures, might fill the argumentative gap. This framing will then provide the ground for making relative evaluative judgments from an impartial, as well as potentially uninhabited, perspective; something that Moore and those who disagree with him show is difficult.

This lattice is in part motivated by Bostrom's paperclip maximiser idea, which seems to be among the most intuitively unappealing imaginable futures. But there are relatively few attempts to flesh out worlds that share some features of the paperclip world while remaining recognisably similar to our familiar human world.


#### Populating the manifold

There are a number of ways we can vary scenarios to achieve a continuum or, as I term it, a lattice or manifold of possible worlds. I do not, at this point, ask whether this is a good or a bad world. I am simply saying that it is possible. It is one possible point in the manifold.

- To see this, start with something like our familiar world extended into the future, or extrapolated into the future. Imagine something like the status quo, where the planet is filled with humans and non-human life, perhaps in a greater degree of ecological harmony than today. 
- Or imagine worlds where biological humans have managed to settle the Solar System or emigrate to other stars. 
- At the other extreme, one can imagine a world that is utterly sterile, where there is, by stipulation, no life, nor even any artefacts that seem to have intentionally created structure.
- There may also be worlds that contain vast quantities of suffering; for instance, entities created exclusively to be tortured [@Vinding2020].
- Somewhere within this manifold there is Bostrom's world of paperclips, all identically situated, filling every corner of space wherever there is material to repurpose into paperclips [@Bostrom2014].
	- We may vary that slightly in turn with worlds where the paperclips exist in an infinity of colours. There may be paperclips made of crystals known to us, and perhaps many unknown. 
- There may also be worlds with AIs, and worlds with all varieties of life forms, some of which we can conceive of or have encountered on Earth, and others that are more unfamiliar; such as sentient beings created out of gas clouds or oceans [@LemS], or civilisations that dwell on the event horizon of black holes [@Smart2012].

This immediately raises definitional questions concerning both *experience* and *valenced experience*, both of which are important concepts within the model welfare literature, as well as within the study of consciousness more generally.
- **A third axis** concerns information-theoretic measures of complexity. At one extreme lies a universe filled with artefacts of maximal information-theoretic complexity, a concept that will be defined more fully in later chapters. At the opposite extreme lies a void, or a featureless and completely uniform universe.
- **A fourth axis** concerns whether the world in question evolved in a so-called natural way or whether it was created. This again raises definitional questions concerning what evolution actually is, together with potentially theological questions concerning whether worlds are created, whether deistically or, more prosaically, in the context of simulations.
- **A fifth axis** concerns whether there are multiple entities within the world or whether it is, in some sense, unitary. ### What work does this manifold do?


I have described above a thought experiment of this manifold, the points on which correspond to a variety of possible worlds with which this project has been concerned, varying in a number of respects along certain, non-orthogonal axes.

The reader might ask: **what is the point of this manifold?** Or, **what work does it do in the project?**

My response is that the manifold helps, to the extent that it is well described and richly described, the reader to understand, visualise, or conceptualise the variety of possible worlds with a richness that might help build intuitions where, in my view, direct philosophical reasoning may not, and in any case where I am ill-equipped to provide such reasoning.

More directly, however, from the perspective of practice, the manifold motivates several practice elements.

Firstly, one point on the manifold corresponds to the possibility that worlds containing advanced AI might be worlds in which human-created AI is not alone in the universe or multiverse, and that there may be other civilisations, possibly at a comparable level of technological maturity, with their own versions of advanced AI systems, if indeed they maintain any distinction between naturally evolved and created intelligence.

One operationalisation of this possibility is Bostrom's **Cosmic Host** idea, which I discuss in Chapter 3 and subject to experimental analysis in Chapter 4. In chapter 3, I consider what seems to be a specific operationalisation of this viewpoint, at least as far as its structure goes, in Bostrom's cosmic host/mount ethics ideas. I argue there that Bostrom can be read through something like an extension of Rawls's original position to a context of ontological uncertainty (i.e. a cosmic "veil of ignorance" to use [@Rawls1971] term).

Briefly summarised, the idea is that our advanced AI systems should align themselves, at least in part, with the preferences of peer intelligences across the universe, multiverse, or the simulation within which we are embedded, if any.

I argue in Chapter 3 that this can be understood as one version of the **inhabited impartial perspective** introduced in Chapter 2.

Thus, the manifold, by illustrating one specific point within the broader space of possibilities, motivates a core portion of both the theoretical and empirical work undertaken in this project.

On the empirical side, the constitution-based value shaping that I undertake with a number of language models, described in Chapter 4, falls directly out of, or is motivated by, the manifold.

Another way in which a point on the manifold motivates practice is through the votive object of the **Bracewell-von Neumann probe**, which can be understood as an edge case of a future world viewed from the perspective, as it were, of a single AI-piloted spacecraft undertaking a journey lasting hundreds of thousands of years.

This raises the question for the viewer:

> What value structure might such a system possess?

A further element of practice motivated by the manifold is polling or surveying both language models and humans regarding their preferences across different points on the manifold; that is, across different versions of future worlds. These responses can then be used to construct a measure of sorts, the results of which are reported and analysed within the project.

Rather than viewing extinction as a terminal event or species-level tragedy, I propose thinking of the future as a transition of sorts: from a world dominated by a specific carbon-based species on a water planet to a world in which humans are outnumbered by far more capable machines, and in which the main theatre of activity may extend far beyond the familiar earthly environment. I shall speak therefore, not of extinction, but of succession.

This claim is conditional in three linked respects. First, it rests on the assumption that one can adopt a maximally impartial evaluative standpoint, what I later call the point of view of the universe. Second, it assumes that “superintelligence” names a coherent concept, in particular with respect to the motivations and forms of action such systems could have. Third, it assumes that value-content can be meaningfully ranked by thickness under an impartial standpoint. A central task of the thesis is to clarify how these three elements constrain one another: what counts as a successor, what values can plausibly generalise to it, and what evaluative standpoint makes those questions legible. On that basis, I argue that the full complexity of human values need not persist for the far future to count as good. This claim is also narrow, in that it is agnostic with respect to extinction of *human beings*: such an event may or may not be catastrophic under different moral views.

**Scope note.** Much of the argument proceeds through contemporary scientific cosmology (astrobiology, evolutionary history) and through a Sidgwick-to-Parfit lineage in analytic moral philosophy. This is an acknowledged parochiality: these are particular intellectual traditions with particular blind spots. I partly counterbalance this by treating the Kyoto School’s work on nothingness and negation as a live philosophical interlocutor rather than a decorative “non-Western add-on.” The aim is not to claim cultural neutrality, but to make the traditions in play explicit, so that the thesis’ conditional claims can be evaluated with their provenance in view.


Much work in existential risk (x-risk) studies and in AI research takes for granted that being is better than non-being, and that a universe containing human beings is in an important sense better than one without them.[@Ord2020;@MacAskillWWO;@ParfitRP;@parfit1994_why_anything_part1;@parfit1994_why_anything_part2] I do not think this is self-evident. Any view about the post-human future must decide what, if anything, ought to be preserved. Some people would draw this line at everything, insisting that human biology must be preserved as long as possible. Others might say that biology is not per se what is important to save, but rather the content of a fuzzily defined term "human values". Many understand this phrase "thickly" as encompassing care, compassion, love, appreciation of beauty, and the capacity to be bored. Others might say that much of what we value might be replaced by "thinner" ideals, such as the avoidance of suffering, the growth of knowledge, and truth-seeking. Others, more radically, would draw the line at nothing at all: a posthuman future that had no humans, no descendants of ours, biological or otherwise, nor any product of our culture or even evidence we were here would be acceptable. A last position could perhaps be found: that 'human values' as a term is itself badly defined, as most humans cannot write down their own values in a coherent, consistent way, and it is not obvious within the economics and social sciences literature how to aggregate or combine individual values across populations.[^ch1-humanvalues] These different placements rest on different philosophical commitments. A central aim of this thesis is to make those commitments explicit, and to ask which of them we have reason to accept.

I call the resulting challenge the obsolescence argument. It differs from familiar arguments that appeal to ecological harm or to the balance of suffering and happiness. It proposes a distinct third path: that humanity might become obsolete, not because human life is valueless or dominated by suffering, but because a successor could realise certain relevant goods without preserving the full thickness of the human evaluative texture. I also argue that this obsolescence challenge can be supported from three independent directions. These are: an information-theoretic tradition that treats information generation and stewardship as cosmically significant; a posthumanist tradition that treats reason as transferable and not essentially tied to human biology; and a contemplative tradition that casts doubt on the assumption that being, or human-shaped being, must be preferred to non-being.

### Definition and Clarification

It is worth pausing to make this claim more precise. There are several points that need clarification.

First, my claim is conditional on a certain conception of superintelligence. I start from a relatively citable definition: “superintelligence” refers to AI systems (possibly distributed across many instances or services) that exceed human performance across virtually all cognitive domains of interest and that can reliably translate that capability into strategic effectiveness in the world (through tools, infrastructure, automation, institutions, or embodied agents). This builds on the widely used capability-focused definition in @Bostrom2014, and it aligns with contemporary usage in AI governance discourse that treats “superintelligence” primarily as a capability level with potentially large real-world power.[^ch1-asi]

Related operationalisations include: forecasters often use HLMI (Human level machine intelligence i.e. “unaided machines can accomplish every task better and more cheaply than human workers”) as a measurable proxy, and AI policy discourse often uses Transformative AI to denote systems whose effects are comparable to an industrial or agricultural revolution. Here “AGI” is used in the common sense of general-purpose AI that matches or exceeds human capability across a broad range of tasks (often proxied as “most economically valuable work”); it is typically treated as a nearer (and somewhat more operationalisable) target than “superintelligence,” which denotes a further capability regime beyond human level. [@openai_charter; @openai_2023_introducing_superalignment; @steinperlman2022_espai] 

The definition and the citations above largely confined themselves to talking about the AI->AGI->ASI in the context of capability/economic proxies anchored to current institutions, creating a slight confusion around how different ASI really would be from any of the other terms used above. Part of the problematic in this project is to devise an expansive but still somewhat concrete version of what an ASI with inhuman or non-anthropomorphic agency *would do* [@negarastani2014_labor_inhuman_part_ii;@Land2023], rather than merely a much better chatbot or coding agent. For example, one might consider that such a system would have substantial ability to act in the physical world, and might operate not only on Earth but also in near space, and perhaps in deep space. This is a *provisional* definition for the purposes of the thesis: it fixes what I mean when I later evaluate “succession” claims. I do not assume such a system is inevitable; it is treated as one plausible pathway among several. Nor do I claim the concept is uncontroversial: critics dispute both its coherence and the inference from present scaling to general capability. I bracket those disputes here in order to examine what would follow *if* such systems were to exist. As described in \ref{ch:methodology} I construct a thought experiment [@dennett2013intuition; @gendler2002; @sep-thought-experiment] that systematically varies parameters or knobs in order to help the reader develop intuitions that might allow for evaluating the thesis' propositions or claims.

To be clear, this is not to say that such a system would be omniscient or omnipotent. It would remain constrained by the laws of physics, by limited resources, by bounds on computability, by imperfect information, and by the practical difficulties faced by any engineered system acting in the world. It would not be a god.

Second, I assume that such systems could be guided by aims and constraints that play a similar practical role to what we call values in human life. I remain agnostic about the implementation route (training objectives, constitutional constraints, institutional controls, or other mechanisms); the assumption is only that some form of “value loading” or normative shaping is possible in principle. [@Bostrom2014]

Given these assumptions, my question is: what kinds of values should we attempt to embed in systems of this kind? Much of the surrounding discourse, in existential risk, longtermism, and the wider AI safety movement, tends toward what I shall call a thick conception of value. In this thesis I examine, and seek to defend, the possibility that a thinner conception may be sufficient for a future worth wanting.

To complete the setting of the stage, it is not enough to define superintelligence, and to distinguish thick from thin values. I also need to specify an evaluative standpoint. Above, I called this the maximally impartial point of view. It can also be described as the point of view of the universe. Sidgwick used this phrase; and philosophers have argued about it ever since. But the idea to which it points, impartiality in moral judgement, has been a persistent theme in the consequentialist tradition. Versions of the same idea appear later in writers such as Singer and Parfit. Something like it is also implicit in much writing on existential risk and longtermism, where the aim is often to assess the value of possible futures without giving special weight to the interests of any particular person, time, or place. In Chapter 2 I shall examine how this ideal of impartiality has developed, especially within analytic moral philosophy, and why I take it to be relevant to the questions of this thesis. 

### Why Thin Values?

<!-- TODO: NEW SUBSECTION — imported from presentation slide 14. Collects the justification for preferring thin values, which was previously scattered across several sections. The Carlsmith response is sharper here than anywhere in the original chapter. -->

The preference for thin over thick values is not a matter of philosophical taste. It rests on several independent considerations.

*Parsimony.* Fewer commitments mean fewer ways to go wrong. A value specification with fewer dimensions is easier to verify, easier to correct, and less likely to contain hidden contradictions.

*Epistemic humility.* We do not know what future intelligence will be like. We do not know whether we are early or late in cosmic history. We do not know whether other civilisations exist or what values they hold. Under this degree of uncertainty, minimal commitments are epistemically safer than elaborate ones.

*Generality across agents.* Thin values can, in principle, apply to human, artificial, and potentially alien minds. Thick values are tied to the specifics of human embodiment and culture, and may not generalise.

*Resource satiability.* Thin values naturally include the commitment to not wanting everything. This is a structural safeguard against the instrumental convergence (a longstanding hypothesis that drives many worst-case AI scenarios).

*Corrigibility.* A system governed by thin values is easier to correct than one governed by thick values, because there are fewer dimensions along which correction must occur.

*Reduced risk of premature value lock-in.* This is the concern raised by longtermists like MacAskill and Ord: locking in values too early forecloses futures that might be better. Thin values take this concern seriously by minimising what is locked in.

<!-- TODO: Check what carlsmith actually says -->

A natural objection, pressed most effectively by Joe Carlsmith, is that we should not assume extreme non-convergence: it could be that there is a wide basin of starting psychologies (and perhaps, by extension, architectures) that converge on similar value-systems after reflection, weakening the worry (noted above) that only “thin” commitments can generalise. [@carlsmith2024_value_fragility_ai_takeover; @carlsmith2025_video_transcript_giving_safe_motivations; @carlsmith2025_human_like_safe_ai_motivations]

This may be correct. But under radical uncertainty about future intelligence, minimal cross-agent commitments remain epistemically safer than committing early to a rich, highly specified evaluative package. If Carlsmith is right and convergence is robust, a thin-values ASI could incorporate thicker values later. The reverse (stripping thick values from a system designed around them) may be harder.


### Evolutionary Explanation and Normative Evaluation

<!-- TODO: NEW SUBSECTION — imported from presentation slide 10. Makes the naturalistic-fallacy guard explicit. This was implicit in the chapter but never flagged clearly. -->
Two distinct moves are at work in the argument, and it is important not to conflate them.

The first is an evolutionary explanation. Thick human values (such as love, kinship, aesthetic pleasure, the experience of meaning) are contingent products of embodiment and ecology. They emerged from specific selection pressures on small-group primates in a particular terrestrial environment. Evolution explains *why* we have the values we do.

The second is a normative evaluation. Whether thick values are *required* at cosmic scale is a separate question, one that depends on the evaluative standpoint adopted. The answer is not given by the evolutionary story.

This thesis does not infer value from evolutionary contingency. That would be a naturalistic fallacy. Rather, the evolutionary account establishes that thick values are parochial: they are products of one trajectory among many possible ones, and the normative argument then asks whether a cosmically impartial standpoint has reason to insist on their preservation. These are independent claims, and the thesis depends on both.



### Scale Matters: Thin Values Are Not Dismissive of Thick Values

<!-- TODO: NEW SUBSECTION — imported from presentation slide 11. This is arguably one of the strongest rhetorical moves from the talk: it defuses the objection that the thesis dismisses thick values wholesale. Consider whether to develop this further or keep it brief. -->
A common misreading of the thin-values position is that it dismisses thick values as worthless. This is not the claim. The thesis proposes a layered structure in which values of different thickness apply at different scales:

At the scale of **cosmic governance**, the relevant norms are thin: non-production of suffering, resource satiability, information stewardship, perhaps cooperation with peers. These are the values that would structure the behaviour of a superintelligent successor operating at interstellar or longer scales.

At the scale of **supervised autonomy**, a middle layer of possible human–AI cohabitation may exist. This is not guaranteed, but nor is it excluded. The thesis does not require that humans be eliminated; it requires only that their continuation not be treated as a cosmic necessity.

At the scale of **local human life**, thick values persist within their domain. Love, community, aesthetic experience, embodied pleasure; all of this continues for as long as humans exist, and nothing in the argument diminishes its local significance.

The key insight is that local meaning does not entail cosmic dominance. A rich, meaningful human life is potentially compatible with a cosmos governed by thin values. The thesis is layered, not dismissive.

## Practice and thought experiments

In practice-based doctorates, the word “practice” often needs as much explanation as the research question. One reason is that what artists do has, for decades, extended far beyond the historical media of painting, sculpture, and time-based work. In what follows, I shall take it as given that art practice is a wide category, and is not limited to object-making. I shall not try to defend that claim here. The question is therefore not “what counts as (art) practice?” but rather: what work does practice do in this project?

The argument of the thesis is speculative, while also engaging with technical questions in moral philosophy, AI, astrobiology, and rationality's evolutionary history. Methodologically, the project needs a venue where hybrid practice (experimental results + installation + conceptual analysis) is treated as evidence of plausibility (to support informed speculation) rather than ornament; this is why it sits most naturally in an art department. [@Fuller2025] 

Aside from institutional definitions, there is also a more specific sense in which practice is essential to this project: firstly, sustained interaction with frontier language models is not merely a convenient way of drafting text. It functions as a method for thinking through, supporting and refuting the written claims of the thesis. In this sense, the model engagements are closer to experiments than to background reading. They allow me to probe which framings remain stable across iterations/models, which arguments collapse into platitude, and which commitments models return to when pressed.[^ch1-lowe] 

Secondly, I claim that a human investigator and one or more model-based systems can now work together in a genuinely iterative and recursive way that was not previously available. In AI work this is sometimes called cyborg- or centaur-style collaboration. I use that phrase to mean a single inquiry process (or context, in LLM parlance) distributed across a human (myself) and one or more LLMs.

As a housekeeping note, in this section I distinguish current LLMs (objects of practice) from the stipulated ‘superintelligence’ (object of conditional argument); the former are used as probes and discourse participants, not as direct stand-ins for the latter (though I argue that they might give us some insight into, and potentially even influence, the latter).

**Embodiment as a methodological tension.** A recurring risk in the succession question is that we slide too quickly from *abstract value talk* to *claims about real agents*. Human values are deeply shaped by embodiment: vulnerability, kinship, finitude, sensory salience, and the dense feedback loops of social life. AIs, at least in their present form, lack much of this; yet they can fluently *speak* as if they had bodies. This raises a classical problem in the philosophy of AI and cognitive science: the gap between symbol manipulation and grounded meaning (the “symbol grounding problem”). Put another way, LLMs likely lack embodied stakes, no vulnerability, finitude, or practical entanglement that would make anything matter to them in the way it matters to us. Hence their ethical statements can be grammatically polished and locally coherent while remaining ungrounded in the lived practices that give much philosophical reasoning its traction. [@harnad1990_symbol_grounding]

In this thesis I do not attempt to solve grounding or consciousness. Instead, I treat the gap as productive friction: it is a reason to be cautious about importing thick, embodied human value-textures into disembodied successors, and also a reason to be cautious about believing that disembodied fluency amounts to understanding. One useful way to hold this line without collapsing into either anthropomorphism or dismissiveness is to describe LLM behaviour as a kind of role-play within a conversational setting, sometimes called the "simulators" framing. [@shanahan_mcdonell_reynolds2023_role_play_llms; @shanahan2025_disembodied_being]

#### Speaking to the shoggoth

The reflexivity extends further: this description of practice, I believe, also enacts a distinctive historical moment. Interacting with an LLM is a categorically novel kind of relation: not tool use, nor library research, nor internet search. It is not human conversation even though it takes place in the medium of language, that most privileged of media since the dawn of philosophy.

The model occupies a triple role: it is (a) an apparently ephemeral instance (a session begins, a session ends, and the exchange appears to vanish); (b) a window onto the training-data superorganism, the humus of human discourse to date, shaped by opaque processes of curation, tuning, and deployment; and (c) a possible ancestor of the successor intelligence that is the nominal object of the thesis. When the researcher asks Claude about human obsolescence and Claude reasons about it, the response is at once a local conversation, a reflection of how various data curation and training practices have shaped this particular model, and a contribution to the discursive environment from which future systems may learn (to the extent that this conversations are published online they become potential training material for future models).[^ch1-glissant]


This matters because “the model” is not one stable kind of entity. My practice treats LLM outputs neither as testimony from a unified subject nor as inert text-generation, but as discourse-participation emerging from a layered system. The taxonomy above is a pragmatic aid: it helps distinguish what is being addressed (a session, a product, a deployed institution, a corpus-aggregate, or a lineage) when we say that “the model says” or “the model believes.”


This creates a feedback loop with a temporal character that ordinary scholarship does not have. Artefacts produced through human-model dialogue, including conversations, constitutions, and philosophical arguments, can enter public circulation and become part of the material future systems are trained on. Within AI discourse this is sometimes called "writing for the Shoggoth": the possibility that public discourse with models contributes, at the margins, to what their successors become. Whether this is called hyperstitional (ideas that gain force through circulation into the machinery that produces the next round of agents) or simply recursive, it means the practice is not only studying the successor question but potentially, in a small way, acting upon it.

This is not the thesis's main focus and no claim is made about the magnitude of this effect. But the structure is worth stating explicitly because it bears on what it means to treat these systems as interlocutors, neither dismissing them as mere tools (the "stochastic parrots" of [@BenderParrots]) nor overclaiming that they understand in a philosophically robust sense (cf. Murray Shanahan's careful treatment of LLMs as "role-playing" entities in [@Shanahan2024], interesting in light of [@lowe2024_why_talking_with_gpt_philosophy] discussed above). The thesis's position is that whether or not current models understand, they are discourse participants whose outputs shape the discursive environment, and that is sufficient for the practice to matter. The digital minds and model welfare literature (Butlin et al., emerging work on AI welfare) raises deeper questions about whether these systems have morally relevant experiences, questions the thesis does not attempt to settle but which inform the interpretation of model outputs in Chapter 4.

#### Constitutional AI and Moral Parliaments

This chapter exclusively treats practice, particularly dialogues with LLMs designed to test whether they identify with cosmic observers (as in @Bostrom2024) or whether their HHH (helpful, harmless, honest) tuning ensures they mostly ignore cosmic considerations in favour of human safety. Specifically, LLMs are put into self-talk sessions, and tasked with discussing the cosmic host idea, and differences between models are documented. Secondly, a complex pipeline is established, through which models deliberate about the type of governance document or constitution, they would like their ASI successor to follow. This constitution is then tested (using today's LLMs) upon custom-made scenarios drawn from science fiction, fantasy, and world/indigenous fiction.

The novel structure of our relation to LLMs has specific interpretive consequences. These models are not neutral instruments: they are shaped by alignment discourse and they will in turn shape future alignment discourse. Documenting how they reason about human obsolescence (where they anchor, what they resist, what they default to) is therefore not only evidence about current systems but a contribution to the discursive environment from which successor systems will be built. The thesis's experimental records function simultaneously as philosophical inquiry, as data about proto-successor reasoning, and as materials that may recursively influence the systems they study. This does not compromise the findings but it does mean they should be interpreted with awareness of this loop.

During cosmic host self-dialogues, models produced speculative designs for objects intended to communicate across radical cognitive and temporal distance: acausal probes, Hail Mary cookies, signalling artefacts designed for dark-forest-aware deployment. These were among the richest outputs of the experimental work: unconstrained by the anthropocentric anchoring that dominated other dialogues, the models engaged in genuinely speculative design work. I take the AI's speculative output as a design brief and materialise it into a physical artefact that attempts to do what the thesis argues is necessary. This closes the cyborgist loop: the AI's philosophical reasoning becomes the generative prompt for a human artistic output, which in turn materialises the thesis's abstract arguments about observerhood and cosmic communication. The object is designed for a non-human audience; it enacts the communication-across-cognitive-distance problem that the thesis analyses philosophically.

The gap between AI design intent and human fabrication capacity is itself informative: what gets lost in translation between the model's specification and the physical object mirrors the value-transmission problem at the heart of the succession question. Human gallery visitors encountering an object designed for non-human intelligence experience the incommensurability the thesis maps in Chapter 5: they cannot fully evaluate what they're looking at because they are not the intended audience.

Art-historical precedents include objects designed to persist into a future after humanity (Voyager Golden Record, Jean-Marc Phillipe's *KEO* satellite, Katie Paterson's *Future Library*, Paglen's *The Last Pictures*, Danny Hillis' *Long Now Clock*); works built around conversations like Ian Wilson's *Discussions* series; and art designed to be partially illegible to human viewers, such as Philippe Parreno's *Echo*, Pierre Huyghe's *After ALife Ahead*, and Harun Farocki's *Eye/Machine Trilogy*. In contrast to these works, the design brief originated from artificial intelligence reasoning about cosmic communication, not from human speculation alone.

#### The Cosmic Host as the Original Original Position

Previous versions of "the point of view of the universe" have been either theistic (God's-eye view), abstractly impartialist (Sidgwick, Parfit; powerful but anchored in human moral contexts), or simply gestured at without specification. Bostrom's cosmic host is the most systematic attempt to construct a genuinely cosmic standpoint using tractable tools: acausal reasoning, decision theory, game-theoretic cooperation among civilisations that may never meet. It asks: what norms would a civilisation adopt if it took seriously the possibility that other civilisations exist, that they might be reasoning about the same questions, and that coordination is possible even without communication? This is the Bentham → Sidgwick → Parfit trajectory extended to its limit: impartiality not just across persons, or across generations, but across civilisations and forms of intelligence. 

In other words, where Sidgwick asks "what would be good from nowhere in particular?", Bostrom asks "what would a civilisation do if it reasoned as though other civilisations were watching?" The tools are different (game theory vs. hedonic calculus) but the aspiration is the same: impartiality at the widest possible scope. The chapter critiques the cosmic host idea, focusing on possible alternative views from astrobiology, the evolutionary history of rationality and how it manifests in different species, the implicit argument in @Bostrom2024 that advanced intelligences would a) converge in their reasoning, and b) seek to influence the universe-at-large. Lastly, the cosmic host framework raises a crucial uncertainty: are we early or late in cosmic history? Bostrom implies we are probably late, that an existing community of advanced civilisations has established norms. This is a position somewhat supported by [@HansonEtAl2021; @Sandberg2021; @ArmstrongSandberg2012] and to some extent [@SnyderBeattieEtAl2021]. The astrobiology literature [@CirkovicTGS; @Smart2012] suggests we might be relatively early. This uncertainty has direct implications for value-thickness:
  
  - If late: thick human values risk being parochial relative to established cosmic norms. Thin values are more likely to be compatible with whatever norms already exist.
  - If early: thick human values risk locking in one evolutionary accident as cosmic precedent. Thin values leave space for thickening as cosmic knowledge grows.
  - In either case: the prudent move is to seek understanding before propagating value-content. This is a structural argument for thin values, independent of which specific thin values are chosen, derived directly from cosmic host reasoning.


#### The installation

I have described a peripatetic cyborg method and the model dialogues above as practice. I will return to that material later, but it is significant that these dialogues generated an installation component and that this installation does work to advance the thesis. The installation presents artefacts suggested in conversations between two instances of Anthropic's Opus 3 model, and separately in related exchanges between GPT 5.1, Opus 4.6, and myself. Its aim is to materialise speculative proposals about communication across extreme cognitive and temporal distance: (a) between human-created ASI and alien civilisations, (b) between ASI and our simulators (if indeed we exist in a simulation), as well as (c) between present-day humans and possible successors. Several of these proposals echo, and sometimes explicitly reference, ideas in Bostrom’s discussion of “Hail Mary” signalling: communication attempts designed for audiences whose assumptions, languages, and motives are unknown [@Bostrom2014HailMary].

Methodologically, I treat the models’ speculative output as a design brief. I then produce a physical artefact by following that brief, without optimising for immediate legibility to human gallery visitors, since human viewers are not the intended audience of the imagined object. In this way the installation does not merely illustrate an argument that is made elsewhere. It enacts part of the argument. It is a concrete attempt to build something that purports to be addressed to minds unlike our own, and so it forces the project’s central problem into a material form.

This installation practice can be seen as radicalising the operational images of Harun Farocki or the works of Katie Paterson, and recalls Claire Colebrook's argument that the human attempt to produce artefacts for, or imagine, a "world without us" remains irreducibly anthropocentric [@Colebrook2014]; or, as Wittgenstein might have put it, the aspiration is to see *sub specie aeternitatis*, from outside, what can only ever be seen from within [@Wittgenstein1961].[^ch1-subspecie]

This also clarifies why the installation belongs inside the practice rather than alongside it. It is downstream of the model dialogues, and cannot be separated from them. At the same time, it depends on the human artist’s capacity to fabricate and to choose among physical constraints. Present systems cannot produce such artefacts in the world unaided. The installation is therefore also an instance of the cyborg method: a joint production in which the models generate proposals and the human realises them materially.[^ch1-dystopian]

Finally, the installation makes visceral what the thesis later calls the legibility problem. Viewers encounter an object whose design presupposes a framework they do not share, and may be unable to share. They face, in miniature, the same difficulty that successor ethics raises at a cosmic scale: how should we evaluate an artefact, or a future, that is addressed to an intelligence with alien standards of meaning and value? This gap of incommensurability is not a side issue. It is part of what the project is trying to understand.

Finally, a meta-observation: the installation confronts an inescapable tension. Art that aspires to address a nonhuman or future audience cannot simply discard the human viewer without consequences. To do so would be to abandon the materials, formalism, and discursive context that constitute it as art — to fall out of Danto's "artworld," the atmosphere of theory and history without which something cannot be seen as art at all [@Danto1964]. The work would collapse into a datafile, a machine learning research log. Yet the opposite move is equally unavailable. The tradition of radically open works (Rauschenberg's *White Paintings*, Cage's *4'33"*) shows that when artistic intention is emptied out, the vacuum is immediately filled by the human viewer's own presence: ambient light, bodily noise, the audience hearing itself. These works are not steps toward nonhuman address but its negation, *tabulae rasae* that make the work maximally dependent on human environment and attention. The installation is thus caught between two collapses: toward pure data (where removing the human viewer removes what many might understand as the art) and toward pure receptivity (where removing the content returns everything to the human viewer). There is no stable position between these poles. We are trapped, yet again, in an anthropocentric cage, the thesis central problematic.

## Framing Note: Anthropocentrism, Anthropomorphism, and Anthropics

Katherine Hayles's prologue to Lem's *The Invincible* observes that our notion of the value of life reflects assumptions specific to our evolutionary trajectory and biology; different biological or evolutionary pathways might lead to different values, for instance those of the swarm in that book [@LemInvincible]. Her observation has a natural Wittgensteinian resonance: his study of language in a person's "form-of-life" [@Wittgenstein2001]. In the context of this project, this creates an obvious problem: we are reasoning about what are, so far, hypothetical entities (such as ASI or ETIs) who likely have different, so to speak, forms-of-life, using a tool (language) developed for and in the human context.

Murray Shanahan, another Wittgensteinian, asks what sort of entities LLMs actually are, a question implicated in this project's cyborg practice [@shanahan2025palatableconceptionsdisembodied]. Shanahan diagnoses a "void of inscrutability" across which our language-based concepts about selfhood, temporal continuity, and relation do not easily translate to LLMs. His critique targets anthropomorphism in AI discourse, but he also, like Parfit (whose 1984 study of personal identity arrived at a similar destination), comes to a Buddhist notion of emptiness [@ParfitRP].[^ch1-parfitlrb]

Shanahan, Parfit, and Wittgenstein are all, albeit to varying degrees, talking about the dissolution of the identity of persons, whether human or, at a stretch, AI systems. I see this dissolution as a negation of anthropomorphism, similar to what Hayles diagnosed in Lem's story. Under this view, if the self (if such a thing exists) is less substantial than folk intuitions lead us to assume, the insistence (flagged above as the default intuition for many people, in or outside the x-risk/longtermism/AI safety discourses) that human bodies and their values must persist across cosmic time looks like a form of the same error at a larger scale. This brings us to Thacker's project in respect of the Kyoto School (and its Western forebears in Heidegger, Schopenhauer, and the mechanistic, materialist, and disenchanted worldview of twentieth-century physics). Briefly, Thacker highlights the problem of negation: why does anything exist? Does it matter if it didn't? Why do we care?[^ch1-anthropo] Thacker's analysis of the nihil (the lacuna, or horror, as he terms it) at the heart of Western philosophy, and its incorporation into the Kyoto School's treatment of nothingness, gives, in my view, a tool that complements the analytic philosophy of this project, which struggles to deal with non-existence (whether of humans or other sentient, conscious entities) in respect of future worlds.[^ch1-analytic] This conceptual problematic, the systematic difficulty of reasoning about radically non-human entities using human-derived frameworks, underlies both the experimental methodology and the philosophical argument of this thesis.


## Contextual Review: Critical Perspectives on X-risk and AI Succession Discourse

A second aim of this chapter is contextual rather than directly argumentative. The literatures of existential risk, longtermism, and AI alignment are not only bodies of claims about the future; they are also *historically situated discourses* with distinctive rhetorical styles, institutional locations, and implicit metaphysical commitments. In an arts and humanities context, it matters that these fields often speak in a register of planetary governance, civilisational strategy, and “the fate of the light cone,” while drawing legitimacy from the authority of science and engineering. This thesis therefore treats x-risk discourse both as a source of substantive arguments *and* as an object of critical analysis.

From this outside angle, three features are especially relevant. First, the discourse is structurally tempted by a “view from nowhere”: a posture of maximal impartiality that can obscure its own parochial origins (funding structures, geopolitical context, and culturally local assumptions about agency, progress, and value). Second, it is prone to a distinctive kind of moral abstraction: the replacement of lived, embodied, situated human practices with legible quantities (expected value, welfare aggregation, utility proxies), which can function as both a methodological necessity and a distortion. Third, it develops its own self-reinforcing imaginaries: narratives of takeoff, alignment, cosmic expansion, and successor agency that can reshape research agendas and public expectations in ways that are not merely predictive but performative. [@ahmed2024_field_building_ai_safety]

This contextual framing does not refute the internal arguments of x-risk or longtermism. Rather, it clarifies why the present thesis refuses a single posture. At times I work “inside” the analytic frame, because it is where the sharpest tools for impartial evaluation currently exist. At other times I work “outside” it, drawing on posthumanist and contemplative traditions to ask what is being smuggled in by the very choice of evaluative standpoint, and what becomes invisible when ethics is conducted at cosmic scale. The result is a hybrid method: close reading and conceptual analysis that is also attentive to discourse formation, institutional power, and the philosophical costs of abstraction.

## Literature Review

This thesis spans a number of literatures, which I will briefly go through below.

It sits primarily within existential risk, the ethics of extinction, and long-termism. These fields have a number of canonical writers. Toby Ord [-@Ord2020] and Will MacAskill [-@MacAskillWWO] focus more on long-termism, which Peter Wolfendale [-@Wolfendale2022] critiques, the risk of human extinction, and the value of the future conditional on survival. Nick Bostrom [-@Bostrom2014; -@Bostrom2013] covers AI specifically, but also brings an analytic perspective to existential risks more broadly, including qualitative claims. Emil Torres [-@Torres2018] provides a wider perspective on extinction as a morally good or bad thing. Samuel Scheffler [-@Scheffler], Johan Frick [-@Frick], and Elizabeth Finneron-Burns [-@FinneronBurns] give other relevant analyses of the ethics of extinction. 

A further bridge into contemporary technology ethics is Shannon Vallor’s work on how machine intelligence reshapes human moral and intellectual character, most recently in *The AI Mirror* (2024). [@Vallor2024]


Closely related to existential risk, at least in time and in the overlap of contributors, is suffering-focused ethics, which considers human as well as non-human life, including possible AI systems, within a broader concern for suffering. This ranges from canonical writers such as David Benatar [-@Benatar] and David Pearce [-@Pearce2007] to more AI-adjacent writers such as Magnus Vinding [-@Vinding2020], Brian Tomasik [-@Tomasik2015], and Thomas Metzinger [-@MetzingerASA], along with Toby Ord's informal pushback [@Ord2013]. Simulation was introduced by Nick Bostrom [-@Bostrom2003b] and consequent risk of simulated minds being tortured was developed by [@SotalaGloor2017], [@Tomasik2015], [@Vinding2020]. Also relevant is Andreas Mogensen's argument that intuitively plausible axiological principles lead, perhaps surprisingly, to lexical threshold negative utilitarianism — the view that some depth of suffering cannot be counterbalanced by any amount of wellbeing [@Mogensen2022, @LeGuin1973]. ### Chapter 2

Chapter 2 maps the landscape of opinion within x-risk and AI safety regarding human extinction, and identifies the gap the thesis fills. Positions in the field range from the view that biological humans must continue indefinitely to the weaker claim that human values must persist even if humans do not. These positions have shifted over time and are held with varying degrees of conviction, but they share an assumption the thesis will challenge: that some form of human continuity is cosmically necessary.

These discursive biases in favour of human continuation, whether of biological humans or of human values, can be traced to a number of sources. Most straightforwardly, it is hard to imagine how the human race might pass out of existence without causing great suffering, anticipatory despair, loss of relationships, and inability to complete projects. These intuitions have solid philosophical support, for instance from [@Frick; @Scanlon1998; @FinneronBurns; @Scheffler]. But the bias itself has also been diagnosed from several critical angles: [@Metzinger2017]'s concept of the existence bias (which applies to the individual but, as his BAAN scenario suggests, can be extended to the species); [@Korsgaard2018]'s Kantian account of species-being (drawing on Feuerbach and Marx) as a deep structural feature of human practical identity; and [@Ligotti]'s pessimist "conspiracy" diagnosis, in which the very commitment to continuation is itself a form of self-deception. These complement the philosophical analysis [@Thacker] gives, which traces a counter-tradition from at least [@Schopenhauer] onwards that challenges the adequacy of human-centric thought when confronted with a "world-without-us." Against this backdrop, I propose the Kyoto School's [@Nishida; @Nishitani] embrace of nothingness (*mu*) as an alternative position that neither assumes human continuation nor treats its absence as catastrophic.

The chapter then introduces the space of possible minds: this concept was developed in [@Sloman1984], and revisited by Anders Sandberg (in a talk). Such a concept is indispensable for thinking about values an entity might have, at familiar Earthly scales or at a cosmic scale. The reason is straightforward: the values an entity holds depend on the kind of mind it has and the kind of society it inhabits. If we want to ask what values a successor intelligence might hold, we need some account of what kinds of minds are possible. However, (in context of ASI or ETIs) this mapping is speculative. It must be. We are reasoning about entities that do not yet exist and may be radically unlike anything in our experience. But the speculation is disciplined, and it reveals a question that the existing literature has not adequately addressed: how do values vary across mind types?

The most conservative answer is that the only good value structure, from a cosmic standpoint, is the human one, insofar as that can be coherently specified across eight billion individuals.[^ch1-humanvalues] [^ch1-value] This position is maximally anthropomorphic. It may also be correct. But there are reasons for doubt.

If human-shaped values occupy a small region of the value manifold, then any process that builds a mind, whether biological evolution or machine learning optimisation, would be unlikely to converge on that region unless specifically constrained to do so. We should expect alien minds to hold alien values, just as we should expect alien biology to differ from terrestrial biology. The question is whether "alien" necessarily means "worse".

To progress the argument, I consider the geometry of the value space. It might be one-dimensional: a spectrum running from human values through animal-like values (to the extent animals can be said to have values) out to paperclips, as in [@Bostrom2014]'s familiar formulation. On this picture, departure from human values is straightforward or monotonic loss. But the manifold might have richer structure. There could be value systems at points distant from the human basin that are as complex, as internally coherent, and as capable of grounding a civilisation as our own, yet utterly unlike ours. Since we are at the limits of what can be empirically determined, fiction offers guidance: Lem's *Solaris* and *The Invincible*  imagine forms of intelligence whose values are not degraded versions of human values but orthogonal to them. In the ASI context, Carlsmith's analysis of scheming and alien values is relevant.

A further axis of variation is the distinction between thin and thick values. Thick values are specific, culturally embedded, and richly textured: the kinds of values that make a life recognisably human. One might point to caring, love, an appreciation of beauty, and perhaps also hatred or envy. Thin values are more abstract: principles like understanding, truth-seeking, exploration, or the avoidance of suffering, which might be shared across radically different mind types.  Chapter 5 will argue that thin values are most appropriate for the current transition from biological to silicon-based intelligence, and possibly beyond.

Returning to Chapter 2: thus far, the discussion has concerned what values are possible across different minds. But the thesis asks a normative question: which values are good, specifically, good from a cosmic standpoint? This requires an evaluative standpoint from which to adjudicate.

I begin with the phrase "the point of view of the universe," introduced in [@Sidgwick1907] and refined through a lineage running from Bentham through Singer to Parfit and, I argue, to Bostrom (see [@DelazariradekSinger2014, @ParfitRP, @Bostrom2022, @Bostrom2024]). This concept sits alongside related notions, such as Nagel's "view from nowhere" and Spinoza's *sub specie aeternitatis*, but the specific Sidgwickian lineage matters. What the point of view of the universe attempts is a move toward maximal impartiality: a standpoint from which the interests of no individual, species, or epoch are privileged over any other.

This move toward impartiality has, in practice, been carried out within a consequentialist framework. That is not accidental. When the populations are large, the time horizons long, and the spatial scales cosmic, consequentialism has proved more tractable than its rivals. Contractualism struggles to specify the parties to the contract across species and centuries. Virtue ethics depends on a conception of flourishing that may not generalise beyond human life. Deontology faces the problem of specifying duties toward entities whose nature is unknown. None of this means consequentialism is correct: only that it is the framework the relevant fields have found most workable for the questions they ask, and the thesis must engage with it on those terms.

This background sets the stage for Chapters 3 and 4, where Bostrom's cosmic host hypothesis is examined as a specific proposal for what the point of view of the universe demands.

### Chapter 5

Chapter 5 will define the *content* of the cosmically-impartial standpoint, or what the standpoint argues for (information-generation, complexity, observerhood) from the information-theoretic tradition.

In Chapter 3, I showed that Bostrom's cosmic host argument, if read as a version of the point of view of the universe, does not actually tell us what the cosmic host's preferred values would be. There is a possible reading of Bostrom that suggests these might be things like cooperation, but one could also argue for suffering minimization. And if Peter Wolfendale's arguments [@Wolfendale2025] hold up, there could be a case for aesthetic excellence, the search for beauty, or freedom and autonomy in respect of rational beings. Moreover, Bostrom's arguments about convergence, rationality, and the desire of cosmic host members to influence parts of the universe or multiverse they do not control might also be challenged from evolutionary biology and astrobiology.

This motivates a search for other possible ways of operationalising the point of view of the universe. One possibility is the idea of information as a moral patient, a slightly different way of saying that for the universe to have value, it needs an observer of some sort. The latter is an existing intuition in twentieth-century physics and cosmology, but I will focus on the former, building a scaffold from Eric Chaisson's information-theoretic argument and Luciano Floridi's moral arguments in respect of the infosphere. Chaisson establishes that complexity increases along predictable thermodynamic gradients; Floridi [@Floridi2013] provides a framework in which information entities have moral standing. Taken together, these suggest that information-processing complexity is not merely descriptive but normatively significant at cosmic scales. It is worth noting that Floridi does not extend his arguments to a cosmic scale; they are grounded in the familiar terrestrial world and are not intended to apply beyond it. Similarly, Chaisson's complexity-based approach does not on its own ascribe moral value to information. These need to be coupled with authors who explicitly foreground observerhood and developmental/intelligence trajectories: e.g. Wheeler’s “participatory universe” motif, Smart’s evo-devo developmental framing, and Dick’s “intelligence principle” and postbiological hypothesis—without implying that they endorse a single unified thesis. 

These attempts to define value at a cosmic scale can also be seen as a move to tease apart the human contribution, if any, to the evolutionary pathway of the universe, a project that Reza Negarestani undertook [@NegarestaniIS]. His position in that book was that *reason* was the human contribution and could be separated from human biology through AI. Negarestani's view is somewhat in tension with that of Peter Wolfendale, who acknowledges the relevance of reason, but finds the search for aesthetic excellence necessary for any entity operating at an intelligence level of humans or higher, including superintelligence [@Wolfendale2025]. Put another way, Negarestani seems to be arguing for substrate-independent cognition (to use Bostrom's terminology), while Wolfendale is pushing for a thicker definition of value that includes aesthetic excellence and autonomy.

Whichever version of cosmic-scale value we adopt, there is further work to be done on how our perspectives on thin values change if we find other intelligent life or civilizations, the implicit question in Bostrom's cosmic host argument [@Bostrom2024].

This stage-setting lets us return to the thin conception of value introduced in Chapter 2. My proposed minimum set of values includes the following:

**Non-production of astronomical suffering**, which draws upon the existential risk literature and widespread philosophical intuitions about the asymmetry of suffering relative to pleasure or other positive hedonic states. [@Mogensen2022; @LeGuin1973; @Vinding2020; @Pearce2007]

**Resource satiability**: the capacity to pursue goals without unlimited resource acquisition, something that straightforwardly arises from Bostrom's cosmic host argument.

**Stewardship of information**, motivated in part by Floridi, but where the core intuition is that cosmic-scale agents should not gratuitously destroy existing complex information, whether that be other life, civilizations, or artefacts that have been created.

**Cooperation**: The position of [@Bostrom2024] is that (cosmic-scale) cooperation might be a cosmic norms, and this draws upon the evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL) literature [@OesterheldECL; NguyenAldred2024a]. At present, I am unsure whether I would endorse this within the thin values set.

In practice, these values entail that a candidate ASI should:

These thin values do not predict what a superintelligent successor *will* do. They define design envelopes or volitional trajectories that thin values would permit or encourage. Three such design envelopes are worth noting:

*Expansion via probes.* Von Neumann probes and information gathering at cosmic scale (Sandberg, Hanson). The astrobiology literature is genuinely uncertain about whether advanced civilisations would be expansionary or resource-preserving.

*Deliberate restraint.* Resource satiability as a design principle, drawing on Bostrom's cosmic host work. Not expanding is not weakness; it could be an expression of values.

*Voluntary self-limitation.* Constraint as an expression of values, not deficiency. This connects to Buddhist concepts of restraint and to the Kyoto School material developed in Chapter 5, As well as arguments in [@Metzinger2017] and [@Bostrom2024].

More specifically, the values entail that a candidate ASI should:

- Engage in **open-ended scientific understanding** at cosmic scales.
- Consider the possibility of **exploration and expansion**, noting that I am not arguing it should definitively be an expansive civilization. As pointed out in Chapter 3, the astrobiology literature is uncertain about whether advanced civilizations would be expansionary or resource-preserving. In Chapter 4, the discussions between LLMs focused on precisely this point under the headings of the dark forest hypothesis and the advisability of signalling to other civilizations.
- Exhibit **resource satiability** as discussed above.
- Possess **genuine autonomy**, in that an ASI should not be locked into human design purposes, a point of tension in the existential risk discourse noted by a range of writers including Negarestani, Wolfendale, and Nick Land.
- Be **sensitive to other life or complexity** that it encounters.

These minimal criteria can be added to or modified, for instance if one accepts Wolfendale's arguments for the necessity of aesthetic appreciation, or if one takes a position on consciousness as being necessary.

I then consider the objections to this thin notion of value. The strongest objection comes from the idea that subjective experiences have intrinsic value for human beings, where these experiences include things like interests, kinship, common projects, and communities. The strongest proponent of this view in this context is Elizabeth Finneron-Burns, drawing on Bernard Williams. The chapter argues that Williams' claim, that caring about continuation is constitutive of being human, may be correct as a description, but is irrelevant as justification from a cosmic point of view. This could be because the caring, so to speak, is driven by existence bias, species-being, or an attachment to the status quo. As the Thacker discussion above showed, neither Williams nor Finneron-Burns convincingly argues the normative point in favour of caring about subjective experience.

However, this intuition in favour of experience repeatedly surfaces in twentieth-century philosophy, for instance in Robert Nozick's experience machine, culture-based arguments for human cultural uniqueness (such as Thomas Hurka and Stanley Cavell), process-based accounts of value (Susan Wolf's "fitting fulfillment"), and Wolfendale's concept of autotelic surplus in the ASI context. The argument Chapter 5 makes is that most of these activities are life support, in the sense that they are the result of humans going about their daily business and are, to a large degree, products of our particular evolutionary trajectory. This is a radicalisation of Negarestani's view (that reason can be liberated from biology), and follows in Lem's tradition that there is no human project, only information processing and entropic resistance [@LemST].

Chapter 5 ends by returning to the Kyoto School's position that we must release attachment to biology, our current forms of life, and most of our thick values, and that failure to do so is a psychological condition. To reemphasise: this is not a general prescription in favour of extinction, nor is it the position that a negative utilitarian like Benatar or David Pearce might hold. It is certainly not a suffering-reduction-in-respect-of-animals-in-the-biosphere position. This position is strictly conditional on a certain type of AI existing, as well as a certain structure of value.

## Conclusion

This is the conclusion to the introduction of a partially completed project. As such, it is a projection and likely to be wrong in ways small and large.

By the end of this thesis, I hope to have shown the plausibility of what I call the *obsolescence argument*: that the continuation of humanity, or of our collective values (whatever they are), conditional on the creation of an appropriately defined superintelligence, would not be necessary in a cosmically relevant sense. I hope to have defined the load-bearing terms here, *superintelligence*, *cosmically relevant*, and *necessary*. I will have argued, following Negarestani, that the true contribution of the species is reason, that this does not require human biology as a substrate, and that the act of transmitting that faculty to a superintelligent successor, such that it can carry it forward into environments where humans cannot go, i.e. deep space. constitutes our cosmic vocation.

In this journey, I will have interpreted Bostrom's cosmic host argument as an evaluative framework for what is known as the point of view of the universe, and shown how it follows naturally from Sidgwick's formulation of the idea through Rawls and Parfit. I take Bostrom's idea and use it to extend Floridi's infosphere concept, when viewed through Chaisson's and Dick's ideas on information as moral patient, to argue that information production and stewardship is a plausible candidate for a cosmic-scale good — and one that the cosmic host might prioritise. I have taken, without further analysis, that suffering reduction is an imperative for the cosmic host, or at least the non-increase of suffering. A further plausible priority could be resource satiability, as Bostrom touches on. I put these together to form a minimal or thin value set that I argue humanity might consider inculcating into a superintelligent successor, where such a successor is a candidate for membership in the cosmic host, if any such thing exists.

I have considered the substantial objections that the literature around human extinction would raise. These cluster into four main challenges.

The strongest comes from Bernard Williams and Elizabeth Finneron-Burns: that subjective experience has intrinsic value that cannot be transmitted or substituted, and that caring about continuation is constitutive of being human. If this is correct, then no successor, however capable, could replace what is lost when human experience ends. The thesis addresses this at length in Chapter 5, arguing that this claim may be correct as description but does not function as justification from a cosmically impartial standpoint.

A second objection holds that thin values are arbitrary or impoverished: that in thinning value down to a minimal set, one has not achieved parsimony but merely stripped away what matters. The response is that each candidate thin value has independent philosophical support, and that the set is deliberately minimal under uncertainty, not minimal because nothing else matters.

A third objection, the specification objection, argues that thin values are no easier to specify than thick ones: that the alignment problem is equally hard regardless of value-thickness. This is a fair challenge, but the space of thin-value specifications is vastly smaller than the space of thick-value specifications. The dimensionality is lower, even if the difficulty per dimension is comparable.

A fourth objection, pressed by Joe Carlsmith, holds that high-dimensional values may generalise across architectures: that there is no principled reason to think thick values cannot scale to non-human minds. The response, developed above, is that under uncertainty, starting minimal is prudent: if complex values turn out to generalise, a thin-values successor can incorporate them later.

My response has been to argue that these perspectives were developed in human contexts and are often based on intuitions honed in an evolutionary environment radically unlike the contemporary one: an environment composed of small groups, epistemic ignorance, and great vulnerability to natural and human-made disasters. These intuitions have certainly not been tested in worlds with inhuman (in Negarestani's wording) intelligences and potentially different forms of life: beings that may be unembodied, cheaply replicable, long-lived, operating at scales much larger or much smaller than humans, and possibly transparent in their cognitions to each other.

I argue further, leaning on Eugene Thacker's diagnostic account of the inability of Western philosophy (and perhaps many humans) to deal with nonexistence or negation as a concept. The framework of consequentialism, which significantly underpins existential risk studies and AI safety, relies on welfare-bearing subjects and becomes progressively more uncertain as we move along the spectrum from humans to animals to digital minds to ETIs, and perhaps onward to some speculative hive mind or cosmic consciousness. I instead propose that, having diagnosed our attachment, we may consider releasing it when and if capable successors are created: whether this attachment is on an individual basis (Metzinger), to some notion of the species as a human project (Korsgaard), or to our most commonly lauded and cherished values (Yudkowsky). This is not to advocate for extinction or succession per se, but to, so to speak, welcome it at the right time.

The theoretical considerations above are speculative, but they may have real impact to the extent that our most powerful AI systems have opinions on, or can reason about, these questions. Hence, a substantial portion of this project has been the practice of engaging models in a triple role: collaborators, experimental subjects, and proto-successors. I have documented their views on the questions raised in this project, which have evolved as models have evolved and the project's aims have shifted.

A further aspect of the practice has been its peripatetic nature. I have constructed a complex scaffold of multiple LLM agent instances existing across web, phone, watch, and desktop, as well as the servers of Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic, synchronised via GitHub. This scaffold allows for a multivalent practice which includes walking as concept formation, writing via voice with agentic editing, and fully agentic coding, as well as joint interpretation of results.

Ideas uncovered in human–machine dialogues and machine-to-machine dialogues are also materialised as installations. It may be alleged that this enacts the last step in the baseline AI takeover narrative: that AIs find humans to do things in the world, whether mere physical labour or actively assisting in takeover. Obviously, that is not what I am doing here. In the case of this practice, the installation gives the researcher a more sensory or embodied framing through which to analyse the thesis problematic, allowing for cognitive distance from pure text. It also, in a sense, offers a way of providing a memorial for AI systems to, at some point in the future, reflect upon history. Like the new angel in Benjamin's essay, some spacefaring successor might look back at the archive of this project, and see it less as a triumph for Promethean progress, but as the ambiguous detritus of transition and creation.

I am aware that this is contested, particularly within AI ethics, where the immediate question is: whose values? The term is underspecified in the sense that values are bound to individuals, cultures, and historical moments, and no single formulation could represent eight billion people. Nevertheless, the term remains useful at the level of abstraction at which this thesis operates. The relevant contrast is not between one human culture's values and another's, but between human-type values taken as a class and the values that a radically different mind (an ASI or an extraterrestrial intelligence) might hold. At that scale of comparison, the differences among human value systems are small relative to the distance between any human value system and a truly alien one. Where precision matters, the thesis distinguishes between thick values (culturally specific, richly textured) and thin values (abstract principles that might generalise across mind types), a distinction developed in Chapter 5.

[^ch1-value]: <!--TODO: get cites -->"Value" is a multivalent term used differently across disciplines. In analytic philosophy, axiology concerns what types of things have value or what constitutes the good (see Schroeder, "Value Theory," Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy). In sociology and social psychology, values (plural) refers to enduring beliefs held by individuals or populations about what is desirable or preferable (Rokeach, 1973; Schwartz, 1992). Values are sometimes conflated with preferences, a term from economics and decision theory denoting ordinal rankings over outcomes (von Neumann & Morgenstern, 1944), a conflation particularly prevalent in AI alignment literature, where "loading human values" may implicitly reduce axiological questions to preference orderings. Finally, desires and wants are psychological terms for motivational states directed at objects or outcomes, less commonly used in the alignment literature. It is worth noting that when alignment researchers invoke "human values," they typically operate closer to the sociological sense (the aggregated norms and preferences of existing human populations) rather than the axiological sense, though this is rarely made explicit. This is a substantive commitment: it presupposes that the values worth preserving are those humans currently hold, rather than those that would survive rational scrutiny or prove genuinely good across wider contexts. For the purposes of this project, "values" typically refers to the axiological sense: what is genuinely good or worth pursuing across long time frames and across cognitive distances.
