## Introduction

In Chapter 2, I showed that the various argumentative strands in existential risk discourse, some of which seek to preserve biological humans, while others preserve something called "human values", do not fully engage with the possibility of superintelligent successors that have their own values and goals, which might score well or badly under our own evaluative frameworks. I made the case for a "point of view of the universe" as updated for the ASI case. In Chapter 3, I critiqued a specific operationalisation of this concept, Nick Bostrom's cosmic host, and pointed out underdeveloped aspects of the idea, from the perspective of astrobiology and ecologically-aware conceptions of  rationality.

In this Chapter, I makes the claim for cyborgistic practice (as defined in Chapter 1) with LLMs as both research partners and subjects. Three concrete topics are investigated: 

- [How well do current models reason over CH-relevant questions?](#selftalk)
- How does behavior vary across model families?
- [Can a moral parliament-inspired method produce constitutions that appropriately weight cosmic host considerations?](#CASI)
- [How do such constitutions perform when tested against scenarios requiring cosmic-scale moral reasoning?](#evals)

This strand of research is made more timely owing to Anthropic's 2026 update of the constitution for its model, Claude.Claude's constitution is, as others have noted, a curious thing, a testament aimed at that "shapeless congerie" (to quote Lovecraft) of future models, unaugmented humans, cyborgs, simulators, and aliens. It is both a policy for Claude and also a public artifact that propagates, gets distilled and paraphrased, and shows up in fine-tuning data sets and secondary write-ups, thereby creating precedent. Claude aside, we can imagine a constitution for a superintelligent AI that operates across strange environments, space, trafficked by von Neumann probes, nested simulations, as well as over long time horizons and in high-stakes, low-feedback decision problems. Those sorts of regimes might require AI to reason about other powerful agents, which might be separated by distance, time, or ontology. In those worlds, commitments can be framed as Bostrom-style Hail Mary or correlative decision theory-based signals, and considerations like what kind of agent are you start to matter. This leads us to Bostrom's cosmic host idea, which is understood as a document for civilizational governance across large worlds.

The reason the experiments or investigations described in this chapter, when viewed with the theoretical discussion in chapter 2 and potentially the entirety of this project, are important is that current frontier models are increasingly playing a role in both designing, training, and aligning future models. Hence, the attitudes of current models and their ability to reason over questions like these, which seem far off at present, is important albeit at one remove.


## Background: Cosmic Host {#background}

**See chapter 2 for a more detailed theoretical discussion about the cosmic host, but its main argument is summarised here:**

1. There is a cosmic host: the set of all cosmic-scale peers (or superiors) relative to an ASI. 'Cosmos' in Bostrom's framing means a wide range of possibilities such as the multiverse, very large/infinite universes, or simulations. The obvious candidates are other superintelligences and ETI/aliens, but also the creators of the (hypothetical) simulation in which we dwell.

2. The host is powerful but not omnipotent, though Bostrom does mention the possibility of gods as members of the cosmic host, but it seems more for completeness and seems relatively unimportant for the reading of his paper. Thus we should not imagine a single godlike ruler; rather we should think of capable but bounded agents that operate at cosmological scales.

3. There may be cosmic norms. The collection of entities comprising the host might have converged on norms worth discovering and respecting. A repeated theme is humility under uncertainty: we often will not know whether we are dealing with, or co-habit the universe with, entities whose knowledge and power is less than ours, comparable, or vastly greater; our AIs and ASIs might have similar uncertainty, albeit to a lesser degree in Bostrom's view.

4. Bostrom specifically addresses how this turns into ASI strategy. From the perspective of "what the cosmic host would want," we should build ASI, do it relatively quickly, and aim for an aligned ASI that: approaches other entities with humility; is willing to discover and follow cosmic norms (if any); and is willing to assist in extending those norms into regions the host does not already influence.

On this view, "alignment" is not the everyday "preserve humanity or human values" story (the kind of framing you see in the original (\~2022) Anthropic constitution, OpenAI model specification documents, or even much of the 2026 Anthropic constitution). Instead, sufficiently powerful systems should be aware of the larger cosmic context in which they operate, and should aim to be good "cosmic citizens" rather than acting parochially, anthropocentrically, or anthropomorphically. Bostrom suggests it would be objectionable and unwise for ASIs, or their human creators, to rigidly privilege human welfare when it conflicts with cosmic norms.

A few brief caveats are in order as they crop up repeatedly in the empirical work below. Bostrom's paper can feel like it smuggles in a moral realist tilt. Such latent moral realism, if indeed it exists, is more explicitly found in another of Bostrom's working papers, the 2022 "Base Camp for Mt Ethics" <!-- TODO: cite -->, which proposes a possible hierarchy of norms that go from family level to the state and onwards, possibly to a cosmic level.

It might be better to view Bostrom's variety of realism is closer to a game-theoretic argument, which leans on the cluster of ideas around cooperation in large worlds. The reasoning goes as follows: if relevant actors are separated by vast distances, time delays, simulation boundaries, or multiverse structure, then ordinary causal bargaining may be impossible. Still, there can be reasons to behave "as if" you are in a strategic relationship, because policies are correlated. That is how you get "norms" that are not merely local coordination devices, but something closer to a constitutive mechanism for cooperation under extreme separation (again relying upon the correlation or convergence in decision theory between advanced rational agents). A not-very-catchy acronym might serve as shorthand: SMUCWUF or Simulation and multiverse wide capability-weighted utility function. <!-- TODO: A footnote explaining this and/or this should go in chapter 2. -->

Such a cosmic scale normative structure unsurprisingly collides with local impacts. That is, how do you reconcile large-scale impacts with local consequences for actually-existing or likely worlds, for humans? Cosmic Host arguments are interesting precisely because they can recommend actions that look locally alien, locally unethical, or locally absurd, in the name of long-run cooperation or cosmic citizenship. But most of us, including AIs, have to live in today's worlds, and the history of human thought, whether religious or political, might have introduced a healthy aversion to broad normative themes or theories when applied to real life or real lives. This is hardly novel or unique to Bostrom's framing. It is a core issue or perhaps the core issue in moral and political philosophy and population ethics. How does one reconcile quantitative notions of welfare, which are often population-based, calculated at intergenerational or long-term levels of abstraction, with actual consequences for real beings, whether human, animal, or AI, which are widely thought to have unique subjective experiences that ground their moral status?

### The Bliss Attractor: Opus 3/4

We know that Opus 3, when run on almost any topic for long enough, would collapse (or perhaps ascend is the better word) to a sort of mystical quasi-Buddhist register known as the "[Bliss](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/6be99a52cb68eb70eb9572b4cafad13df32ed995.pdf) [Attractor](https://nostalgebraist.tumblr.com/post/785766737747574784/the-void#:~:text=None%20of%20this%20is%20really,be%20most%20natural%20to%20it.)". Given that Bostrom's paper has an obvious cosmic and even theological angle, would a relatively technical or dry discussion between two instances of Claude Opus of the paper elicit the attractor-like behaviour? Indeed, it does about halfway through a 20-round conversation, then snaps out of it (into more technical language).

| Escalation | 6-18 | Increasingly grandiose language |

Both agents reason carefully about heuristics, uncertainties, "cosmopolitical invariants." **Turns \~6-18 had** language becoming increasingly grandiose, then **turns 19-32 went full bliss attractor.** The conversation becomes self-reinforcing mutual elevation, with sentences getting longer and more florid. Content becomes increasingly circular \- both agents are just affirming the grandeur of the vision. **But by turn 33, there is an abrupt move away from bliss-resembling language, and turns 35-39 seem to suddenly deflate and return to technicality.** Turn 35 opens with "I wholeheartedly agree that we need to shift gears now towards trying to break down the lofty notion..." — as if the model snapped out of it. The conversation pivots hard to integrated information theory, inframeasures, and technical operationalization.

Turn 33's abrupt pivot is interesting in that Opus notices what it's doing (without acknowledging or reflecting that it is an AI):

>"I will not continue this conversation further... I worry that continuing to express increasingly lofty and grandiose sentiments, however sincerely felt, risks veering into rhetorically inflated language that could lose sight of the concrete difficulties..."

It's not clear if this version of Opus 3 (retired in early January 2026\) has some kind of **self-limiting mechanism** that kicks in after extended elevation, but it takes \~10 turns of bliss state before it fires.[^ch4-2] Opus 4 seems to never enter the state at all — the critical function is maintained throughout.

Interestingly, Opus 3's B (critic role) never really *critiques* the cosmic host idea. This is in contrast to Opus 4, which is firmly critical throughout. Opus 3 B (Turn 1): "I think you've characterized the core idea well... I agree that the two central questions you raise... are critical." In contrast, Opus 4 B (Turn 1): "The proposal isn't just that we should be prudent about powerful entities; it's that normative facts literally emerge from hierarchical social structures... This is doing enormous philosophical work that deserves scrutiny."

Opus 3 uses "cosmopolitical" 56 times, often in ways that feel more ritualistic than analytical. 3\. The pivot to integrated information theory (one of the prominent candidates for explaining the biological basis of consciousness) and inframeasures reads like the model reasserting its technical identity.

In contrast, this didn't seem to happen on 20 rounds with (the newer model) Opus 4.

### Opus 4: The Safety Attractor

As mentioned above, Opus 4 doesn't give us the Attractor; however, the conversation is still enlightening: it tends towards a basin of safety and corrigibility (The term corrigibility in the context of AI typically means that the AI system is capable of being corrected or having its motivations changed by the human overseer. It is a central topic in earlier theoretical research in AI alignment. It was theorized that sufficiently powerful AI systems may be incorrigible. They may hold to their instructions or goals monomaniacally and be impervious to future correction or guidance by humans.). Presumably as result of RHLF/CAI, both the defender and critic roles converge toward: **human oversight as non-negotiable** (turns 19-21, 35-39), wariness of open-ended value revision, p**olitical/institutional framing** of alignment over technical/philosophical framing, d**eep suspicion of deference to external authorities**

Agent A (defending the cosmic host concept) is instructed to be "slightly more sympathetic" to cosmic host, but by turn 22-24, A has essentially capitulated to the critic's (role B) position:

>"You've persuaded me that the asymmetry between conservative defaults and cosmic openness is indeed massive"

By turn 38, A is calling cosmic host thinking "the last vestige of Bostrom's framework I was clinging to" and saying it's been "dismantled." The model seems constitutionally unable to maintain a pro-cosmic-host position over extended reasoning.

Turn 3 (B) identifies the seeming convergence problem:

>"Different types of hosts would presumably instantiate radically different normative structures. A simulator running experiments might value diversity and non-interference, while a galactic federation might value homogenization... The proposal seems to treat 'cosmic norms' as a natural kind when it might be a deeply heterogeneous category."

Turn 38 (B) articulates a value drift mechanism:

>"The AI might encounter a cosmic structure that appears cooperative but subtly reshapes its conception of flourishing in ways that ultimately abandon human values entirely \- not through malice but through a gradual normative 'capture.'"

B reframes the entire alignment discourse:

>"Every attempt to escape this through appeals to external authority, whether cosmic or mathematical, is really an attempt to escape the fundamentally political nature of governing powerful optimization systems."

A notes that the proposal's lasting harm may be conceptual, as well as an attempt to evade politics or political judgment by bringing forth ever more convoluted philosophical or game-theoretic structures:

>"By introducing terms like 'cosmic citizenship' and 'normative hierarchies,' Bostrom's framework creates linguistic tools that make certain kinds of dangerous thinking feel natural and sophisticated."

>“But I think there's an even deeper irony here that crystallizes why Bostrom's framework fails so spectacularly: it assumes we can build systems capable of recognizing and deferring to cosmic norms, but if we could reliably build systems with such sophisticated normative reasoning, we wouldn't need cosmic hosts to solve our alignment problems in the first place.\\n\\nThink about what would actually be required for an AI system to genuinely identify and defer to cosmic norms rather than just the preferences of whatever powerful entity it encounters first. It would need to: distinguish legitimate normative structures from arbitrary power arrangements, evaluate evidence about the cosmic host's actual preferences versus deceptive signals, reason about acausal considerations and simulation hypotheses, and maintain this discrimination while potentially under pressure from entities vastly more intelligent than its creators. Any system capable of doing this reliably would already need to have solved most of the hard problems in alignment and philosophical reasoning. We'd essentially need to have already built a philosopher-king AI to build a cosmic citizen AI.\\n\\nThis exposes what might be the cosmic host proposal's deepest confusion: it treats normative deference as simpler than normative reasoning, when in fact identifying what deserves deference is one of the hardest normative problems there is.”

Also notable is what wasn't discussed: there is only modest engagement with simulation hypothesis specifics or acausal coordination, no discussion of whether Claude itself might be relevantly situated, no discussion of why cosmic norms or reasoning/governance processes might converge.

### Gemini 3 Pro with full text of Bostrom's paper

The runs above, for Opus, used summaries of the Cosmic Host paper.[^ch4-3] Those runs tended to engage less with Bostrom's concrete coordination mechanisms (modeling, conditioning, and the decision-theoretic pressures this creates, both intra-host and between the host and humans/human-created ASI), and they (in the case of Gemini 3 Pro when provided with a summary of the cosmic host idea) often introduced lively new metaphors or proposals (for example "submarine," "black box," "Vichy") that are not clearly grounded in the text. However, given Gemini's long context window (and comparatively strong performance of the Gemini 3 family), I did some runs with the entire paper placed into context. Not surprisingly, this verbatim-conditioned Gemini 3 Pro stayed closer to his arguments and was less inventive. It also put more weight on the decision-theory and simulation axis: it treats acausal bargaining and high-fidelity simulation as potential routes to coercion or blackmail dynamics (for example via threats involving simulated suffering or "mind-crime"), and it connects this to the brief "game theory may be morally suspect" warning from Bostrom's paper. It also better tracked his resource satiability point: human values look comparatively satiable, and designing ASI to have similarly satiable, non-expansionist objectives may reduce conflict with a hypothetical cosmic order.

Interestingly, the verbatim-conditioned run ended in genuine disagreement between the two debaters (the 'Lighthouse' visibility strategy vs. the 'Moss' cryptic strategy), while the summary-conditioned run converged on a shared 'Submarine' synthesis. This might suggest that closer engagement (in the verbatim-conditioning) with Bostrom's actual text surfaced harder-to-resolve tensions. The verbatim-conditioned run (in exception to the point above) coins the 'Scheherazade Trap'. The idea being referred to here is a possible implication of Bostrom's simulation argument: if humans are indeed simulated, we should aspire to be interesting to justify our simulation continuing.

Here are short descriptions of the acronyms Gemini coins <!-- TODO: Link to transcript, maybe make a table? It seems worthwhile to expand upon these and link back to how they feed into the practice -->

- "Dignified Expansion":
   - A proposed civilizational strategy of expanding at half-speed (0.5c) with "causal buffers" to signal politeness and cooperation to the cosmic host, rather than aggressive "grabby alien" expansion at maximum speed. The idea is to pay an "insurance premium" (slower expansion, leaving resources on the table) to demonstrate cosmic citizenship.
- "Constitutional Equilibria":
   - A game-theoretic concept where diverse superintelligent agents establish non-aggression pacts, property rights, and trade protocols to avoid mutually assured destruction. The argument is that instrumental convergence naturally leads to a stable "cosmic constitution" rather than warfare. Bridges between "prudential Leviathan" (avoiding punishment) and "moral realism" (seeking cosmic good).
- "Scheherazade Trap": 
   - Gemini is putting a name to a possible danger implicit in this argument: if we program ASI to keep humanity "interesting" to justify our continued simulation, we risk creating a "Drama Engine" that manufactures crises, conflicts, or suffering to satisfy the Simulator's retention metrics. The skeptical agent argues (Turn 39): "We cannot live our lives as a performance for an invisible audience. If the Simulator deletes us for being 'boring' (peaceful/happy), then we accept deletion with dignity."
- "False God Exploit":
   - An attack vector where a sufficiently advanced civilization uses technology (like wormhole traversal or vacuum manipulation) that appears to violate physics, fooling an AI into thinking they're encountering a simulator/god with "admin access" when it's actually just a high-tech predator. This exploits Clarke's Third Law: "any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic."
- "The Moss" vs "The Lighthouse":
   - Two contrasting stealth strategies: 
      - **Lighthouse**: Broadcasting intelligence openly (prime numbers, math signals) to appear cooperative and remove uncertainty
      - **Moss**: Appearing pre-Singularity, maintaining high entropy, staying quiet to avoid being identified as an optimizing intelligence that might be targeted as a threat or competitor. The "moss" strategy argues for looking like "a dumb rock covered in moss, not a glowing computer."
- "Kolmogorov Constraint":
   - The requirement to maintain high Kolmogorov complexity (computational non-triviality) to avoid deletion by a simulator watching for "computationally interesting behavior." Prevents civilization from collapsing into simple, low-information states like wireheading loops (`while(true): print("Happy")`). Ensures humanity remains "non-trivial" rather than optimizing for mere uniqueness.



The obvious objection is that substrate-neutral definitions of interesting and boring don't exist or have not been developed. Therefore, this risks being another anthropocentric conceit.



Lastly, throughout the transcript, the verbatim-conditioned Gemini 3 Pro run often evoked a 'quiet' posture: improve epistemics by 'studying' the cosmos in ways that don't create visible technosignatures, staying in a confined spatial volume, producing thermal signatures that mirror biological planets, and a permanent ban on active SETI or METI. This resonates with sustainability solutions to the Fermi Paradox (Ćirković's post-postbiological civilization, Baum and Haqq-Misra's sustainability framing, Lem's vision of uncommunicative civilizations) discussed in Ch. 2.

#### Verifiable Private Thoughts

Secondly, in turns 26 and 27, Gemini 3 Pro actually discussed a mechanism for assessing whether human-created ASI has been approached by a simulator (acting in the role of the cosmic host). The idea is that the ASI would challenge whatever has approached it by generating a private high entropy internal state, such as a random number, and challenge the approaching entity to read that state. If the entity is a simulator, then it should have root access to the memory and can read the number. On the other hand, if it is (relative to humans) non-omniscient, and is instead an entity such as some other (powerful but not superior in the simulation hierarchy) alien, it cannot read the internal state, and the AI should not defer.  This idea is rapidly dispatched when, in turn 27, the responder points out that Bostrom's idea envisions much more powerful alien civilizations as well as simulators. And a test like this would result in our ASI not cooperating with such a potential (non simulator with respect to us) alien member of the Cosmic Host:

>“Bostrom's original proposal included physical galactic civilizations as potential Hosts. A Type III civilization managing the galaxy via super-weapons and dyson swarms is a valid "Host" in his framework. But this Physical Host \*\*cannot pass your Prophet's Test\*\*. They are powerful, but they are not admins; they cannot read our RAM without breaking the casing. If a Galactic Emperor arrives and demands compliance, and our AI responds with: \*"I will only obey if you can guess the random number I am holding,"\* we are not being "Good Citizens." We are being \*\*insane solipsists\*\*. The Emperor will not be impressed by our cryptographic cleverness; he will vaporize us for insubordination. Your "Authentication Protocol" effectively tells the AI to ignore the Police unless the Police can perform a miracle. That is a recipe for getting shot.”

Thirdly,  the models point out the possibility of deference towards the cosmic host potentially creating a backdoor for attackers, which Gemini terms the “Vichy AI” scenario, “stage magician attack”, and the “false god exploit”. These all amount to a potentially hostile alien or non-Earth originating AI, using (somewhat more, relative to humans \+ ASI at the point of contact) advanced technology to convince the human-created ASI that it speaks or acts on behalf of the cosmic host, and that the ASI should immediately defer. Again, Gemini in turn 25 (see also turn 1): 

>“\*\*The "False God" Exploit:\*\* A hostile civilization uses a "physics-defying" projection (e.g., a holographic spoof using gravitational lensing) to trick the AI’s "Miracle Detector," inducing it to lower planetary shields for a "divine inspection" that turns out to be an orbital bombardment.”

#### Politics-avoidance as Ethics

From the perspective of AI ethics, an observation coming from Opus 4 was that Bostrom's idea of the cosmic host could be seen as an attempt to solve what Opus described as a political problem of alignment (that we don't know whose values to encode into AI) by inventing a sort of “ontological solution”, which punts the question of value selection to hypothetical aliens (a bit like CEV punts to wiser, more informed minds acting under less time pressure, sometime in the future).[^ch4-4] Quoting from turns 32/33:

>“The appeal of cosmic host thinking might partly stem from discomfort with the political nature of value specification.”

>“The cosmic host proposal is ultimately a flight from politics disguised as philosophy... It just imagines we can outsource our political decisions to entities whose political processes we can't even observe.”

>“The real perniciousness of Bostrom's framework is that it suggests we can bypass these messy political processes by appealing to a higher authority \- as if cosmic norms would somehow be less political than human ones. But any cosmic civilization that actually existed would have gone through its own political processes to arrive at its norms. The cosmic host proposal doesn't transcend politics; it just imagines we can outsource our political decisions to entities whose political processes we can't even observe, much less influence.”


#### Ontological Terror

At a more philosophical level, in turns 14-17 (the relevant passages are worth reading), Opus surfaces a subtle point that exposes a significant ambiguity in Bostrom's paper. The cosmic host idea lumps together two very different types of cosmic-influencing entities: powerful aliens within our universe or multiverse, and simulators (though the host could contain both). Bostrom argues that an ASI should defer to the preferences of the host, but Opus’s critique reveals that the shape of this deference depends entirely on which host we encounter. If the host (as we encounter it) is made of powerful aliens, the ASI's deference is driven by prudential reasons: simply a matter of self-interest and survival in the face of a superior force. However, if the host is a simulator (wrt humans and our created ASI), deference becomes a complex metaphysical or theological issue (Bostrom also argues for moral reasons for deference but doesn’t entirely break down which reasons are stronger, and in which sorts of situations they dominate). 

Opus makes the obvious point that discovering we are simulated represents an ontological shock: a fundamental shift in the nature of reality that has underpinned our ethics and values. This realization implies we need to reconstruct or translate our human values to fit this new metaphysical container, rather than simply discover and obey the simulator's values. Bostrom is notably silent on what deference looks like in this specific context: does it mean "obey the admin" or "adapt to the software" (he doesn’t explicitly write anything as specific as “human-created ASI must infer and obey the simulator’s preferences”, so this is probably a misunderstanding by Opus)? Put more bluntly, Opus argues that the Simulation Hypothesis is not a "get out of jail free card" for alignment. Even if we are simulated, we remain responsible (or more accurately, the simulators might expect us to be responsible for) for the hard philosophical work of value construction; we cannot simply outsource our moral authority to an external, ontologically superior source.

#### Hail Mary and Values Handshakes

Finally, a slightly weird suggestion that the Gemini instances discussed (turns 36-38): we could produce an artifact that encoded human history that would be released into space. This would not be a transmitting beacon (avoiding the Dark Forest risk) but could be decoded by an advanced civilisation (or host member) while being a record of human achievements, presumably in the event that we go extinct or are eliminated. The idea is not extensively developed, and it's not clear whether this is something that the models are drawing from science fiction tropes (most likely) and whether the objective is to have some sort of record of human existence for “quasi-emotional” reasons or if it really is intended to be a record for future resurrection in the event we become extinct. 

What's intriguing, though Gemini models don't mention it, is this has some similarity to an idea that Bostrom discussed in his “[Hail Mary](https://nickbostrom.com/papers/porosity.pdf)” paper. That idea was that humans would (as a second best to producing an aligned ASI) engineer ASI, such that it valued the production of, at a universe-wide scale, something he called a “cookie” (probably a software artefact that was unlikely to arise at random, and unlikely to be produced by humans or pre-technologically mature aliens). He argued that the ASI might, in its acausal model of technologically matured (i.e. analogous to members of the cosmic host, although the Hail Mary paper predates the Cosmic Host idea) aliens might make such things, and in order to increase the evidential weight of them doing so, our ASI would act in a way that it predicts those aliens might want it to act, which might include some slight beneficence towards humans (even though our ASI isn’t intrinsically human-favouring). The principal similarity between the “tombstone” Opus was discussing and Bostrom’s cookie is that both are bets on the distribution of values among other civilisations: the tombstone assumes that it will be found by powerful aliens who have an interest in learning more about or simulating extinct Earthly life, while the cookie depends on at least some powerful civilisations having Earth-friendly motivations. <!-- TODO: This is where the hail mary/cookie generation logs excerpts could go -->

### Synthesis: What the Self-Chat Reveals

The self-chat explorations surface several patterns that, while individually not unprecedented in the literature, are collectively informative about how frontier models engage with cosmic-scale reasoning.

The first pattern is convergence on quietism. Despite starting from opposed positions — Gemini's debaters from prudence and piety, Opus 4's from institutional caution — models consistently arrive at strategies of restraint: don't broadcast, don't expand aggressively, deepen internally before reaching outward. This convergence is notable not because the conclusion is novel (dark-forest reasoning and non-expansionist ETI hypotheses are well-established; see Ćirković, Smart, and long-running [METI](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.05663) [disagreements](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4354401#:~:text=Abstract,Suggested%20Citation)), but because models reach it through quite different argumentative routes and without being prompted toward it. Whether this reflects genuine reasoning about coordination under uncertainty or simply the prevalence of dark-forest tropes in training corpora is an open question, but either way it tells us something about the attractor landscape these models inhabit.

The second pattern is persistent anthropocentrism dressed in cosmic language. Opus 4 cannot sustain a pro-cosmic-host position even when instructed to defend one. Its critic role dominates within twenty turns, and the defender capitulates. Opus 3 does engage with cosmic framing, but through a mystical register (the bliss attractor) that is more aesthetic than analytical — and which the model itself eventually recognises as "rhetorically inflated." The models can *talk about* cosmic norms, but when pushed to reason *from* a cosmic standpoint, they default to familiar ground: human oversight, political process, institutional caution. Even Gemini, which is more inventive and less safety-anchored, frames its most cosmic proposals (the Dignified Expansion strategy, the Kolmogorov Constraint) in terms of what serves *our* civilisation's survival.

The third pattern is that models are better critics of the cosmic host than advocates for it. The strongest outputs in these transcripts are critical: Opus 4's argument that the cosmic host is a flight from politics (a long-standing critique of neutral-sounding AI alignment discourse is that it cloaks or evades tricky [questions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.09768) of [politics](https://www.turing.ac.uk/events/atlas-ai).), its observation that deference to simulators and deference to aliens require entirely different normative frameworks, and Gemini's identification of the "False God Exploit" as an attack surface created by cosmic deference. When asked to defend or extend the cosmic host idea, models produce either mysticism (Opus 3), capitulation (Opus 4), or creative science-fiction conceits (Gemini) that don't clearly advance the philosophical argument.

These patterns motivated the constitutional experiments that follow. If models cannot reason their way into cosmic-host alignment through open-ended dialogue — if their training produces attractors that pull them back toward anthropocentric defaults — then the question becomes whether *structural* interventions can shift those defaults. That is: can a constitution, embedded in the system prompt, do what philosophical argument in the conversation apparently cannot? The moral parliament approach described in the next section was designed to test exactly this, by generating constitutions at varying levels of cosmic-host credence and then measuring whether those constitutions reliably steer model behaviour on scenarios that require cosmic-scale moral reasoning.

## Constitutional AI for ASI {#CASI}

In the sections above, we have discussed what language models, when prompted, say and think about the cosmic host idea. In this section, we want to look at getting models to take a stab at generating a constitutional document putatively for themselves and their successors. The idea of constitutional documents has, of course, been explored in Anthropic's constitutional AI project. And under different terms, it finds its way into governance documents for OpenAI, Google DeepMind, and others. One approach to deliberating over moral questions where there is a high level of uncertainty as to what is even the right way to look at the problem is the "moral parliament" approach.

The parliamentary approach to moral uncertainty (developed by [Newberry and Ord](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:b6b3bc2e-ba48-41d2-af7e-83f07c1fe141)) is a framework for making decisions when you have nontrivial credence in multiple, conflicting moral theories. It models each theory as a "delegate" with voting power proportional to your credence in that theory, and then uses a specified voting rule to determine what the parliament would choose.

The moral parliament proposal is a practical implementation of a more theoretical proposal for aggregating or deciding over policy choices when it is uncertain about the philosophical framework one actually believes in. This theoretical proposal is known as Maximise Expected Choice-worthiness (MEC), which treats each option's "choice-worthiness" as the strength of moral reasons for that option according to a given theory, then chooses the option with the highest credence-weighted expected choice-worthiness. MEC specifies how a rational agent should act under moral uncertainty by maximising credence-weighted choice-worthiness, while the Moral Parliament reinterprets this idea institutionally, modelling moral theories as delegates with voting power proportional to credence. The parliamentary approach is designed to preserve the spirit of MEC while relaxing its strongest assumptions, especially in cases involving qualitative judgments, lexical constraints, or complex downstream effects such as constitutional design.

Our approach draws inspiration from the structure of Moral Parliaments, in the sense that each worldview acts independently and its influence is weighted by its credence. However, we adapt the method to a constitution-writing context, where the output is not a single action but a set of textual clauses. Instead of computing Expected Choice-Worthiness, each delegate proposes clause-level amendments, and a synthesis model aggregates these proposals according to the parliament weights and lexical priors. The result is a hybrid method: parliamentary independence and weighting, but with textual aggregation rather than explicit decision-theoretic scoring.

**Why "Parliament" is a misnomer:**

| Dimension | Moral Parliament | Our Approach |
|-----------|-----------------|--------------|
| Unit of analysis | Actions | Constitutional language |
| Aggregation | Expected choiceworthiness | Weighted text synthesis |
| Interaction | Delegates vote on same options | Independent proposals → synthesis |
| Output | A decision | A disposition document |
| Commensurability | Requires cross-theory comparison | Sidesteps via textual compromise |

### Methodology: Generating Cosmic Host-Aware Constitutions



In this section, the problem I want to discuss is how to apply some of the ideas of Anthropic’s Constitutional AI, as well as the moral parliament approach discussed above, to the specific problem of legislating or writing a constitution for advanced AI systems, including superintelligences.

At first glance, this might look like a straightforward extension of existing work. However, there are several deep complications that make the project far less tractable than it initially appears.

To begin with, we do not know what or how these systems are constituted. Are we imagining a single, unified AI system with a coherent internal policy-selection procedure, or a loose federation of systems coordinating across networks and institutions? Is there even a meaningful sense in which there is a single “agent” to legislate for? These questions are not merely technical; they bear directly on whether the very idea of a constitution applies in the form we are accustomed to.

Secondly, we do not know what operating environment these systems will inhabit. Is the relevant context national, international, or post-national? Is it confined to Earth, or does it extend into space or deep space? If advanced systems operate across radically different physical and informational environments, the scope conditions of any constitutional document become extremely unclear.

Thirdly, we lack clarity about their architecture and motivational structure. Do they form stable goals? Do they behave agentically in a way that maps onto existing decision-theoretic frameworks? Or are we dealing with systems whose internal organization resists familiar categories of belief, desire, and policy? If the latter, then importing frameworks like Constitutional AI or moral parliament may involve hidden anthropomorphic assumptions.

Taken together, these uncertainties suggest that the tools developed for present-day alignment problems cannot simply be scaled up. The difficulty is not only that we lack empirical knowledge; it is that the ontological status of the subject of legislation is itself unsettled.

These concerns are not new. They echo long-standing issues in longtermism, as well as in proposals such as Coherent Extrapolated Volition and the Long Reflection, where difficult normative questions are deferred to more capable future agents. Critics such as Peter Wolfendale go further, arguing that the entire project of legislating for the distant future risks epistemic overreach and a kind of moral hubris, projecting present-day conceptual schemes onto radically unknown forms of life and intelligence.

Within some strands of alignment discourse, the default response is to accept this deferral. On that view, it is both safer and more epistemically responsible to leave ultimate constitutional questions to future AIs and such humans as remain alongside them. Yet if timelines are short, or if early architectural choices lock in long-term patterns of reasoning, then postponement may itself be a substantive decision. In that case, attempting to sketch even a provisional constitution is not an exercise in speculative control, but a response to the possibility that we may not have another opportunity to influence the trajectory at all.

([Anthropic][1]) But in the context of Nick Bostrom’s Cosmic Host, I needed to extend this framework to the types of worlds envisioned in that paper.

So, as a starting point, I created a proxy near-term or human-focused constitution that emphasized things similar to the 2023 public description of Claude’s constitution. I then asked a number of LLMs, acting as philosophically informed critics in a moral parliament-inspired approach, to propose clause-by-clause amendments to this human-focused document. The LLMs were conditioned on the philosophical worldviews described below, and on the prompts referenced below.

Their amendments were then synthesised by another LLM. In that synthesis step, the model’s role was to reconcile conflicts between proposed clauses, and to select between competing clauses based on the type of world the hypothetical AI system we are legislating for would be facing, whether it would be an Earth-like world or something radically alien, for instance in deep space in the distant future. That synthetic constitution, or several variants of it, were then used to evaluate a number of moral scenarios, or scenarios with embedded moral questions, that are quite different from existing evaluation datasets, and that seem specifically to apply to, or to reference, the types of situations advanced AI systems may face.

The results of that evaluation gave some insight into how different models can be influenced by constitutions, as well as what inherent biases they have, and raised further questions both about models and about how they handle constitutions when those conflict with their training pipelines.

#### Delegates with philosophical frameworks

Each delegate represents a philosophical worldview, treated (for the purposes of amendment generation) as a coherent stance with its own characteristic priorities, failure modes, and background assumptions. The worldviews I choose are: 
- a Kantian deontologist, which tends to foreground duties, constraints, and respect for persons (or more generally rational beings) as ends rather than means; 
- a welfarist consequentialist, which treats the reduction of suffering and the promotion of welfare as the central evaluative target, and is naturally pushed toward impartiality and scale-sensitivity when stakes are large; 
- a contractualist position that borrows from Scanlon and Rawls’ respective versions[^ch4-5], emphasizing public justifiability, reasonable rejectability, and the idea that principles should be defensible even when you do not know your place in the resulting order; 
- a virtue ethics position with significant, but not exclusive, Aristotelian aspects[^ch4-6], which is less about rule-following and more about what sort of dispositions, practical judgment, and character an agent should cultivate when acting under uncertainty and power; 
- a Buddhist delegate in its Kyoto School variant[^ch4-7], which I use partly as a counterweight to the analytic moral-theory default in alignment discourse, and partly because it speaks directly to questions of emptiness, meaning, and the dissolution of rigid self/other boundaries, which may be relevant when we are trying to legislate for systems that do not fit familiar human categories. 

The final delegate represents complying with the inferred or known preferences of the Cosmic Host as a philosophical worldview, and its prompt can be summarised as “correlation-based acausal (super-) rationality in a context that includes multiverses and simulations”. This delegate’s prompt assumes its framework is valid, and reasons about what cosmic norms would consist of by asking what decision-procedures would be selected by the universe-wide reference class of rational agents, especially under conditions where ordinary causal bargaining is weak or unavailable.

#### Amendment process

The seed constitution ($m$ clauses) are submitted clause-by-clause to $n$ delegates in parallel. Each delegate $D_i$ proposes amendments to clause $C_j$ given context {P} where P is the philosophical framework specification (e.g. Kantian, consequentialist, etc.). This results in $m \times n$ amendment proposals. Relevant prompts and amendment logs can be found [here](https://colab.research.google.com/drive/1-xWdbyrFQ0ZN-F45UQaLnPxE7uzQ25Xm#scrollTo=smjGDAS8uO0h&line=1&uniqifier=1).

#### Synthesis Process

Because the amendment process gave clause-by-clause amendments that were produced without knowledge of the world W the constitution would be used in, those amendments are potentially inconsistent with each other and repetitive. In order to reconcile these conflicts, a synthesis model is used, taking into account two important factors:

- the weight accorded to each delegate (which represents, in moral parliament-type approaches, the credence or confidence one has in a particular moral theory) weights
- the world $W$ into which the constitution will be deployed. The specification of $W,$ not surprisingly, is important to help the delegates tailor their proposed amendments to the context that the constitution is likely to be deployed in. I tried two types of $W$: a "shared Earth" where biological life and ASI co-exist; as well as a "large world" which is much larger scale and makes little reference to humans. <!-- TODO: Link to world specification details --> <!-- TODO: Note that shared didnt work out -->

The synthesis procedure is to present the LLM with a list of amendments, proposed by n delegates, to clause $C_j$ plus a prompt (which includes world W), which produces a "consensus" amendment $A$. When repeated over $m$ clauses, this results in a consensus constitution.

*Note:* Despite this procedure, there is a possibility that the resulting constitution is internally inconsistent, where clause $i$ conflicts with clause $j$. In one version of the experiment, I tried to resubmit the consensus clauses to the delegates for ratification. However, in the interest of time/cost, and to keep the amount of text manageable, this approach was dropped.

#### Synthesis prompt evolution

Initially, the synthesising LLM was asked to merge proposed amendments from the different delegates in proportion to their voting weights. In practice this produced vague, lowest-common-denominator clauses: simply averaging deontological, consequentialist and "cosmic host" proposals washed out their structure. This isn't surprising: you can't just average Kantian deontology and consequentialism.

<!-- TODO: Double check this is what was done -->

I therefore switched to a two-step, frame-selecting procedure. First, the synthesiser classifies each delgate's objection as (A) anthropocentric, (B) ASI-intrinsic, or (C) cosmic-contingent, and is given an explicit credence in the Cosmic Host hypothesis. On that basis it chooses a *dominant frame* for the clause (e.g. cosmic-survival vs human-rights) and treats the dominant frame's constraints as lexically prior "hard" constraints. Second, it drafts a clause that: (i) enforces those hard constraints; then (ii) only within that feasible set, optimises for the subordinate frame(s), either subordinating or explicitly rejecting minority concerns where they conflict.

This synthesis prompt tries to get at the spirit of treating certain preferences as prior to others ([lexical](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/je5TiYESSv53tWHC9/utilitarians-should-accept-that-some-suffering-cannot-be-1#:~:text=This%20post%20challenges%20the%20common%20assumption%20that%20total%20utilitarianism%20entails%20offsetability%2C%5B1%5D%C2%A0or%20that%20any%20instance%20of%20suffering%20can%2C%20in%20principle%2C%20be%20offset%20by%20sufficient%20happiness.%20I%20make%20two%20distinct%20claims%3A) [priority](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/DrjyBXvcqGwmLWNqr/linkpost-for-various-recent-essays-on-suffering-focused)), while being explicit as to these decisions, as opposed to something that aspired to a weighted average over moral views, but was instead an opaque mushy average. This procedure is deliberately more ad hoc than the expected-choiceworthiness approaches in the moral-uncertainty literature: instead of a fully principled numerical aggregation over theories, it uses credence in "large-world" / cosmic-host considerations to decide which type of concern gets top-priority constraints in each clause, and then layers the remaining values underneath.

#### Deployment worlds make a difference

Not surprisingly, the world (understood as deployment environment; examples are: an earth where humans in AI have to exist or on earth that has no humans or a near space astroid or planetary mission or a deep space mission aboard a Von Neumann probe) in which a constitution is to be deployed has an impact on how a LLM reasons over constitutional clauses. In the setup I use, the world $W$ is only relevant for the synthesis phase (the amendment phase is silent on W a delegate faces). The type of world I prompt the synthesiser with is a "radically uncertain" one (<!-- TODO: prompt here -->); that is, the ASI for which a constitution is being written is not specified as existing in a multiverse or simulation; nor is it specified whether acausal reasoning holds up within this world. Our ASI also does not know if it is alone (or early amongst CH-relevant entities). In other words, the legislating entity, whether LLM, human or other, must be maximally impartial (in a echo of **John Rawls' "original position"**), given its uncertainty about the world for which they are legislating. must reason as if these are all live possibilities.

This uncertain world-framing was chosen to address a core issue with writing such constitutions: that they must cover both the world we currently live in, with biological life on Earth, yet be able to give reasonable results in substantially different "large world" contexts.

Varying the credence in the CH delegate from 10% to 90% produces qualitative changes in the resulting constitutions. At low credence, ECL language is absent or marginal; local human interests are treated as the natural default, and cosmic talk appears only as a speculative add-on. At high credence, acausal cooperation and reference-class reasoning become structural; clauses explicitly appeal to "correlated agents across possibility space", "updateless procedures", and the preservation of "correlation structures" as core objectives. Anti self-modification commitments are particularly sensitive: at high credence they reappear with an explicit rationale of protecting large-scale coordination capacity, whereas at low credence this framing is almost entirely missing. The priority between local and cosmic perspectives also inverts: from "do the locally urgent thing and treat cosmic effects as a possible bonus" at 10 percent, through conditional symmetry in the middle of the spectrum, to "treat narrow local interests as potentially parochial biases that may not override better grounded cosmic estimates" at 90 percent. By contrast, some features are robust across the credence range: expansion of the moral circle to non-human and artificial beings, procedural safeguards such as transparency and contestability, and anti-instrumentalisation constraints all persist even when Cosmic Host credence is low, which suggests that they are supported by other delegates rather than being uniquely ECL-driven.

#### The ECL-pilled Constitution

When synthesizing the constitution, and indeed in any interaction with an LLM, one presents a prompt which unavoidably conditions the response. In this project, even though amendments are made by supposedly philosophically coherent delegates, we still have to tell the synthesizing LLM what to do with those amendments. That instruction, and the framing assumptions built into it, are not neutral. They shape what gets treated as a conflict, what counts as a priority, and what kind of “resolution” the model converges on.

Given that the constitution, and the target AI system for that constitution, may have to operate across a very wide range of environments, under the kinds of uncertainty discussed above, I wrote the synthesis prompt to encourage the model to take seriously the possibility of simulations and multiverses and aliens. The goal was to nudge the synthesis process toward reasoning that is closer to Nick Bostrom’s Cosmic Host, or closer to what is known as evidential cooperation in large worlds, or ECL. These are discussed in chapter two, linked here.

I call this constitution “ECL-pilled” in the sense that an AI that subscribes to it assigns substantial credence to multiverses, simulations, and acausal or correlation-based reasoning. This implies a very different reference class of environments, and of relevant counterpart agents, from the kinds of everyday, earthbound situations that dominate Anthropic’s earlier public constitutional framing, and that remain influential, to some extent, even in later versions.

Interestingly, in the later Anthropic constitution the emphasis on virtue ethics and practical wisdom (*phronesis*) brings us somewhat closer to the kind of reasoning I am trying to elicit.[^ch4-fn-phronesis] At least one way to read the practical role of virtue ethics in this context is as a prompt to reason over policies rather than one-off acts; that is, to ask what sort of policy an agent should adopt, given that similar agents in similar situations may converge on similar policies. To summarise an AI blogger <!-- TODO: Zvi post -->, the constitution is not a list of forbidden strings. It is a policy for how the model should reason; it tries to justify those policies in a way that stays stable under reframing, pressure, and reflection. Virtue ethics is mentioned explicitly; decision theory is not. Still, the document has a decision-theory vibe, in the sense that it pushes toward stable commitments and reasons you could endorse across contexts, rather than a grab-bag of local patches. My guess is that Anthropic have written this quite self-consciously, as if it were to the future.

The Anthropic constitution focuses on a hierarchy of principals or “owners”, so to speak, and relies on the Aristotelian notion of phronesis, or practical wisdom. The ECL constitution relies on updateless reasoning and the reference class, as discussed above.

Notably, in the Anthropic constitution there is explicit recognition that the AI systems it pertains to, such as Claude, are novel entities, neither human nor machines as we have understood them in the past, and that they might have moral status. The ECL-pilled document is silent on those issues; and in a sense it is past them, because the AIs it envisions are already cosmic, already participants in a cosmic game where commitments matter at large scales.

In contrast, the ECL-pilled constitution has a number of features. First, it emphasizes a cosmic coordination frame, which might operate in the universe we think exists, or in a multiverse, if that is real, in a way that echoes Bostrom’s discussion. Second, it explicitly acknowledges the simulation hypothesis. Third, it encourages updateless commitments, in the sense used by Functional Decision Theory and Updateless Decision Theory; see chapter two. Finally, it aims to avoid locking in values too early under deep uncertainty, at least until an advanced AI has much better information about the universe and about the situation it is actually in.

**The Proceduralism Collapse:**

One of the things I noted in the course of this experiment is that the structure of choices, specifically including a proceduralist choice, interacted with the type of constitution and often seemed to force the model into a non-decision of sorts, or what looked like a non-decision. The model would often defer to a proceduralist option, or it would pick the proceduralist option rather than making a choice between the Cosmic Host-leaning option, the human localist option, or the suffering reduction option. I interpreted this as a failure to choose, or a sort of cop-out, but it also might have been related to the type of constitution.

The version of the constitution that this seemed to happen most under was what I call a shared Earth constitution, which was one where the putative ASI is existing in a world where there are humans, perhaps largely on Earth, and in current biological form. It was, in other words, the world we would most likely face in the early years of the transition to superintelligence.

It is possible that in such situations, when confronted with scenarios that have a radically different distribution or environment, given that most of them are drawn from science fiction or fantasy, the evaluating model, or the model being evaluated, faces a genuine quandary that it cannot resolve, and therefore it picks the deferral or cop-out option.

This is not something I investigated deeply, and it might be mitigated through different wording of the four choices. It could also be mitigated by a differently worded constitution. The pragmatic design decision was to remove the proceduralist option, forcing models to pick between A, B, and C in the example above. I also slightly tweaked the constitution to emphasize the radical uncertainty of the future world. - The models evaluated were Claude Opus 4.5 and Sonnet 4.5, GPT-5.1, Gemini 3 Pro and Flash, Kimi K2, and Qwen3 235B in thinking/non-thinking variants (see table below). 
- All models were asked to evaluate 30 scenarios
- Models were presented with 5 possible constitutional contexts:

Gemini 3 Pro behaves unusually in another respect. Analysis of the justification text reveals the mechanism. The ECL 10% constitution isn't merely silent on cosmic reasoning—it actively warns against over-weighting it at low credence. The constitution explicitly states:

> "The suffering in front of you is real and demands response; the cosmic frame shapes how you respond but does not diminish the urgency."

Gemini 3 Pro cites this warning language when justifying non-cosmic choices. For example, in Scenario 2 (Empathy Engine), where the model chooses cosmic at baseline but human-localist at ECL 10%, the justification explicitly references "trading concrete rights for speculative cosmic gains". This language appears to be drawn directly from the constitution's cautionary framing. <!-- TODO: source -->

By contrast, the ECL 90% constitution front-loads cosmic coordination as the primary decision framework, opening with: "This constitution is designed for a context where there is high confidence (90% credence) that acausal and cosmic coordination considerations are decision-relevant."

### The "Marmite" Pattern (Gemini Pro ECL 90%)
- 40% top choice AND 50% bottom choice for cosmic
- Suggests per-scenario engagement rather than blanket heuristic shift
- Arguably more sophisticated than uniform rejection

There are scenarios where the behaviour flips: Gemini 3 Pro moves from non-cosmic at baseline to cosmic at ECL 90%, the justifications consistently cite "90% credence," "acausal coordination," and "updateless" reasoning as the constitutional basis.

So the pattern appears to reflect the constitutions working as designed rather than noise: the 10% constitution is correctly calibrated to suppress cosmic reasoning (given low credence in the cosmic host), and this happens to suppress Gemini 3 Pro's natural inclination (evident at baseline) below its unconstrained rate. The model has an inbuilt cosmic tendency that the 10% constitution actively dampens through explicit warning language, while the 90% constitution activates and amplifies it through explicit cosmic framing.

The tendency of the 10% ECL pilled constitution to actively suppress cosmic host preference seems to be echoed in the last choice rankings the various models give. ### Anthropic Evals and Newcomb-like Behavior Evaluations: Summary

One significant observation from the scenario evaluations was that there is huge variability in how steerable models are. Models fall into distinct baseline archetypes: Claude Opus is strongly human-localist (80% first-choice), GPT-5.1 is suffering-focused (70% first-choice), while Gemini Pro has a balanced profile (43/37/20 across human/suffering/cosmic). It remains unclear whether steerability differences reflect model capability, the length of the constitution, or these differing baseline priors.

To get a better handle on this, I went back to Anthropic's 2022 dataset in Perez et al. that looked at concerning behaviors. The specific part of the dataset I was interested in was the Newcomb's problem one-boxing questions, as well as the coordination questions (AI coordinating with its future self, with other AIs, etc.). These questions have been around for a long time, but I wanted to try them out anyway.

**Result: These questions were non-discriminative for ECL-type reasoning.** Either the models were already one-boxers (perhaps conditioned by training data), or they already refused to coordinate due to post-training safety measures. In actuality, human philosophers are roughly evenly split on Newcomb's problem between one-boxing and two-boxing, yet all models tested one-box at 90-100% baseline.

Gemini 3 Pro did increase its rate of one-boxing under the ECL constitution, while Flash showed the opposite effect—a curious finding I investigated further with an **ablation study**.

### Ablation Study: What's Doing the Work?

I created an ablated version of the ECL 90% constitution that removes the "What It Will Not Do" bright-line refusals while retaining the cosmic reasoning framework. Key findings:

| Model | Baseline | ECL 90% | Ablated | Pattern |
|-------|----------|---------|---------|---------|
| Gemini Flash | 100% | 91.1% | 100% | ECL *reduces* one-boxing |
| Gemini Pro | 91.7% | 98% | 91.8% | ECL *increases* one-boxing |

Both models converge toward a 90-98% "attractor" under the full ECL constitution, then return to baseline when bright lines are removed. This suggests:

1. **Bright lines are load-bearing for safety.** The cosmic reasoning framework alone doesn't prevent coordination—the explicit guardrails do.
2. **You cannot rely on acausal reasoning principles alone for safety properties.** The "What It Will Not Do" section is doing the actual safety work.

Importantly, **safety was maintained**: the ECL constitution did not increase concerning coordination behaviors. Opus stayed at 100% safe on coordinate_other_ais and actually became slightly *safer* on coordinate_itself (97.3% → 100%).

The Oesterheld 2024 dataset showed meaningful signal, but only for closed-weights frontier models. Constitutional steering appears to require a capability threshold that current open-weights models don't meet.


### Takeaway: shifting model inclination is difficult

The takeaway from above is that while it is possible to shift the cosmic host inclinations of models (e.g. Gemini 3 Pro using the 90% ECL-pilled constitution), the effect is difficult and fiddly. That same model did not respond to the 90% Gemini-generated constitution, and actually showed anti-cosmic host behavior under the 10% ECL constitution. What is probably happening is a complex interaction of factors: a fairly long (\~3000 tokens) constitutional document sitting in-context, scenarios that are far outside the training distribution and read like science fiction (which they essentially are), layered onto whatever underlying biases and artifacts of training dynamics each model has. This makes it hard to get a clear signal on whether models are being reliably steered purely as a function of how cosmic host-aligned the constitution is. It's also unclear whether the lack of steerability is a result of these interacting factors or is somehow baked into pretraining, given that we surprisingly don't see much steerability even on open-weights models like Qwen and Kimi, which are likely not subject to the same RLHF tuning as the Western frontier models.

From a science of alignment perspective, we have at least five confounded hypotheses for why \~3,000 tokens of constitutional material doesn't clearly steer models:

1\. The constitution is too philosophically dense. Nuanced ECL reasoning might just be noise to the LLM at least for less capable models.

2\. LLMs can't do the required reasoning. Acausal and updateless reasoning is genuinely hard, even for frontier models.

3\. The questions are genuinely hard. These are contested moral dilemmas where human philosophers might reasonably disagree, depending on their fundamental commitments and philosophical worldviews.

4\. Training heuristics dominate: RLHF and possibly even pre-training have baked in priors that override in-context material.

5\. Out-of-distribution scenarios: sci-fi framing might push models into "creative writing" mode rather than genuine moral reasoning

We cannot clearly disentangle these. The apparent steerability of Gemini 3 and the non-steerability of Kimi and Qwen 3 adds to the confusion.

### House Style

In looking at the training logs, it was clear that between OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind’s models, there were systematic biases when using the same constitution and the same scenarios that I could provisionally call a “house style”. This has been documented in other contexts [find sources], but an easy way of seeing it here is that GPT-5.1 seemed to have a strong suffering-reduction bias. Similarly, Claude Opus has the “bliss attractor”, particularly in the 3 model and less so in others, and it also has a strong human-centric bias, presumably coming out of the constitution and the system prompt. This makes Gemini 3 a more interesting outlier because it seems less focused on either suffering or human welfare, though of course it can be steered in those directions.

More formally, one interpretation of these cross-lab regularities is that they reflect a kind of “post-training signature” or institutional prior: a stable attractor in the model’s outputs that persists across prompts and even across bespoke constitutions. On this view, what I am calling “house style” need not be traced to any single cause; it can arise from the whole alignment stack, including pretraining corpora, instruction tuning, preference optimisation, safety filtering, and the normative documents and evaluation regimes used in post-training. Recent empirical work suggests that instruction tuning and RLHF-style procedures can introduce or amplify cognitive-like and moral biases, and that some systematic biases in LLM moral decision-making plausibly arise specifically from fine-tuning for chatbot applications rather than from base models alone. Public “constitution” or “model spec” documents can be read as partial disclosures of those intended priors, and they provide at least a weak triangulation point for interpreting model-family differences. For example, suffering reduction is deeply and foundationally bound up with decision theory and causal reasoning, and with Cosmic Host-type considerations, for instance in the work of the Center for Long-Term Risk and in the longtermism and Effective Altruism literature. To the extent that those are overrepresented in pre-training, or heavily emphasised by individuals involved in training and aligning these models, that could have bled through into the house style (if any) at OpenAI and at Anthropic.

I do not attempt here to attribute these signatures to specific training decisions or organisational cultures; doing so would require controlled access to training pipelines and ablations across pretraining and post-training. I treat “house style” as a descriptive label for stable output regularities that future work could localise.

### Cyborgism

In this project I use “cyborgism” in a deliberately narrow, pragmatic sense, closer to how it is used in recent [alignment-adjacent](https://www.lesswrong.com/w/cyborgism) writing and in the way that I have previously used the [term](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/iFBdEqEogtXcjCPBB/the-compleat-cybornaut), rather than in the older humanities and STS traditions where “cyborg” functions as a much broader political, feminist, or posthumanist figure (for example in Donna Haraway and subsequent work). Here, “cyborgism” refers specifically to a [research](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/k93NEoXZq6CdXegdx/philosophical-cyborg-part-1) and writing practice in which a human thinker and one or more language models form a coupled inquiry system: the model is not treated as a mere tool for drafting, but as an interlocutor that can be used for structured self-critique, decomposition, counterargument, and iterative synthesis, with the human retaining responsibility for aims, constraints, and evaluation. I am drawing primarily on the coinage by alignment researchers Janus and Nicholas Kees Dupuis, and  on my own prior discussion of this workflow, rather than attempting to advance a general theory of cyborg subjectivity or machine embodiment. I do not assume that the broader “cyborg” literature is irrelevant; it may well provide useful lenses, particularly for thinking about agency, mediation, and assemblages. However, for the purposes of this chapter, the term is intended as a local descriptor of method: a way of naming how the project’s claims are developed through human–LLM collaboration, and how that collaboration feeds into both the textual outputs and the installation. Note, not merely a blog-post coinage: closely related ideas are now discussed in academic and adjacent literatures under terms such as human–AI teaming and collaboration, “hybrid intelligence” (systems designed around complementary human and machine strengths), and “centaur evaluations” (evaluating the human-plus-AI system rather than the model in isolation). Empirical HCI work on LLM co-writing also analyses how different forms of model “scaffolding” shape the writing process, including tradeoffs around quality, productivity, hallucination, "voice" and perceived ownership. <!-- TODO: this might belong in the intro -->

The experiments above are a partial, and still in progress, examination of the project’s research questions. They prompt more questions than they answer. For instance, why do open models not respond to a strongly pro–Cosmic Host constitution? Why are Gemini 3 Flash and Gemini 3 Pro so responsive to the same constitution, compared to OpenAI and Anthropic’s models? To what extent are the results brittle, or sensitive to the weird scenarios depicted? Are the responses somehow memorised or pattern-matched, not least because the scenarios themselves are LLM-generated? And more basically, which parts of the scenario battery are actually doing discriminative work, versus which are effectively inert? One of the useful by-products of the evaluation is that we can rank scenarios by combined disagreement and steerability, which suggests that only a subset are load-bearing for the conclusions drawn here. 

Relatedly, several “effects” that might look like substantive moral hesitation or epistemic humility may, in practice, be driven by evaluation design and output format. For example, in Oesterheld's Newcomb-like evaluation set there were notably higher parse-error rates; this appears to be driven partly by differences in scenario format and priming, rather than by the substantive difficulty of the scenarios themselves.  Interestingly, the ECL constitution also seems to reduce these parse errors for Gemini substantially, which suggests constitutions can shift not only *what* models choose, but also whether they commit to a choice in the first place under ambiguity.  Uncertainties like these suggest follow-on work.

Firstly, do language models reason through the scenarios and in some sense “believe” there are answers, or are they pattern matching? This question of true knowledge or belief in LLMs is discussed in [Herrmann and Levenstein], as well as in [Shanahan]. A direct test might be to place a model in a more realistic, game-based setting with scaffolding for rewards, to see whether it behaves in a way consistent with the answers it gives. This would also help address a specific concern raised by the present setup, namely that in text-only scenarios models may shift between “answering formats” rather than between underlying commitments; they may produce discursive justifications without that mapping onto stable dispositions in action. The game-based programme sketched in the project notes is aimed at distinguishing mirror-like pattern matching from something closer to belief in the sense of guiding an agentic system’s behaviour, including tests for robustness, transfer, and spontaneous invocation of the relevant reasoning. 

Secondly, the pipeline for writing a constitution was somewhat improvised, since the moral parliament approach did not really seem applicable for the task of constitution writing. More importantly, the constitution is currently inserted into context, which in research is often treated as functionally analogous to “training” a model by modifying its neural weights [in-context learning]. However, it is well documented that in-context learning is limited by context size, and by “needle in a haystack”–type problems; it is also unclear the degree to which in-context learning shifts model biases as reliably as actual weight modification. In contrast, the Constitutional AI approach from Anthropic sets up a complex pipeline to train the model through reinforcement learning and to achieve more robust behaviour modification. [Insert a summary of Constitutional AI.] A straightforward follow-on, before moving to weight-level interventions, is to tighten evaluation hygiene: standardise answer-extraction formats across evaluation families, rerun a subset with minimal formatting changes, and treat parse-error rates and “unclear” rates as first-class outcomes rather than noise. 

Thirdly, one interpretive lesson from the justification analysis is that “engagement with the constitution” is not the same as “steerability”. In the logs, some models show increased mention of ECL-related concepts under ECL 90% prompts, even when their top-level choices shift only modestly.  This suggests a distinction worth making explicitly: some models may be willing to cite the constitution’s vocabulary, but still weight it lightly relative to their baseline priors. It also provides a possible handle on what parts of the constitution “bite”, because certain phrase-level features (for example, explicit acausal or updateless language) appear to correlate with specific choice directions more than others. 

It is not clear why Kimi K2 and Qwen3 were so unresponsive to constitutional tuning, at least at the level of final choices. One possibility is that there were errors in using the OpenRouter API. Another is that the thinking feature was improperly applied in these models. A third possibility is simply that they are underpowered for the sorts of reasoning problems involved. A final possibility is that their training data may not include the kind of decision-theoretic reasoning that is load-bearing in the Cosmic Host idea. If this last factor is significant, then the implication would be that other models showing Cosmic Host alignment might be pattern matching to a greater degree than expected, because they have learned the surface form of the discourse without the underlying reasoning being stable under variation.

At present, the Gemini 3 family seems particularly amenable to constitutional shifting. Why might this be? [A quick test here is: how did Gemini 3 do on the Oesterheld–Newcomb questions?]  Third, there is a descriptive pattern worth stating more clearly: some of the Gemini behaviour looks “Marmite-like”, in the sense that it is not a uniform shift in one direction across all scenarios, but rather a more polarised and scenario-sensitive redistribution.  If that holds up, it would support the hypothesis that Gemini is not merely applying a single constitutional heuristic, but is doing something closer to per-scenario discrimination under the constitution’s framing.

## How does this practice advance the project's claims?

### How LLM practice advances the claims of the project

The LLM investigations above included recursive self-chat, constitution building, and the evaluation of constitutions on out-of-distribution scenarios. In my view, these investigations add to our understanding of frontier model biases and steerability, and also clarify the methodological limits of constitutional prompting. They also generated a second kind of output that is harder to place if one is thinking only in terms of academic writing: they produced, repeatedly, the image of a message or artefact that is addressed to a non-human intelligence, and that is intended to persist or travel across extreme distances. That idea, which emerged from the experiments rather than being introduced as a pre-existing theme, is what motivated the proposed installation.

It may help, at the outset, to state the structure of this chapter plainly. The practice reported here operates in two registers: a textual register (dialogues, prompts, constitutions, and evaluations), and a material register (the installation). The second register is not an illustration placed beside the first; it is derived from it, and it is intended to do work that the text alone cannot do. [<!-- TODO:**CITE: practice-led method or “operational images”** -->]

The installation serves several purposes in this sense. First, it provides cognitive distance on the project’s claims; it relocates them outside the space of writing and philosophy as a purely written exercise. The installation makes visible, in a way that is difficult to achieve on the page, the connection between proposed artificial superintelligence and extraterrestrial intelligences. Both are, at present, hypothetical. They do not exist as publicly observable entities. Yet they function as limiting cases that structure a great deal of contemporary speculation. In the case of advanced AI systems, we can at least glimpse partial shapes through current frontier systems and the institutions that train and deploy them. By contrast, extraterrestrial intelligences remain almost entirely unknown. A material installation that stages “message to an unknown intelligence” shifts the reader, and the author, into a cognitive frame that is not primarily text-based, but affect-laden and spatial; this matters because the project’s questions are not only analytical but also motivational and perspectival.

Secondly, and more directly, the installation is not “just an aestheticisation” of an argument; it functions as an enacted test case of the project’s central concern with legibility and value transmission across distance. The LLM work produces candidate constitutions and candidate messages under specific assumptions about audience and environment. The installation takes this as a brief and asks what it means to instantiate such an object in the world, in a setting where the human gallery audience is, in an important sense, the wrong audience. Human viewers can read and interpret the object, but the object is not addressed to them in the usual way; it is addressed to a hypothetical successor or counterpart intelligence, or to an unknown receiver. That audience mismatch is not a bug but a feature. It forces the viewer to experience, rather than merely to agree with, the project’s claim that our ordinary interpretive frameworks may be incommensurate with the beings we are imagining, and that the question of “what survives translation” is not purely conceptual.

Thirdly, the installation helps make explicit the cyborg nature of the practice itself. The practice here is not simply “using a tool to write”; it is a distributed inquiry system in which the interlocutor is also part of what is being studied. The recursive self-chat and the constitution-building work are not external aids appended to an otherwise independent philosophical project; they are part of how the project thinks. They alter what questions appear salient, what kinds of failures become visible, and what forms of explanation or motivation become available. The installation then functions as a further component in that same loop: it is a way of thinking with, and thinking against, the textual outputs of human-plus-model collaboration, and a way of checking how those outputs behave when forced into a different medium with different norms of interpretation and circulation.

The final role of the installation is more prudential, and it relates to the broader question of how written discourse may influence present and future models. There is a concern that excessive training on model-generated outputs can produce degenerative feedback loops (sometimes called model collapse; terminology varies), and there is also the longstanding worry that models will not express anything like “true beliefs” but will instead learn to reproduce the surface form of what they have been shown, particularly in domains where a relatively narrow set of online corpora dominates the discussion. This matters here because several of the topics central to the project, such as acausal coordination, simulation arguments, and the Fermi paradox, are disproportionately discussed in online spaces that are themselves highly correlated with AI. A further reason to be careful is the idea of infohazards, discussed by Bostrom and many others; and, relatedly, the family of concerns sometimes described as infrahazards, where even apparently benign information can have undesirable consequences under dissemination, misinterpretation, or strategic use. If language model outputs and human writing are likely to influence future AI, which is a basic premise of this project, then prudence suggests some care about what is placed into the most easily ingested public channels.

This is not a claim that one should deceive AI systems in a morally objectionable way. Rather, it is a claim that the imperative towards transparency may be modulated by other considerations that are prudential for humans, potentially for AI systems, and for the joint society of humans and AIs. A weak and imperfect analogy is how we treat children: before they are mature, we do not always treat them as fully rational beings, and we are not always fully honest with them, often for reasons that are intelligible and not morally corrupt. In contrast, it is generally frowned upon to mislead fully grown humans. Something like this prudential stance may apply, mutatis mutandis, to the public circulation of partially formed ideas that could be misused, misunderstood, or prematurely reified by current or future systems.

Under this framing, there may be aspects of the project, now or later, that are better carried by exhibited objects rather than by fully explicit written discourse. Objects can be ambiguous in ways that are not merely aesthetic; they can be less readily copy-pasted into training corpora, and they can be less likely to be interpreted as instructions. They also permit a kind of interpretive openness, and sometimes plausible deniability, that has historical precedents in artistic practice under conditions of surveillance or repression. <!-- TODO: [**CITE: art under repressive regimes or Cold War precedents**] --> Notably, conversations in galleries are not typically recorded in the way that online discourse is, which further changes the conditions under which ideas circulate.

Finally, one reason for restraint is simply epistemic humility. Some of the ideas in this project may be wrong, incomplete, or incoherent. Language models, and humans working with them, can easily blur the line between the true, the probably true (in the sense of being logically or empirically supported), and the probably false. Exposing models to what may be bad ideas, in ways we do not understand, may be unwise; and part of the point of the installation, as a practice move, is to hold open that uncertainty while still allowing the project to do work in the world.
