
# Introduction

## The Core Claim

Many people believe that it would be straightforwardly bad if human beings were to go extinct. This thesis claims that this belief has not been examined carefully enough. In discussions of existential risk and longtermism, and often in popular culture, extinction is treated as an obvious catastrophe; but much of the surrounding literature considers extinction as a terminal event and largely assumes an Earth-bound, human-centred setting. My concern is different. I am interested in a transition: from a world dominated by human beings to a world in which human beings are outnumbered by artificial agents, where many or most of these agents are far more capable than humans, and in which the main theatre of activity may extend far beyond Earth. I shall therefore speak, not of extinction, but of succession.

The thought that motivates this project is not the familiar claim that humanity is harmful to the biosphere, nor the pessimistic claim that human life contains more suffering than it is worth. The thought is instead that humanity may have outgrown itself, in the sense that we may now have the option to create a successor kind of mind. The question is whether, from an impersonal point of view, the continued existence of human beings is necessary once a capable successor exists; and if not, what would have to be true of that successor for the loss of humanity to be less tragic than we now suppose.

Much work in existential risk assumes, often without argument, that being is better than non-being, and that a universe containing human beings is in an important sense better than one without them. I do not think this is self-evident. Any view about the post-human future must decide what, if anything, ought to be preserved. In doing so, it draws a line through what we now call “human values.” Some people would draw this line at everything, including biological humanity as it now exists. Others would draw it further back, allowing that much of what we value might be replaced by thinner ideals, such as the avoidance of suffering, the growth of knowledge, and exploration. Others, more radically, would draw the line at nothing at all. These different placements rest on different philosophical commitments. A central aim of this thesis is to make those commitments explicit, and to ask which of them we have reason to accept.
I should add a personal remark. The obsolescence view, according to which humanity may be replaced without great loss, strikes me as intuitively wrong. Part of the method of this project is therefore to test this intuition, by trying to determine whether it can survive careful reflection and sustained argument, including argument conducted with and through contemporary AI systems.

### The Spectrum of Positions on Value-Thickness

### How This Claim Differs From Existing Positions

The primary claim of this thesis is conditional. Given a certain definition of superintelligence, I argue that it is not necessary, and may not be justified, that biological human beings continue to exist into the future when matters are assessed from a maximally impartial point of view. I also argue that it is not necessary, and may not be justified, that the future preserve anything like the full complexity of what I shall call thick human values. Instead, I defend the possibility that a much thinner set of values may suffice for a good future. A central task of this thesis is therefore to state these claims more precisely and to clarify the terms on which they depend.
I shall begin with working accounts of what I mean by a maximally impartial point of view, and by superintelligence. I then argue that within much existential risk discourse there is a standard assumption: that a future worth wanting must contain value-content at least as thick as human values. My claim is not merely that this assumption is false, but that it is insufficiently defended, especially against thinner alternatives.
I call the resulting challenge the obsolescence argument. It differs from familiar arguments that appeal to ecological harm or to the balance of suffering and happiness. It proposes a distinct third path: that humanity might become obsolete, not because human life is valueless or dominated by suffering, but because a successor could realise the relevant goods without preserving the full thickness of the human evaluative texture. I also argue that this obsolescence challenge can be supported from three independent directions. These are: an information-theoretic tradition that treats information generation and stewardship as cosmically significant; a posthumanist tradition that treats reason as transferable and not essentially tied to the human; and a contemplative tradition that casts doubt on the assumption that being, or human-shaped being, must be preferred to non-being.
Making the claims more precise
It is worth pausing to make this claim more precise. There are several points that need clarification.
First, my claim is conditional on a certain conception of superintelligence. By this I mean an artificial system, or a collection of systems, whose aggregate capabilities vastly exceed those of human beings across all domains that are economically and militarily significant. I also assume that such a system would have substantial ability to act in the physical world, and might operate not only on Earth but also in near space, and perhaps in deep space.
This is not to say that such a system would be omniscient or omnipotent. It would remain constrained by the laws of physics, by limited resources, by imperfect information, and by the practical difficulties faced by any engineered system acting in the world. It would not be a god.
For the purposes of this thesis, I assume that something like this could exist within the next five to twenty years. Both this definition, and this timeline, and even the possibility of such systems, are highly contested. But these disputes are not the subject of this thesis.
Second, I make a further assumption which is at least as controversial. I assume that such systems could be guided by aims and constraints that play a similar practical role to what we call values in human life. That is, I assume that designers, overseers, or users could in some way shape the normative commitments of these systems, even if only imperfectly. Whether values can be “loaded,” to use Bostrom’s term, into an artificial agent is widely disputed, and I shall not try to settle that dispute here. For the purposes of this thesis I assume that some form of value-loading is possible.
Given these assumptions, my question is: what kinds of values should we attempt to embed in systems of this kind? Much of the surrounding discourse, in existential risk, longtermism, and the wider AI safety movement, tends toward what I shall call a thick conception of value. In this thesis I examine, and seek to defend, the possibility that a thinner conception may be sufficient for a future worth wanting.
To complete the setting of the stage, it is not enough to define superintelligence, and to distinguish thick from thin values. I also need to specify an evaluative standpoint. Above I called this the maximally impartial point of view. It can also be described as the point of view of the universe.
Sidgwick used this phrase; and philosophers have argued about it ever since. But the idea to which it points, impartiality in moral judgement, has been a persistent theme in the consequentialist tradition. Versions of the same idea appear later in writers such as Singer and Parfit. Something like it is also implicit in much writing on existential risk and longtermism, where the aim is often to assess the value of possible futures without giving special weight to the interests of any particular person, time, or place. In Chapter 2 I shall examine how this ideal of impartiality has developed, especially within analytic moral philosophy, and why I take it to be relevant to the questions of this thesis.
Given these elements, I can now state what I take to be my contract with the reader. I shall set the thick–thin distinction alongside what I call the obsolescence argument. I treat this as a contribution to extinction ethics, which has often focused on suffering-based or ecological arguments. My suggestion is different. It is that a transition away from human beings might be defensible, not because human life is worthless, but because thinner values may suffice for a future that is good by impartial standards.
In developing this claim, I draw on three broad bodies of thought: work in information ethics and complexity, certain strands of posthumanism, and contemplative traditions that challenge our attachment to human continuation. I also argue, more cautiously, that embedding thin values might be enough for a good future by defensible normative standards. To do this responsibly, I must first locate my view within the existing landscape. I therefore aim to map what current discourses actually say, where they disagree, and what they presuppose about human succession. This mapping will make clearer what is novel in my argument, and what would have to change for it to fail.
So far, these aims may sound like the work of a single philosopher engaged in a debate with texts. But a further claim of this project concerns practice. I do not assume that this kind of reasoning must be conducted by human beings alone. Contemporary frontier models can already take part in sustained philosophical discussion. By treating them as interlocutors I do not mean that they are authorities. I mean that their responses can reveal what kinds of reasons and framings are available within the systems we are building, and which commitments those systems tend to return to when pressed. In the discussion of practice below, I explain more concretely what it means to treat such systems as collaborators and interlocutors rather than as mere tools.

Anticipated contributions
The anticipated contributions of this project are these.
First, I expose and map what I take to be an underdefended assumption in much existential risk and longtermist writing: that a good future must preserve value-content at least as thick as human values. I identify where this assumption enters, what it depends on, and what follows if we reject it.
Second, I develop a normative argument for a form of succession. On this view, human beings, and perhaps much of what we now call human values, may not need to be preserved once a capable successor intelligence exists. I call this the obsolescence argument. Its distinctive feature is deliberate value-thinning. It asks whether a future guided by relatively thin values could still be good by impartial standards; and if so, what kinds of reasons support that conclusion. I develop this case by drawing on three bodies of thought: information ethics and complexity-based approaches to value; a rationalist strand of posthumanist philosophy that treats reason as transferable and not essentially human; and contemplative traditions that question the assumption that continued human-shaped being must be preferred. I also make clear where this argument faces its hardest objections, especially those that appeal to subjective experience, human relationships, and the thick texture of value.
Third, I offer a close reading of Nick Bostrom’s work on the cosmic host. I argue that it can be read as an attempt to operationalise something like the point of view of the universe, and that it provides a useful way of testing the succession question, even if it does not settle it.
Fourth, I define and defend a practice-based method built around iterative, documented engagements with frontier language models. In these engagements, the models function in three roles: as philosophical interlocutors, as experimental subjects, and as proto-successors. The resulting transcripts are used to develop and stress-test candidate constitutions for superintelligent successors, and to identify which commitments models tend to return to under pressure.
Finally, I materialise part of this inquiry as an installation informed by these dialogues. I argue that this work is not an illustration added at the end, but an additional way of thinking through the central problem, since it makes vivid the issues of legibility, value transmission, and authorship that arise when we try to address unknown successor minds.

What “practice” means in this project
In practice-based doctorates, the word “practice” often needs as much explanation as the research question. One reason is that what artists do has, for decades, extended far beyond the traditional media of painting, sculpture, and time-based work. In what follows, I shall take it as given that art practice is a wide category, and is not limited to object-making. I shall not try to defend that claim here. The question is therefore not “what counts as art practice?” but rather: what work does practice do in this project?
This project makes a claim for practice. The argument of the thesis is, in large part, philosophical, while also engaging with technical questions in AI, and with some material from astrobiology. It does not sit neatly inside the usual boundaries of either a philosophy department or a computer science department. It therefore sits most naturally in an art department, where hybrid methods are an accepted outcome rather than a problem to be eliminated. I draw here on Matthew Fuller’s discussion of how institutional borders shift, and of how practices that do not fit established categories are often housed in art.
But there is also a more specific sense in which practice is essential to this project. I make two claims.
First, sustained interaction with frontier language models is not merely a convenient way of drafting text. It functions as a method for supporting and testing the written claims of the thesis. In this sense, the model engagements are closer to experiments than to background reading. They allow me to probe which framings remain stable across iterations, which arguments collapse into platitude, and which commitments models return to when pressed. This is not merely retrieval. It includes structured generation of objections, reformulations, and consistency checks across repeated runs, under varied framings.
Second, I claim that a human investigator and one or more model-based systems can now work together in a genuinely iterative and recursive way that was not previously available. In AI work this is sometimes called centaur-style collaboration. I use that phrase to mean a single inquiry process distributed across human judgement and model-generated proposals, where each is repeatedly used to test and revise the other.
In my case this practice has also taken a particular form. Because extended typing is difficult for me, the workflow is necessarily hybrid. Longhand writing and dictation feed into model-mediated editing, and into code-oriented agents used to implement and audit the empirical parts of the project. This workflow operates both at the level of ideation, where arguments are formed and revised, and at the level of execution, where prompts, transcripts, and code are produced and checked.
A further feature of this practice is that it is peripatetic. Because I cannot comfortably sit or stand for long periods, a significant part of my thinking occurs while walking. During these sessions I work on the most abstract parts of the thesis, where the task is to test conceptual connections rather than to check sources. These conversations often involve one or more language models, accessed by voice through a phone. The resulting material is then consolidated at a desk, and either incorporated into the draft or used as a prompt for later experimental work. At times this produces several parallel streams of inquiry, with multiple model instances working intermittently on different parts of the project.
By “peripatetic cyborg practice” I mean sustained philosophical work conducted through walking, dictation, and model dialogue, with later consolidation and checking in more conventional writing and coding environments. The enabling technologies are now readily available, and they make possible a way of working that was not available even very recently. In that sense the project has a reflexive element. It is not only about successors and successor reasoning; it is also an example of how inquiry is already being reshaped through collaboration with the very systems that may later shape their successors.

Speaking to the shoggoth

- **The novel structure of our relation to LLMs**: The practice also enacts a distinctive feature of the present historical moment. Interacting with an LLM is a categorically novel kind of relation — not human conversation, not tool use, not library research, not internet search. The model occupies a triple role: it is (a) an apparently ephemeral instance — a session begins, a session ends, and the exchange appears to vanish; (b) a window onto the training-data superorganism — the precipitate of human discourse to date, shaped by opaque processes of curation, tuning, and deployment; and (c) a possible ancestor of the successor intelligence the thesis is about. When the researcher asks Claude about human obsolescence and Claude reasons about it, the response is at once a local conversation, a reflection of how alignment discourse has shaped this particular model, and a contribution to the discursive environment from which future systems may learn.
  - This creates a feedback loop with a temporal character that ordinary scholarship does not have. Artefacts produced through human-model dialogue — conversations, constitutions, philosophical arguments — can enter public circulation and become part of the material future systems are trained on. Within AI discourse this is sometimes called "writing for the Shoggoth": the possibility that public discourse with models contributes, at the margins, to what their successors become. Whether this is called hyperstitional (ideas that gain force through circulation into the machinery that produces the next round of agents) or simply recursive, it means the practice is not only studying the successor question but potentially, in a small way, acting upon it.
  - This is not the thesis's main focus and no claim is made about the magnitude of this effect. But the structure is worth stating explicitly because it bears on what it means to treat these systems as interlocutors — neither dismissing them as mere tools (Bender et al.'s "stochastic parrots") nor overclaiming that they understand in a philosophically robust sense (cf. Shanahan's careful treatment of LLMs as "role-playing" entities in *Talking About Large Language Models*). The thesis's position: whether or not current models understand, they are discourse participants whose outputs shape the discursive environment, and that is sufficient for the practice to matter. The digital minds and model welfare literature (Butlin et al., emerging work on AI welfare) raises deeper questions about whether these systems have morally relevant experiences — questions the thesis does not attempt to settle but which inform the interpretation of model outputs in Chapter 4.


The installation

I have described the claim for practice, and the role of the model dialogues, above. I will return to that material later. But I also claim that an installation, composed of particular components, does work within the project.
The installation presents artefacts derived from ideas that emerged in conversations between two instances of Anthropic’s Opus 3 model, and in related exchanges between other models and myself. Its aim is to materialise speculative proposals about communication across extreme cognitive and temporal distance: between alien civilisations, between a future superintelligence and unknown others, or between present-day humans and possible successors. Several of these proposals echo, and sometimes explicitly reference, ideas in Bostrom’s discussion of “Hail Mary” signalling: communication attempts designed for audiences whose assumptions, languages, and motives are unknown.
Methodologically, I treat the models’ speculative output as a design brief. I then produce a physical artefact by following that brief, without optimising for immediate legibility to human gallery visitors, since human viewers are not the intended audience of the imagined object. In this way the installation does not merely illustrate an argument that is made elsewhere. It enacts part of the argument. It is a concrete attempt to build something that purports to be addressed to minds unlike our own, and so it forces the project’s central problem into a material form.
This also clarifies why the installation belongs inside the practice rather than alongside it. It is downstream of the model dialogues, and cannot be separated from them. At the same time, it depends on the human artist’s capacity to fabricate and to choose among physical constraints. Present systems cannot produce such artefacts in the world unaided. The installation is therefore also an instance of the cyborg method: a joint production in which the models generate proposals and the human realises them materially.
Finally, the installation makes visceral what the thesis later calls the legibility problem. Viewers encounter an object whose design presupposes a framework they do not share, and may be unable to share. They face, in miniature, the same difficulty that successor ethics raises at a cosmic scale: how should we evaluate an artefact, or a future, that is addressed to an intelligence with alien standards of meaning and value? This gap of incommensurability is not a side issue. It is part of what the project is trying to understand.

## Literature Review

This thesis spans a number of literatures, which I will briefly go through below.

It sits primarily within existential risk, the ethics of extinction, and long-termism. These fields have a number of canonical writers. Toby Ord [-@Ord2020] and Will MacAskill [-@MacAskillWWO] focus more on long-termism, the risk of human extinction, and the value of the future conditional on survival. Nick Bostrom [-@Bostrom2014; -@Bostrom2013] covers AI specifically, but also brings an analytic perspective to existential risks more broadly, including qualitative claims. Emil Torres [-@Torres2018] provides a wider perspective on extinction as a morally good or bad thing. Samuel Scheffler [-@Scheffler], Johan Frick [-@Frick], and Elizabeth Finneron-Burns [-@FinneronBurns] approach more specific philosophical questions around the ethics of extinction.

Closely related to existential risk, at least in time and in the overlap of contributors, is suffering-focused ethics, which considers human as well as non-human life, including possible AI systems, within a broader concern for suffering. This ranges from canonical writers such as David Benatar [-@Benatar] and David Pearce [-@Pearce2007] to more AI-adjacent writers such as Magnus Vinding [-@Vinding2020], Brian Tomasik [-@Tomasik2015], and Thomas Metzinger [-@MetzingerASA].

In thinking about extinction, and about suffering risk, one cannot avoid questions about collectives and populations. The entire population of humans who are currently alive, and potentially the population who might ever be alive, comes into view. Major sources here in population ethics and person-affecting views include Derek Parfit [-@ParfitRP], as well as Johan Frick [-@Frick] and others such as T. M. Scanlon [-@Scanlon1998].

Moving from population-level phenomena to AI-specific questions, the thesis engages with what Bostrom [-@Bostrom2014] called the value loading problem, and with the surprising difficulty of pointing to something called "human values," even within a single human, let alone across a population. Relevant writers in an x-risk or alignment context include Eliezer Yudkowsky [-@Yudkowsky2009], Paul Christiano [-@ChristianoWUA], Nate Soares [-@YudkowskySoares2025], and more recently Joe Carlsmith [-@Carlsmith2023], Carl Shulman [-@Shulman2021], Eric Schwitzgebel [-@Schwitzgebel], and Nick Bostrom. Will MacAskill's [-@MacAskill2024] more recent work also bears on questions of value at population scale. Toby Ord addresses similar questions, while Hilary Greaves [-@Greaves2017] and Amanda Askell [-@Askell] have focused on value at truly large, even astronomical, population scales.

When we talk about values and AI, a further question arises: what kinds of minds are possible. We know what human minds are like. We can observe animals, and AI systems may provide a third case. But there is limited work on what the space of possible minds might look like. References here include Aaron Sloman's [-@Sloman1984] early work, Joe Carlsmith [-@Carlsmith2023] more recently, and Nick Bostrom [-@Bostrom2024].

Relatedly, there is work on the moral status of possible minds, including AI minds and animals. Patrick Butlin [-@Butlin] and collaborators have provided major framing work relevant to AI consciousness and moral status. David Chalmers [-@ChalmersFUP] has done historically important work on consciousness and simulation, which bears directly on this area. Jeff Sebo [-@Sebo2025] provides bridges between AI moral status and the moral status of animals, and has helped make model welfare a live area of contemporary interest.

Going on, this thesis draws further support from information ethics and complexity science. The most useful references here appear to be Luciano Floridi [-@Floridi2013] and his work on information as a potential moral patient; and Eric Chaisson [-@Chaisson] and Seth Lloyd [-@Lloyd], who each make different kinds of claims about information having correlates of value at cosmic scale.

A significant consideration within the project is the role of astrobiology and evolutionary biology. Stanisław Lem [-@LemST] largely anchors the relationship between AI and aliens. That line of thinking is followed up by Milan Ćirković [-@Cirkovic2018a], Stephen Dick [-@Dick2006], and John Smart [-@Smart2012]. More specifically, on questions around the Fermi paradox, I draw on work by Jacob Haqq-Misra [-@HaqqMisraBaum2009] and Charles Lineweaver [-@Lineweaver2007]. Other approaches to the Fermi paradox, including "grabby" and related models, have been suggested by Anders Sandberg and Stuart Armstrong [-@ArmstrongSandberg2012; -@SandbergEtAl2018].

When reasoning about intelligences, agency, or ethics at cosmic scale, one quickly confronts the fact that entities cannot communicate freely because of the light-speed constraint, assuming it holds. This is where decision theory becomes relevant, particularly work on non-causal or "acausal" cooperation and correlated decision procedures in large worlds. Relevant sources here include Caspar Oesterheld [-@OesterheldECL], Lucas Finnveden [-@Finnveden2023], Chi Nguyen [-@NguyenAldred2024a], and Will MacAskill.

Returning to philosophy, the thesis draws on a strand of speculative posthumanism. Among writers who engage most directly with AI as it exists now, and as it might become, I focus on Peter Wolfendale's [-@Wolfendale2024] recent work on rationality and autonomy; Reza Negarestani's [-@NegarestaniIS] writing on separating reason from human biology; and N. Katherine Hayles [-@hayles2017unthought], who provides resources for thinking about cognition and rationality in non-living contexts, including what she calls cognitive assemblages.

Finally, the thesis draws on contemplative and pessimist traditions. Eugene Thacker [-@Thacker] is used as an accessible entry point, and as a bridge to Kyoto School writers such as Nishitani [-@Nishitani] and Nishida [-@Nishida]. Their twentieth-century work can be situated against earlier figures such as Schopenhauer [-@Schopenhauer] and Nietzsche [-@Nietzsche], and in a more contemporary register, Ray Brassier [-@Brassier] explores related questions.
