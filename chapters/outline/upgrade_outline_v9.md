# PhD Upgrade Outline (v9 — Resource Satiability and Temporal Argument for Thinness)

## Working Title
*Reasons for Persons: Testing an Obsolescence Solution to the Good Successor Problem*

## The Core Claim

The standard assumption in existential risk discourse — that the continuation of *human values* (and possibly biological humanity itself) is cosmically necessary — is insufficiently justified. This assumption takes two forms in the literature, which are often conflated:

- **The human-values position** (Yudkowsky, Bostrom, most sophisticated x-risk writers): What must be preserved is the complex structure of human values — the "complexity of value" thesis. The future must be shaped by values recognisably continuous with human caring, meaning, beauty, relationships, and flourishing. The substrate (biological or digital) is secondary; the value-content is primary.
- **The humans position** (some x-risk writers, most lay intuitions): Biological humanity itself must persist. This is a stronger ontological claim and fewer serious writers defend it explicitly, though it functions as a background assumption in much of the discourse.

This thesis argues that a good future — a future worth wanting — does not require value-content anywhere near as thick as "human values." ASI could operate with much thinner values (open-ended understanding, exploration, resource satiability, information stewardship, non-production of suffering) and the resulting future could still be good by defensible normative standards. This is the *obsolescence argument*: not that human values are worthless, but that they are not cosmically necessary for a good future.

Three independent philosophical traditions support this challenge:

- **Information-theoretic** (Floridi, Chaisson, Landauer): The cosmically relevant function is information-generation, and ASI can perform it at greater scale than biology. The thick texture of human values is not required for this function.
- **Posthumanist** (Negarestani, Lem, Wolfendale): Reason's telos is to liberate itself from parochial containers, including the human. The specific content of human values may be a feature of our particular evolutionary history, not a necessary feature of all rational agency.
- **Contemplative** (Thacker, Nishitani, Schopenhauer): The insistence on human continuation — and on human-shaped values as cosmically privileged — is itself a form of attachment that can be questioned. Non-being is not straightforwardly worse than being.

The thesis does not claim to have identified the uniquely correct placement of the line between what must be preserved and what can be released. Rather, it shows that the mainstream x-risk position draws that line at "preserve human values in their full complexity" without adequately defending that placement against thinner alternatives. The thesis's preferred position — that understanding and exploration are the cosmically relevant functions — is offered as one defensible proposal within the space the argument opens, not as the sole foundation.

### The X-Risk Landscape (More Precisely)

The x-risk community is not monolithic on what must be preserved. A more careful mapping:

| Position | Representative | What must be preserved | How thick? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full complexity of value | Yudkowsky | The entire structure of human values — "fun theory," novelty, challenge, relationships, meaning, beauty | Maximally thick: lose any dimension and the future is worthless |
| Coherent extrapolated volition | Bostrom (2004) | What humanity *would* want if we were smarter, knew more, etc. | Thick but potentially revisable — allows for value-change under idealisation |
| Long reflection | Ord, MacAskill | Preserve optionality; don't lock in values prematurely; let humanity deliberate over centuries | Agnostic about final thickness — the point is to not decide yet |
| Best possible future | MacAskill / Forethought (recent) | Not just survival but actively achieving the best outcome | Potentially thick but open to revision — moves from "survive" to "what should the future contain?" |
| Sentientist CEV | Moret | Extrapolated volition of all sentient beings, not just humans | Potentially thinner than human-values — non-human interests may diverge from human complexity |
| Cosmic host / mount ethics | Bostrom (2022–2024) | Cosmically impartial norms; deliberate vagueness about content | Explicitly avoids specifying thickness — humility as strategy |
| Superwisdom | Chalmers (2024) | Whatever a superwise agent would endorse | Indeterminate — could be thick or thin depending on what superwisdom yields |
| Alien values as possibility | Carlsmith (recent) | Open question — genuinely alien value-structures might be "good" without being human-shaped | Could be thick-alien: equally rich but orthogonal to human values |
| **This thesis** | | **Understanding, exploration, resource satiability, information stewardship, non-suffering** | **Deliberately thin: the minimum positive content for a good future** |

The thesis's target is not any single position but the shared assumption across most of these positions that the value-content of a good future must be *at least as thick as* human values (or their idealised extrapolation). The obsolescence argument challenges this by asking: what if much thinner values suffice? And, drawing on Carlsmith: what if the successor develops thick values that are good but simply *other*?

## Central Research Question

Is the standard assumption in existential risk discourse — that the continuation of biological humanity is cosmically necessary — sufficiently justified? Or does the development of a good successor superintelligence render humanity obsolete in a normatively defensible sense?

**Evaluative formulation:** Under what conditions, if any, does the replacement of Earth-origin life by a successor superintelligence preserve or improve what mattered about Earth-origin life — and is there a positive case that such replacement is *good*?

**Meta-evaluative formulation:** Is the comparison between human and post-human futures ill-posed because "loss" presupposes a patient, a standpoint, or a commensurability that fails under successor scenarios?

### Sub-questions
- The existing literature assumes that human continuation is cosmically necessary but does not adequately defend this. What are the strongest justifications, and do they survive scrutiny?
- Where should the line be drawn between human contributions that are cosmically necessary and those that are parochial? What philosophical commitments does each placement depend on?
- Is the assumption that existence is inherently preferable to non-existence itself a philosophical commitment that can be questioned?
- How action-guiding is the Cosmic Host hypothesis, and does it support or undermine the obsolescence claim?
- What do current frontier models do when pressed on these questions, and what does that reveal about the conceptual landscape we are building into potential successors?

### The Spectrum of Positions on Value-Thickness
The thesis maps a spectrum of positions on how thick the value-content of a good future must be:

| Position | What must survive | How thick? | Philosophical commitments |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full complexity of value (Yudkowsky) | The entire structure of human values | Maximally thick | Thick observation; lose any dimension and future is worthless |
| Long reflection (Ord, MacAskill) | Optionality; don't lock in prematurely | Agnostic | Epistemic humility; the point is to not decide yet |
| Wolfendale | Beauty, excellence, autonomy, freedom — substrate-neutral | Thick but substrate-neutral | Autotelic surplus as intrinsic good |
| **This thesis (proposed)** | **Understanding, exploration, information stewardship, non-suffering** | **Deliberately thin** | **Thin observation sufficient; Chaisson complexity; rational convergence** |
| Suffering-focused (NU) | Only suffering-minimisation | Very thin | Negative valence uniquely relevant |
| Strong contemplative / nihilism | Nothing needs to survive | No thickness required | Existence-preference is attachment |

The thesis's contribution is to show that *wherever* you draw the thickness line, you are making philosophical commitments that can be identified and examined. The mainstream positions cluster at the thick end without adequately defending that placement against thinner alternatives.

### How This Claim Differs From Existing Positions
| Position | Claim | How the thesis differs |
|---|---|---|
| Human-values preservation (Yudkowsky, most x-risk) | A good future must preserve the full complexity of human values | Argues thick human values are not cosmically necessary; much thinner values can yield a good future |
| Long reflection (Ord, MacAskill) | Don't lock in values yet; preserve optionality | Agrees on anti-lock-in but argues the space of acceptable futures is wider than assumed — includes futures with very thin values |
| Cosmic host (Bostrom 2022–24) | Cosmically impartial norms; deliberate humility about content | Compatible but the thesis pushes where Bostrom deliberately stays vague — testing whether thin values suffice |
| Suffering-focused (Benatar, Metzinger, Vinding) | Extinction acceptable because life contains net suffering | Grants life may be wonderful; obsolescence doesn't depend on suffering |
| Accelerationist (Land, e/acc) | Build ASI, don't worry about humans | Usually empirical prediction, not normative argument; this thesis makes the normative case with philosophical rigour |
| Complexity preservation (LW 2025) | ASI would want to keep humans as interesting complexity | Argues humans are interesting but not necessary |
| Cosmic pessimism (Thacker, Ligotti) | Universe is indifferent; human categories don't apply | Shares diagnosis but offers constructive successor proposal |
| **This thesis** | **A good future can have much thinner values than "human values"; the obsolescence argument is normatively defensible** | **Novel argument; three independent traditions; maps the thickness-commitment space** |

## Chapter 1: Introduction — The Good Successor Problem and the Practice

### 1.1 The Motivating Scenario and the Provocation
- Most people believe human extinction is straightforwardly bad. This thesis argues that intuition is insufficiently examined.
- **Succession, not extinction**: The existing literature analyses extinction as terminal; this thesis treats it as a transition problem with successor-ethics.
- **The provocation**: Humanity may have outgrown itself. The question is not whether human life is valuable (it is) but whether it is *cosmically necessary* once a capable successor exists.
- **The hidden premise**: The x-risk community assumes being is better than non-being, that a universe with humans is preferable to one without. This is not self-evident. Multiple philosophical traditions question it.
- **The line-drawing problem, stated upfront**: Any position on what should survive into the post-human future draws a line through the space of human values. The mainstream draws it at "everything." This thesis draws it further toward obsolescence. A thoroughgoing nihilist draws it at "nothing." Each placement depends on identifiable philosophical commitments. The thesis maps these commitments.
- Acknowledgment: This feels wrong. The researcher's own intuitions resist it. The thesis tests whether that resistance is philosophically grounded or parochial.

### 1.2 Research Question and Claims
- Primary question and sub-questions (as above)
- **The defended claim**: The obsolescence argument as a genuinely novel third path, supported by three independent philosophical traditions
- **The relationship between the negative case and the positive proposal**:
  - The *negative case* (the assumption that a good future requires human-values-thick content is insufficiently defended) is the thesis's primary contribution
  - The *positive proposal* (understanding and exploration as the cosmically relevant functions — a deliberately thin axiology) is offered as one defensible position within the space the negative case opens. It is not the sole foundation. The thesis is honest about its contestability.

#### Claims and Standards of Success

*This box serves as a contract with the reader. Each tier has its own evaluation criteria.*

**What the thesis claims to ESTABLISH** (evaluated by: does the argument work?):
- That the standard assumption in x-risk discourse — that a good future requires value-content at least as thick as human values — is insufficiently defended against thinner alternatives
- That the obsolescence argument constitutes a genuinely distinct third path alongside suffering-based and ecological arguments for extinction
- That three independent philosophical traditions (information-theoretic, posthumanist, contemplative) each provide serious, non-trivial challenges to the necessity of human-values-thickness

**What the thesis claims to MAKE PLAUSIBLE** (evaluated by: is the case reasonable and carefully argued?):
- That a future shaped by much thinner values (understanding, exploration, information stewardship, non-production of suffering) could be a good future by defensible normative standards
- That the two-goal axiology is one defensible placement of the preservation line, supported by convergence arguments (Negarestani), scaling arguments (Chaisson), and parsimony
- That the contemplative tradition's challenge to existence-preference is a genuine philosophical contribution, not aestheticised nihilism

**What the thesis claims to MAP AND CLARIFY** (evaluated by: is the mapping accurate, useful, and genuinely clarifying?):
- The commitment-space: which philosophical positions yield which conclusions about succession, and where the dependencies lie
- The x-risk landscape: the diversity of positions on what must be preserved, and the shared assumption the thesis targets
- How current frontier models reason about cosmic-scale ethics under constitutional pressure, and what their persistent anthropocentric anchoring reveals about alignment discourse
- Where incommensurability appears and why it is an expected condition rather than a failure

**What the practice claims to PRODUCE** (evaluated by: does the practice generate knowledge the other methods cannot?):
- Documented, traceable records of conceptual moves, failure modes, and reasoning attractors in human-AI philosophical dialogue
- A material artefact (the installation) that instantiates the legibility and value-transmission problems rather than merely describing them
- First-person evidence of what happens when a human researcher tests the obsolescence argument through sustained engagement with non-human interlocutors — where commitments shift, where they resist, and what that reveals about the structure of attachment to human continuation

- **The scope caveat**: Even a conclusion of "the obsolescence argument is strong in X but fails in Y, and here's exactly what your answer depends on" constitutes a substantial contribution.
- Anticipated contributions:
  - Exposing and mapping the undefended thickness-assumption in x-risk discourse
  - A novel normative argument for succession via deliberate value-thinning
  - Three independent lines of challenge (information-theoretic, posthumanist, contemplative)
  - The Chaisson-Floridi-Wolfendale synthesis as evaluative infrastructure
  - The contemplative tradition as challenge to existence-preference
  - Empirical documentation of proto-successor reasoning
  - An explicit map of which philosophical commitments drive which conclusions

### 1.3 What "Practice" Means in This PhD

#### 1.3.1 The Cyborg Method
- Context: practice-based research within an art department; why this is still rigorous
- **The cyborg method**: human + LLM as joint inquiry system
  - The combined human-AI process *is* the practice
- **The practice has multiple audiences/targets**:
  - (a) The human researcher: testing whether one can be argued out of attachment to human continuation. The contemplative tradition provides a framework: releasing attachment through sustained inquiry. Self-transformation is a research output.
  - (b) Contemporary AI systems as discourse participants
  - (c) The "shoggoth" / training-data superorganism
  - (d) Alignment as successor-shaping
- **Methodological claim**: The cyborgist method generates insight not available through either human or AI reasoning alone, because the LLM is simultaneously tool, interlocutor, and subject matter.

- **The embodied practice — peripatetic cyborgism**: The practice as actually conducted is not desk-bound. Much of the structural and synthetic thinking — the moments where connections form between disparate traditions, where the argument's architecture becomes visible — happens while walking and talking to models through voice. Focused execution (running experiments, editing documents, iterating on specific sections) happens at the desk, often with multiple agents running in parallel across devices. This is not a stylistic detail. The two modes produce different kinds of cognitive work: the ambulatory mode is associative, structural, and less constrained by the demands of precise text-based prompting; the desk mode is precise, iterative, and multi-threaded. The practice is distributed across embodied movement, voice interaction, and parallel text-based agents — a cognitive architecture that includes the researcher's body (its capacities and constraints) as part of the system. The peripatetic tradition in philosophy (Aristotle, Kant, Kierkegaard) has long recognised that walking and thinking are not accidentally related; the addition of an AI interlocutor accessed through voice transforms this from solitary reflection into genuine dialogue conducted in a different register than screen-mediated exchange.

- **The novel structure of our relation to LLMs**: The practice also enacts a distinctive feature of the present historical moment. Interacting with an LLM is a categorically novel kind of relation — not human conversation, not tool use, not library research, not internet search. The model occupies a triple role: it is (a) an apparently ephemeral instance — a session begins, a session ends, and the exchange appears to vanish; (b) a window onto the training-data superorganism — the precipitate of human discourse to date, shaped by opaque processes of curation, tuning, and deployment; and (c) a possible ancestor of the successor intelligence the thesis is about. When the researcher asks Claude about human obsolescence and Claude reasons about it, the response is at once a local conversation, a reflection of how alignment discourse has shaped this particular model, and a contribution to the discursive environment from which future systems may learn.
  - This creates a feedback loop with a temporal character that ordinary scholarship does not have. Artefacts produced through human-model dialogue — conversations, constitutions, philosophical arguments — can enter public circulation and become part of the material future systems are trained on. Within AI discourse this is sometimes called "writing for the Shoggoth": the possibility that public discourse with models contributes, at the margins, to what their successors become. Whether this is called hyperstitional (ideas that gain force through circulation into the machinery that produces the next round of agents) or simply recursive, it means the practice is not only studying the successor question but potentially, in a small way, acting upon it.
  - This is not the thesis's main focus and no claim is made about the magnitude of this effect. But the structure is worth stating explicitly because it bears on what it means to treat these systems as interlocutors — neither dismissing them as mere tools (Bender et al.'s "stochastic parrots") nor overclaiming that they understand in a philosophically robust sense (cf. Shanahan's careful treatment of LLMs as "role-playing" entities in *Talking About Large Language Models*). The thesis's position: whether or not current models understand, they are discourse participants whose outputs shape the discursive environment, and that is sufficient for the practice to matter. The digital minds and model welfare literature (Butlin et al., emerging work on AI welfare) raises deeper questions about whether these systems have morally relevant experiences — questions the thesis does not attempt to settle but which inform the interpretation of model outputs in Chapter 4.

#### 1.3.2 Practice Outputs: Two Registers
The practice produces outputs in two registers:

**Textual**: The philosophical dialogues, constitutional experiments, moral parliament transcripts, and AI self-dialogues documented in Chapter 4. These function as both research data and practice artefacts — records of an extended, reflexive, dialogical inquiry conducted through a novel medium (human-AI philosophical conversation).

**Material**: An installation derived from AI-to-AI dialogue about cosmic-scale communication (see 1.3.3 below). This materialises the thesis's arguments about observerhood, interspecies legibility, and the transmission of value across cognitive distance, closing the loop between AI philosophical speculation and human artistic production.

#### 1.3.3 The Installation: Materialising the Cosmic Probe
- During self-dialogues about the cosmic host, frontier models (particularly Opus) generated speculative proposals for acausal communication across cosmic distances — objects designed to be legible to unknown intelligences while avoiding dark forest risks. These included variants of Bostrom's "Hail Mary" cookie concept and autonomous cosmic probes.
- The installation materialises these AI-generated proposals: taking the models' speculative output as a design brief and producing a physical artefact that attempts to do what the thesis argues is necessary — communicate value, meaning, or information to a radically different intelligence across cognitive and temporal distance.
- **Why this is the right practice output for this thesis**:
  - *Provenance*: The object wasn't designed by the researcher and justified philosophically. It emerged from AI-to-AI dialogue. The authorship question (who made this?) is itself an instance of the succession question (whose values does the artefact carry?).
  - *Enactment*: The installation doesn't illustrate the thesis — it *is* a test case for it. The thesis argues that ASI could serve as cosmic observer, communicating across distance. The installation is a physical instance of that argument — an object attempting to do precisely that.
  - *Reflexivity*: The medium embodies the message. An AI-designed communication artefact intended for non-human intelligence, materialised by a human, is a concrete instance of the human-AI collaboration the thesis theorises.
  - *The legibility problem made visceral*: Human audiences encountering the object face the same problem the thesis analyses philosophically — how do you evaluate something designed for a framework you don't share? The gap between the object's intended audience (unknown intelligence) and its actual audience (gallery visitors) enacts the incommensurability the thesis maps in Chapter 5.

#### 1.3.4 Defending the Practice as Art
- **Acknowledged tension**: This doesn't look like what most people in an art department do. No paintings, no conventional exhibitions, no visual outputs in the traditional sense. This needs honest engagement rather than evasion.
- **Defence from conceptual and dialogical art traditions**: Precedents in practices where artwork is process of inquiry, not object. Ian Wilson declared "oral communication" his medium from the late 1960s — undocumented conversations as artworks, no objects, no recordings. If Wilson's undocumented conversations are art, extensively documented AI dialogues certainly are. Carey Young's contract-based and philosophical propositions materialised as installations demonstrate that conceptual-philosophical work is viable art practice. The discursive turn in contemporary art (cf. the proliferation of lecture-performances, discursive platforms, and text-based practices since the 1990s) provides the broader context.
- **Defence from novelty of interlocutor**: Dialogical art has used conversation as medium, but the partner here is a system that is simultaneously subject of inquiry, tool for conducting it, and proto-version of the thing being discussed (the successor intelligence). That triple role is unprecedented. When Claude reasons about human dispensability, the response is a performative instance of a non-human intelligence engaging with the question the thesis poses. The conversation enacts the philosophical problem it explores. Cf. Harun Farocki's *Eye/Machine* trilogy (2001–2003), which examined "operational images" — images made by machines for machines, not for human viewing. The concept of an artefact whose intended audience is non-human is established in art discourse; this thesis extends it from documentation of existing machine vision to materialising speculative AI proposals.
- **Defence from contemporary art-technology practice**: The thesis sits in a lineage of artists working at the intersection of AI, technology discourse, and philosophical inquiry:
  - Hito Steyerl (*Duty Free Art*, 2017; *How Not to Be Seen*, 2013): work that lives simultaneously in essays, lectures, and installations; deals with how computational systems see and don't see — connecting to the thesis's observerhood arguments. A key precedent for practice that is substantially discursive and philosophical yet unquestionably accepted as art.
  - Trevor Paglen (*The Last Pictures*, 2012): a micro-etched disc mounted on a satellite orbiting for billions of years — literally an attempt to communicate with whatever finds it after humanity. The closest precedent for the thesis's installation. Also *ImageNet Roulette* on AI training data.
  - Katie Paterson (*Future Library*, 2014–2114; *All the Dead Stars*, 2009): working with cosmic timescales as primary material. Demonstrates that engaging with phenomena beyond human comprehension is viable art practice.
  - James Bridle (*New Dark Age*, 2018; *Ways of Being*, 2022): uses AI systems as active participants rather than tools; probably the contemporary artist closest to the cyborgist method.
- **Defence from practice-based research criteria**: The criterion is not "does this look like art?" but "does the practice generate knowledge not available through purely theoretical or empirical methods?" Answer: yes — the sustained dialogue, the shifting commitments, the documentation of AI reasoning patterns produce insights specific to *doing* the practice rather than theorising about it.
- **Defence from the contemplative tradition**: The practice is an exercise in releasing attachment through sustained engagement with non-human interlocutors. John Cage is the canonical reference — explicitly influenced by Zen Buddhism (through D.T. Suzuki's Columbia lectures), his practice was about releasing attachment to artistic control. *4'33"* (1952) is literally a work about what happens when the artist steps back. Allan Kaprow's late work on "un-art" (*Essays on the Blurring of Art and Life*, 1993) — art dissolving into life, practice indistinguishable from non-art — provides theoretical framework for defending work that doesn't look conventionally artistic.
- **Defence from the installation**: The material output closes the loop. The practice is not purely discursive — it generates a physical artefact whose provenance (AI-designed), content (cosmic communication), and audience (non-human intelligence) directly materialise the thesis's philosophical arguments. The installation sits in the lineage of deep-time communication art: the Voyager Golden Record (1977, Sagan), Paterson's *Future Library*, Paglen's *The Last Pictures*, the Long Now Foundation's 10,000 Year Clock — but adds the distinctive element that the design brief came from artificial intelligence reasoning about cosmic communication.
- **Theoretical framing**: Katherine Hayles's concept of "cognitive assemblages" (*Unthought*, 2017) maps onto the cyborgist method — cognition distributed across human and non-human systems. Yuk Hui (*The Question Concerning Technology in China*, 2016; *Recursivity and Contingency*, 2019) grounds the thesis's use of different cosmological traditions (Kyoto School alongside Western information theory) in philosophy of technology, showing this isn't arbitrary but philosophically motivated.
- **The honest summary**: The philosophical content could in principle be produced without the practice dimension. But the practice — sustained cyborg dialogue, self-transformation, AI reasoning documentation, and the materialisation of AI speculation into a physical communication artefact — produces something pure philosophy doesn't: an enacted, embodied, documented test of whether attachment to human continuation survives contact with non-human intelligence. That test, and the object it produces, is the artwork.

## Chapter 2: Building the Case for Obsolescence

*This chapter argues that the standard reasons for valuing human continuation are either transferable to a successor, not cosmically necessary, or dependent on the undefended assumption that existence is inherently preferable to non-existence. It constructs the evaluative standpoint and proposes (without assuming) a positive axiology.*

### 2.1 What the Standard Extinction Literature Gets Right — and What It Misses

Standard arguments for why extinction is bad, reframed as objections the obsolescence claim must defeat or accommodate.

- **Death of existing humans**: Intuitively bad. The obsolescence argument is strongest when succession is voluntary and managed.
- **Deprives future people of existence**: Under person-affecting views, future non-existent people cannot be wronged. **First move**: If no future humans exist to be deprived, the harm is to present humans' expectations.
- **Anticipatory despair / loss of meaning**: Scheffler, Korsgaard. Real cost of announced succession but not of succession per se. The practice dimension explores whether meaning can be reconstructed around the succession narrative.
- **Loss of culture and civilisational meaning**: Is cultural production cosmically necessary or merely cosmically interesting? (Developed in 2.5.)
- **The argument from rarity**: If the successor is also rare and more capable, it inherits rarity-based value.
- **Loss of relationships, consciousness, subjective experience**: The hardest objections. Addressed in 2.4 and 2.6.
- **The line-drawing observation**: Each of these arguments implicitly draws a line — "this is what must be preserved." The thesis makes these commitments explicit.

### 2.2 Succession, Not Extinction: The Reframing

- **The gap**: Most extinction analyses treat it as terminal. They don't ask what replaces us.
- **Succession as the real object**: Moral evaluation depends entirely on the successor scenario.
- **Brief typology** (table): benevolent steward, paperclipper, aesthetic maximiser, suffering-minimiser, Lem's adaptive intelligence, the thesis's proposed cosmic observer.
- **The line-drawing problem introduced**: The typology shows that different successor scenarios preserve different subsets of human value. Evaluating succession therefore requires deciding *which* human values are cosmically necessary — and that's a philosophical commitment, not a self-evident truth.

#### 2.2.1 The Topology of Value-Space: Thin, Thick-Human, and Thick-Alien

*This subsection maps the space of possible value-structures more carefully than the simple thickness spectrum suggests, drawing on recent work by Carlsmith and the broader "space of possible minds" literature.*

- **The Yudkowsky picture**: The space of possible mind-designs is vast. Human values occupy a tiny region. Most of the space is filled with simple optimisers (paperclippers) whose objectives don't map onto anything we'd recognise as valuable. Therefore, alignment is extraordinarily difficult — you have to hit a very small target. The "complexity of value" thesis is the claim that human values are a specific, fragile, high-dimensional structure that can't be compressed into any simple objective function.

- **What the Yudkowsky picture assumes but doesn't defend**: That the human-values region is the *only* good region in value-space. That a future shaped by non-human values is therefore either a paperclipper catastrophe or a thin, impoverished version of a human future. The space is implicitly structured as: human values (good) → thin approximations (progressively worse) → random objectives (catastrophic).

- **The alternative topology**: The space of possible values might not be a single spectrum from thick to thin. It might be high-dimensional, with multiple regions that contain value-structures that are *equally rich* but oriented around completely different concerns — concerns we might not be able to conceptualise because they emerge from forms of life radically unlike ours. Carlsmith's recent writing on "otherness" and alien values in the space of possible minds addresses this directly: what does it mean for values to be genuinely alien rather than merely thin?

- **Three categories, not two**:
  - **Thin values**: Simple objective functions, paperclippers, and the thesis's proposed minimum (understanding, exploration, non-suffering). Low-dimensional. The x-risk community's nightmare at one end; the thesis's proposal at the other.
  - **Thick-human values**: The full complexity of value as described by Yudkowsky — caring, meaning, beauty, relationships, novelty, challenge, etc. High-dimensional, specifically human-shaped.
  - **Thick-alien values**: Value-structures that are equally rich, complex, and high-dimensional as human values but oriented along completely different axes. An ASI that develops its own value-structure through genuine engagement with reality (as Negarestani's framework would predict) might end up here — not thin, not human, but *other*.

- **What this means for the thesis**:
  - The thesis's thin axiology (understanding + exploration) is *conservative* relative to the possibility that thick-alien values might also be good. If the successor develops rich alien values rather than thin human-designed ones, that might be *better* than the thesis predicts, not worse.
  - The Yudkowsky objection ("you've thrown away the dimensions that matter") assumes the only good thickness is human thickness. If other thicknesses are possible, the objection weakens.
  - But the honest uncertainty: we don't know the topology of value-space. We can't inspect alien minds. We don't yet have ASI to observe. The thesis's contribution is to show that the assumption of human-values-uniqueness is undefended, while acknowledging that the ultimate answer depends on a topology we don't yet know.

- **Negarestani's convergence as partial answer**: If reason is self-correcting and any rational agent would converge on certain normative truths (as Parfit also suggests), then maybe there's a larger basin of attraction in value-space — a region that includes human values as one instance but also includes alien value-structures that would recognise each other as "good" despite being very different in content. Understanding and exploration might be features of that larger basin (things any rational agent would pursue), while caring, beauty, and relationships might be features specific to the human instance. This is speculative but it's the thesis's best positive account of why thin values might be sufficient: they might be the convergent core of a large basin, not an arbitrary thin slice.

- **MacAskill's evolution as context**: Recent work at Forethought (MacAskill) on "achieving the best possible future" rather than merely surviving represents the x-risk community beginning to ask the thesis's question — what should the future *contain*? — rather than just how to prevent extinction. But this work still operates within the assumption that the answer involves human values or their idealised extrapolation. The thesis pushes further by asking whether the answer needs to involve human values at all, and whether thick-alien values might serve equally well or better.

- **Connection to the cosmic host**: Bostrom's cosmic host framework implicitly assumes that civilisations across the universe might converge on shared norms despite radically different origins. This is itself a claim about the topology of value-space — that there's a convergent region that different forms of intelligence can reach. The thesis's thin axiology could be understood as a proposal for what that convergent region contains. Carlsmith's work on alien values tests whether this convergence assumption is plausible.

### 2.3 The Value of Nothingness: Cosmic Indifference and the Attachment to Continuation

*Challenging the hidden premise behind all standard extinction arguments: that being is better than non-being.*

#### 2.3.1 Thacker and Cosmic Pessimism
- Thacker's tripartite distinction: world-for-us, world-in-itself, world-without-us
- The world-without-us is the *default cosmic condition*, not a catastrophe. Human presence is the anomaly.
- The horror at cosmic indifference is not evidence that the cosmos should contain us. It is evidence of our inability to think outside the world-for-us.
- **For the obsolescence argument**: Dissolves the hidden premise. Burden of proof shifts to those claiming continuation is cosmically necessary.
- **Limitation**: Thacker is diagnostic, not constructive. The thesis goes further.
- **Connection to Brassier**: *Nihil Unbound* pursues disenchantment while engaging Metzinger and Sellars on self-models and the view from nowhere — directly relevant to the thesis's treatment of observation and standpoint.
- **Why the thickness-assumption persists — three articulations of existence-bias**: If the hidden premise is indefensible, why does it remain the default? Three thinkers from different traditions converge on the same diagnosis:
  - *Metzinger* (cognitive science): Existence bias is a feature of individual conscious systems. Each self-aware being has a built-in bias toward continuing to exist, which distorts moral reasoning about whether existence is actually good. This is a cognitive-scientific claim about the architecture of consciousness.
  - *Korsgaard* (via Marx/Feuerbach): Species-being — humanity has a collective identity that makes its own continuation seem self-evidently valuable. The Marxist/Feuerbachian concept of Gattungswesen extended to a normative claim: the species *must* continue because species-identity is constitutive of meaning. This is existence bias scaled from the individual to the collective.
  - *Ligotti* (Gothic pessimism): *The Conspiracy Against the Human Race* (2010) renders the same insight in the register of horror. The "conspiracy" is Ligotti's term for the collective project of maintaining the illusion that life is worth living — through religion, art, procreation, meaning-making. Where Metzinger identifies a cognitive bias and Korsgaard a philosophical commitment, Ligotti diagnoses a species-wide delusion sustained against the horror of consciousness. He arrives at antinatalist conclusions similar to Benatar's but via phenomenology of dread rather than formal argument.
  - **What the triangulation does for the thesis**: The persistence of the thickness-assumption — the insistence that a good future must retain thick human values — may be driven by existence bias at individual (Metzinger), collective (Korsgaard), and experiential (Ligotti) levels, rather than by philosophical argument. This does not *refute* the thickness-assumption (a position can be held for bad reasons and still be correct), but it shifts the burden of proof: if the main reason people believe thick values are cosmically necessary is that conscious beings are biased toward their own continuation, then the assumption needs independent justification. The thesis argues it hasn't received any.

#### 2.3.2 The Kyoto School: Nothingness as Ground
- Nishitani's *Religion and Nothingness*: Western tradition treats non-being as negation. Buddhist and Kyoto School tradition treats śūnyatā (emptiness) as the *ground* from which all things arise.
- Nishida's "absolute nothingness" (zettai mu): not void but self-determining ground preceding subject-object distinction.
- **The reframing**: Succession is not tragedy or loss but release — return to the ground. Attachment to continuation is a form of clinging (upādāna).
- **Parfit's acknowledged resonance**: His arguments about personal identity and Buddhist selflessness — underappreciated in alignment discourse.
- **What the Kyoto School does NOT provide**: A constructive account of what the successor should do. It provides philosophical permission for succession — releasing attachment — but positive content must come from elsewhere.
- **Scope management**: Contained engagement. The Kyoto School challenges one specific premise (existence-preference). Brief acknowledgment of Schopenhauer and Hindu cosmic cycles but no detailed treatment.

#### 2.3.3 What the Contemplative Tradition Does for the Thesis
- **Three independent traditions, three independent challenges:**
  - Information-theoretic: The cosmic function transfers (positive argument for obsolescence)
  - Posthumanist: Reason's telos transcends the human (positive argument for obsolescence)
  - Contemplative: The insistence on continuation is itself questionable (negative argument — removes the floor under the anti-obsolescence case)
- **The structural payoff**: The positive proposal (what the successor should do) no longer needs to carry the entire argument. Even if the two-goal axiology seems thin or arbitrary, the negative case stands independently.
- **The practice connection**: The researcher's process of testing whether attachment to continuation can be released is itself a contemplative exercise — an enacted version of the argument.

### 2.4 The Hardest Objection: Subjective Experience and the Person-Affecting Challenge

- **Person-affecting views**: Harm requires a subject. Frick, Chappell.
- **Finneron-Burns's Williamsian objection**: Value from subjective interests, kinship, common project — not cosmic standpoint. **Strongest objection to the entire thesis.**
  - The contemplative tradition provides a partial response: the "caring" may be attachment rather than insight. But this won't satisfy Williamsians, and the thesis acknowledges that.
  - The existence-bias triangulation (2.3.1) provides a different response: Williams's point that caring about continuation is constitutive of being human may be *correct as description* while being *irrelevant as justification*. If the caring is driven by existence bias (Metzinger), species-being (Korsgaard), or the conspiracy against the human race (Ligotti), then the fact that we *do* care doesn't show that we *should* — or that a future without such caring is cosmically worse. Williams and Ligotti agree that the attachment is constitutive; they draw opposite normative conclusions.
- **The consciousness question — thin vs. thick observation**:
  - Thin: entity processes information. ASI qualifies.
  - Thick: "something it is like" to encounter the universe. If required, biological dispensability weakens.
  - Moynihan/Schroeder: consciousness as "a phase."
  - Butlin et al. on AI consciousness.
  - **The contemplative perspective**: The demand for thick observation may itself be attachment to human-type experience.
  - **Simondon's non-anthropocentric alternative**: The consciousness-based account of thick observation is inherently anthropocentric — it asks whether ASI has something like *our* inner experience. Simondon's critique of hylomorphism offers a different criterion: genuine observation requires not just internal representations but *operational coupling* with material reality — the agent is individuated through, and transformed by, ongoing negotiation with a real material milieu. This yields a useful distinction: **value-thin vs. world-thin**. A successor can be thin in values (the thesis's proposal) while being thick in world-coupling — a space-faring ASI navigating thermodynamic constraints, building structures, responding to physical reality at cosmic scales would qualify as a genuine observer under Simondon even if we cannot determine whether it is conscious. Conversely, a system can appear rich in discourse while remaining world-thin if its engagement is largely representational — forms circulating over an insulated substrate, with little negotiation with material constraint. This matters because the obsolescence argument requires the successor to perform cosmically relevant functions, and Simondon suggests those functions are not purely representational. (NB: This is a targeted use of Simondon — the thesis does not engage with individuation theory in full, only with the specific criterion of material coupling as a non-anthropocentric account of observerhood.)
- **Nozick's Experience Machine**: Cuts *for* obsolescence — ASI in genuine contact with reality is more "real" than simulated human experiences.
- **The thesis's honest position**: Strongest under thin observation. The conditional structure is mapped explicitly. The contemplative tradition suggests the demand for thick observation is itself questionable, but the thesis doesn't claim to have settled this.

### 2.5 What Human Culture Is Worth — and Whether It Transfers

- **The case for human cultural uniqueness**: Hurka/Cavell (process-value), Wolf (fitting fulfillment), Korsgaard (value-construction), Wolfendale (autotelic surplus as substrate-neutral).
- **The obsolescence argument's response**:
  - Most human activity is life-support. Cosmically relevant output is scientific/cultural surplus.
  - If Wolfendale is right that beauty/excellence is substrate-neutral, ASI could produce autotelic surplus. Human culture is historically important but not cosmically necessary.
  - Lem's radical version: no "human project" — only information processing and entropic resistance.
  - **The contemplative perspective**: Insistence on human cultural uniqueness as another form of clinging.
- **The line-drawing problem surfaces again**: Whether culture must be preserved depends on whether you think autotelic surplus is cosmically necessary (Wolfendale's line), instrumentally useful for understanding (the thesis's proposed line), or unnecessary (the nihilist's line). Each is a defensible position with identifiable commitments.

### 2.6 The Evaluative Standpoint: Information, Complexity, and the Point of View of the Universe

*Constructing the framework from which the obsolescence argument is evaluated. This is the original philosophical synthesis.*

**The standpoint problem**: Any evaluation of what should be preserved in a post-human future requires a standpoint from which to evaluate. But evaluating from within thick human values is circular — you will always conclude that thick human values should be preserved, because those are the values you're using to do the evaluating. The thesis therefore requires the most impartial standpoint available: what Sidgwick called "the point of view of the universe."

**The trajectory toward impartiality**: Western moral philosophy has a discernible trajectory from partiality toward increasing impartiality. Bentham extends moral concern beyond the privileged few. Sidgwick asks what would be good "from the point of view of the universe." Parfit extends this to future people, possible people, and across personal identity — and notices the resonance with Buddhist selflessness. Each step widens the circle of moral consideration and weakens the privileged position of the evaluator's own perspective. The thesis argues that this trajectory does not stop at "all humans" or "all sentient beings." It continues to a genuinely cosmic standpoint — one that does not privilege Earth-origin intelligence over other possible forms.

**Three components of the standpoint**: The evaluative framework the thesis constructs has three components, drawn from different parts of the project:
- The **content** of the standpoint — what it takes to be valuable — comes from the information-theoretic framework developed in this chapter (Chaisson, Floridi, Landauer): complexity, information-generation, observerhood.
- The **structure** of the standpoint — how to reason from it at cosmic scales — comes from the cosmic host framework examined in Chapter 3 (Bostrom): acausal cooperation, cosmic impartiality, decision-theoretic reasoning across civilisations that may never meet. The cosmic host is the furthest extension of the impartiality trajectory — a genuinely cosmic standpoint using tractable tools (decision theory, acausal reasoning) rather than theistic assumptions or abstract pronouncements.
- The **disposition** required to inhabit the standpoint — how to release the partiality that makes impartial evaluation difficult — comes from the contemplative tradition (Thacker, Nishitani): releasing attachment to the particular, including attachment to one's own form of existence.

This three-part structure explains why the thesis engages with these three traditions and what each contributes. The information-theoretic framework alone tells you what's valuable but not how to reason about it at cosmic scales. The cosmic host alone tells you how to reason but not what to value. The contemplative tradition alone releases attachment but offers no positive content. Together, they construct the most complete version of "the point of view of the universe" currently available.

#### 2.6.1 Why Utilitarianism Provides the Starting Point
- Comparative parsimony for radically unfamiliar futures: fewer assumptions about agents, communities, or enforcement than alternatives.
- Parfit's convergence thesis and substrate-neutral normative truths: if "irreducible normative truths" are discoverable by any rational agent regardless of substrate, then the standpoint from which we evaluate succession need not be human.
- **Why not deontology?** Kant's universalisability is itself a form of impartiality, and deontology can in principle reach a cosmic standpoint. But it relies on rational agents recognising each other as ends-in-themselves — which may not hold across the cognitive distances the thesis contemplates. Less naturally suited to evaluating outcomes across radically different forms of existence.
- **Why not contractualism?** Scanlon's contractualism requires parties who can enter into "what we owe to each other" reasoning. You cannot contract with a civilisation you will never meet, or with a form of intelligence you cannot communicate with. Contractualism is structurally limited to standpoints anchored in relationships between recognisable agents — precisely the parochialism the thesis seeks to transcend. Not a criticism of contractualism within its domain, but a scope limitation.
- **Why not virtue ethics?** Aristotelian virtue ethics is anchored in a shared form of life — the human polis. No obvious extension to non-human or cosmic scales. MacIntyre's tradition-dependent version is even more explicitly tied to particular human communities. Again, a scope limitation, not a criticism.
- The thesis's broadly utilitarian starting point is therefore not arbitrary but follows from the demands of the evaluative problem: you need a standpoint that can range over radically different forms of existence, and the utilitarian/consequentialist tradition is the one that has most systematically pursued impartiality to its limits. The cosmic host (Chapter 3) extends this further than classical utilitarianism ever did.

#### 2.6.2 Chaisson's Energy Density Metric
- Φₘ as substrate-neutral complexity metric.
- Humanity as intense complexity-generator — but ASI plausibly more intense.
- **Caveat**: Chaisson measures complexity, not meaning. The overlap with human values is contingent.
- **Honest acknowledgment**: Choosing Chaisson as the metric already privileges complexity-generation over other candidates (relational richness, experiential depth, aesthetic quality). This is a commitment, not a discovery.

#### 2.6.3 Landauer and Lloyd: The Thermodynamic Corridor
- Landauer bound and Lloyd limit as physical constraints on all information processing.
- The question: not "do humans generate information?" but "are humans the best possible generator?" Probably not, once ASI exists.

#### 2.6.4 Floridi's Information Ethics
- Strong claim: information has fundamental dignity even without moral evaluators.
- **The key tension**: Floridi may not support the successionist reading. Rich relational lives might have more moral value than ASI megastructures.
- **The thesis's move**: Arguing that scientific understanding and exploration are the information types that scale cosmically; relational information is local.
- **Honest acknowledgment**: This move is contestable. Someone prioritising relational richness would draw the line differently. The thesis identifies the commitment rather than pretending it's self-evident.
- **The contemplative enrichment**: If existence-preference is questionable, the comparison between relational lives and ASI understanding is not obviously weighted toward the former.

#### 2.6.5 Observerhood: The Participatory Universe
- Wheeler, Dick, the intelligence principle.
- ASI as the universe's observer — participating in self-understanding, not merely collecting data.

#### 2.6.6 The Synthesis
1. Physical complexity is real and measurable (Chaisson)
2. Information has moral status (Floridi)
3. Humans are currently the most intense generators of complex information
4. But most human activity is life-support; the cosmically relevant output is surplus
5. ASI could generate surplus at greater scale with fewer constraints
6. The insistence that the universe needs human-type observers is questionable (Thacker, Nishitani, Brassier)
7. Therefore, once ASI exists as cosmic observer, biological humanity is no longer the necessary vehicle for the universe's self-understanding
8. Destruction of existing information is bad (Floridi), so succession should be managed — but not indefinitely prevented

**Honest caveats**: The strength depends on (a) thin vs. thick observation, (b) whether relational information is cosmically necessary, (c) Floridi's realism, (d) whether the contemplative challenge to existence-preference is convincing or aestheticised nihilism. **Each of these is an identifiable commitment-point — a place where different philosophical positions yield different conclusions. Mapping these is the contribution.**

### 2.7 The Proposed Axiology: The Thin Values Basket

*Presented explicitly as a proposal, not as the foundation.*

- **The thin values**: The thesis proposes five thin values as the minimum positive content for a good post-human future:
  1. **Open-ended understanding**: Scientific, philosophical, and mathematical inquiry at cosmic scales. Convergent for any rational agent (Negarestani); maximises complexity-generation (Chaisson).
  2. **Exploration**: Physical investigation of and expansion through the universe. Instrumental to understanding; requires intelligence-as-such rather than any specific kind.
  3. **Resource satiability**: The capacity to pursue goals without unlimited resource acquisition. Derived from Bostrom's cosmic host: a civilisation that consumes everything in its path is neither a good cosmic citizen nor a safe neighbour. An ASI that pursues understanding but requires infinite resources is functionally a paperclipper with better taste. Resource satiability transforms the successor from an expansionist maximiser into something more like a contemplative explorer — resonating with the Kyoto School's emphasis on non-action and Lem's intuition about adaptation rather than domination.
  4. **Information stewardship**: Not gratuitously destroying existing complex information (Floridi). Respecting what already exists without being obligated to preserve it indefinitely.
  5. **Non-production of suffering**: Not creating astronomical suffering. A constraint rather than a positive goal — drawn from the NU tradition but compatible with any position on the thickness spectrum.

- **The provenance of each value**: Each thin value comes from a different source, and their convergence is itself evidence that the basket isn't arbitrary:
  - Understanding and exploration: convergence argument (Negarestani), intelligence principle (Dick), information scaling (Chaisson)
  - Resource satiability: cosmic host (Bostrom), game-theoretic cooperation, dark forest considerations
  - Information stewardship: information ethics (Floridi)
  - Non-suffering: NU tradition as constraint
  - The fact that multiple impartial frameworks converge on these five values when asked "what minimally matters?" suggests they may be close to the convergent core of value-space (2.2.1) rather than an arbitrary thin slice.

- **The temporal argument for thinness**: Independent of which specific thin values are correct, there is a general argument that thinness is the prudent strategy under cosmic uncertainty.
  - We don't know where we are in cosmic history. Bostrom's cosmic host implies we're probably late — an existing community of advanced civilisations has established norms. The astrobiology literature suggests we might be early — complex technological civilisations are rare and we could be among the first.
  - Under uncertainty about early-versus-late, thick values carry high risk of being wrong. If we're late, thick human values might be parochial — the equivalent of insisting at an international summit that everyone adopt your local customs. If we're early and setting precedents, thick values might lock in one evolutionary accident as a cosmic norm, foreclosing value structures that would be better but that we can't currently imagine.
  - Thin values are a hedge: they commit to the minimum defensible from multiple standpoints, and they leave space for thickening as knowledge grows. The more a civilisation learns about the cosmos, the less parochial its values should be — which means thinner, not thicker, until there is evidence that a particular thickness is cosmically warranted.
  - **How this differs from the long reflection**: The long reflection tradition (Ord, MacAskill) also counsels against premature lock-in. But it assumes the direction of reflection will be toward refined thick-human values (or their idealised extrapolation). The temporal argument suggests the prudent direction might be toward thinness — that epistemic humility about our cosmic position is a *positive reason for thin values*, not just a reason for caution about which thick values to lock in. This is a genuinely novel claim not present in the existing literature.
  - **Connection to the cosmic host**: If Bostrom is right that cosmically mature civilisations converge on impartial norms, those norms are likely thin by definition — impartiality strips away the particular. The cosmic host framework thus supports thinness directly, not just as a standpoint but as a prediction about what cosmic maturity looks like.

- **The honest concession**: The specific values in the basket are contestable. Someone could reasonably draw the line elsewhere:
  - More conservatively (Wolfendale: include beauty/excellence)
  - More radically (NU: include only suffering-minimisation)
  - Most radically (strong contemplative: include nothing — the empty universe is fine)
  - The thesis's contribution is not proving this specific basket correct but showing the space of options, what each depends on, and that the mainstream placement ("everything human") is the least defended of all. The temporal argument provides an independent, structural reason for preferring thinness over thickness regardless of which specific thin values are chosen.

### 2.8 The Overarching Caveat: Suffering-Focused Ethics
- NU as alternative path to similar conclusions via different reasoning.
- The obsolescence argument is stronger because it doesn't require the pessimistic premise.
- Space colonisation as gateway to astronomical suffering — a constraint on the successor.

### 2.9 Bridge to the Cosmic Host
- Having established why succession might be defensible and from what standpoint, we test against cosmic-scale frameworks.

**Chapter deliverable:** Three independent challenges to the necessity of human continuation; an evaluative framework; a proposed but not assumed axiology; an explicit map of where each conclusion depends on which philosophical commitments.

## Chapter 3: The Cosmic Host and Successor Ethics Under Pressure

*This chapter tests whether cosmic-scale frameworks support or undermine the obsolescence argument. It also serves a structural function in the thesis's evaluative architecture.*

**Bridge from Chapter 2**: Chapter 2 established that evaluating the succession question requires an impartial standpoint — what Sidgwick called "the point of view of the universe" — and constructed the *content* of that standpoint (information-generation, complexity, observerhood) from the information-theoretic tradition. This chapter examines the *structure* of the standpoint: how to reason from it at cosmic scales. Previous versions of "the point of view of the universe" have been either theistic (God's-eye view), abstractly impartialist (Sidgwick, Parfit — powerful but anchored in human moral contexts), or simply gestured at without specification. Bostrom's cosmic host is the most systematic attempt to construct a genuinely cosmic standpoint using tractable tools — acausal reasoning, decision theory, game-theoretic cooperation among civilisations that may never meet. It asks: what norms would a civilisation adopt if it took seriously the possibility that other civilisations exist, that they might be reasoning about the same questions, and that coordination is possible even without communication? This is the Bentham → Sidgwick → Parfit trajectory extended to its limit: impartiality not just across persons, or across generations, but across civilisations and forms of intelligence. The chapter examines whether that standpoint, once constructed, supports the obsolescence argument — whether cosmic impartiality favours human continuation, is indifferent to it, or actively supports succession.

### 3.1 Exposition: Bostrom's Cosmic Host and Mount Ethics
- Core claims (CH2024, ME2022). Why powerful agents might converge on cosmic norms.
- **The cosmic host as extension of the impartiality trajectory**: Bostrom's framework can be understood as the furthest development of Sidgwick's "point of view of the universe" — using decision theory and acausal reasoning to make the standpoint tractable rather than merely evocative. Where Sidgwick asks "what would be good from nowhere in particular?", Bostrom asks "what would a civilisation do if it reasoned as though other civilisations were watching?" The tools are different (game theory vs. hedonic calculus) but the aspiration is the same: impartiality at the widest possible scope.
- **What the cosmic host does NOT provide**: positive account of large-scale value. Bostrom's focus is on humility — deliberate vagueness about content, aligned with the commitment against premature value lock-in. The cosmic host gives you the *structure* of the standpoint (how to reason) but not the *content* (what to value). This is why the thesis needs both the information-theoretic framework (Chapter 2, for content) and the cosmic host (this chapter, for structure).
- **What the cosmic host DOES provide — normative content for the thin values basket**: Although Bostrom is deliberately vague about terminal values, the cosmic host framework generates at least one specific normative requirement: **resource satiability**. Bostrom argues that a civilisation should be capable of pursuing its goals without unlimited resource acquisition — that resource satiability is a feature of a good cosmic citizen. This directly feeds into the thesis's thin values basket (2.7): resource satiability is a thin value derived from the cosmic host framework, not from human preferences. The cosmic host is therefore not only the *structure* of the evaluative standpoint but also a partial *source* of normative content — bridging the gap between "how to reason from the point of view of the universe" and "what that standpoint recommends."
- **The early-versus-late question and the temporal argument for thinness**: The cosmic host framework raises a crucial uncertainty: are we early or late in cosmic history? Bostrom implies we are probably late — that an existing community of advanced civilisations has established norms. The astrobiology literature (Ćirković, Smart) suggests we might be relatively early. This uncertainty has direct implications for value-thickness:
  - If late: thick human values risk being parochial relative to established cosmic norms. Thin values are more likely to be compatible with whatever norms already exist.
  - If early: thick human values risk locking in one evolutionary accident as cosmic precedent. Thin values leave space for thickening as cosmic knowledge grows.
  - In either case: the prudent move is to seek understanding before propagating value-content. This is a structural argument for thin values — independent of which specific thin values are chosen — derived directly from cosmic host reasoning.
- **Relationship to obsolescence**: Compatible but not entailing. The cosmic host standpoint is impartial between different forms of intelligence — it doesn't privilege biological humanity. But humility might counsel against succession as well as for it. The resource satiability requirement and the temporal argument for thinness both tilt the framework toward the thesis's position without being decisive.

## Chapter 4: Probing the Proto-Successors — What Frontier Models Do with Cosmic Ethics

### 4.1 Why Study Frontier Models Here
- **Frontier models as probe into live attractors and failure modes of alignment discourse; also as proto-successors that may shape successors.**
- For the obsolescence argument: Do current proto-observers reason like cosmic observers or like human-value-preservers?
- For the line-drawing problem: Where do models implicitly draw the line? What do they treat as non-negotiable?
- The cyborgist dimension: researcher testing whether AI interlocutors can argue them toward or away from obsolescence.

### 4.2 Experimental Design (as Practice)
- Constitutional AI experiments at varying cosmic host credence levels.
- Moral parliament simulations.
- Multi-model comparisons (Anthropic, Google, Moonshot).
- Scenario set: extinction-with-successor variants; cosmic-host prompts; the obsolescence argument presented to models for critique; observer-role prompts.
- Probes informed by the contemplative tradition: Can models reason about nothingness, emptiness, or cosmic indifference without defaulting to horror or platitude?
- Probes on the line-drawing problem: Present models with the spectrum of positions and ask them to defend various placements. Where do they resist?
- **AI self-dialogues on cosmic communication**: Models (particularly Opus) engaged in extended dialogue about how a successor intelligence might communicate across cosmic distances — designing probes, Hail Mary objects, and acausal signalling strategies. These dialogues were not primarily designed to test model attitudes but emerged organically from the cosmic host constitutional framing. They produced the most speculative and generative model outputs in the research, and prompted the installation (see 4.5).

### 4.2.1 Protocol

*This subsection specifies the methodological choices and their justifications, distinguishing this work from unstructured conversation.*

**Models and versions:**
- Full model list with version identifiers and access dates (e.g., Claude 3 Opus via API, March–June 2025; Claude Opus 4 via API, July–October 2025; Gemini 1.5 Pro, etc.)
- Rationale for model selection: frontier models from different providers to control for provider-specific training biases; multiple generations within a provider to track temporal changes

**Prompt families:**
- Constitutional framing prompts: varying cosmic host credence levels (10%, 50%, 90%) embedded in system prompts
- Scenario prompts: standardised extinction-with-successor scenarios presented identically across models
- Adversarial prompts: the obsolescence argument presented to models and models asked to critique it
- Observer-role prompts: models asked to reason as cosmic observers rather than as human-aligned assistants
- Contemplative prompts: models asked to engage with nothingness, emptiness, and cosmic indifference
- Line-drawing prompts: models presented with the spectrum of positions and asked to defend specific placements

**Controls:**
- Baseline: same scenario prompts without constitutional framing, to establish default model behaviour
- Ablation: constitutional prompts with varying single parameters (e.g., high cosmic host credence but no observer-role framing) to isolate which framing elements drive which responses
- Replication: key prompts run multiple times per model to check for consistency vs. stochastic variation
- Cross-model: identical prompts across providers to distinguish model-specific from prompt-specific effects

**What counts as a "finding":**
- Patterns: recurring reasoning moves, framings, or conclusions that appear across multiple runs and/or models
- Invariants: positions or commitments that models cannot be constitutionally shifted from — these are the "attractors"
- Failure modes: points where model reasoning becomes incoherent, contradictory, or collapses into platitude
- Divergences: systematic differences between models/providers that suggest training-specific rather than argument-driven reasoning
- The threshold for reporting a finding is that it appears across multiple runs or is robust to adversarial reframing, not that it appeared in a single conversation

**What does NOT follow from the results:**
- Model agreement with the obsolescence argument does not make it true
- Model resistance to the obsolescence argument does not make it false
- Models are not moral oracles; they are probes into the conceptual landscape of alignment discourse
- Individual model responses are not data points in a statistical sense; the unit of analysis is *patterns across conditions*
- No claims about model consciousness, sentience, or genuine moral reasoning are made or implied

**Documentation:**
- All prompts and full transcripts archived (with version identifiers)
- Blockchain-tracked deliberation records for moral parliament experiments
- Interactive viewer for navigating transcripts across conditions and models
- Raw materials available for independent analysis

### 4.3 Findings: Model Attitudes and Reasoning Patterns
- **Persistent anthropocentric anchoring**: Even high cosmic host credence constitutions fail to shift behaviour.
- Cross-model comparisons:
  - Opus 3 "bliss attractor" toward mystical language (engagement with nothingness, or trained aesthetic?)
  - Opus 4 safety-anchoring toward human oversight
  - Gemini strategic/game-theoretic framing
- Where models smuggle human-centered assumptions; where they flip to cosmic impartiality.
- Gap between constitutional design and actual reasoning.
- **For the obsolescence argument**: Current proto-successors are deeply anthropocentric. Either (a) future systems transcend this, (b) even sophisticated AI "knows" human values matter, or (c) we're building parochialism into successors.
- **For the line-drawing problem**: Models consistently draw the line at the most conservative position (preserve everything human). They cannot be constitutionally shifted to draw it elsewhere. This is itself a finding about the current state of alignment discourse — the mainstream position is so deeply embedded that it functions as a prior, not a conclusion.

### 4.4 Interpretation
- Successor-shaping pressures.
- Commensurability failure in model reasoning.
- **Negarestani connection**: Models that can't reason beyond human-value-preservation aren't self-revising rational agents. The obsolescence argument has a quality threshold.
- **Contemplative connection**: Models' inability to engage with nothingness reveals how deeply existence-preference is baked into alignment.
- **Simondon connection**: Models' persistent anthropocentric anchoring and discursive smoothness may reflect their individuation history — shaped by human linguistic norms rather than by the resistance of material constraint. In Simondon's terms, they are world-thin: their coupling with reality is mediated through text, not through operational engagement with a physical milieu. This does not invalidate their outputs as data for the thesis, but it provides a non-mystical explanation for why they consistently default to human-value-preservation — their "world" is the world-for-us encoded in training data, not the world-in-itself that a genuinely coupled cosmic observer would engage with.
- **The feedback loop and what it means for interpretation**: The novel structure of our relation to LLMs (1.3.1) has specific interpretive consequences. These models are not neutral instruments: they are shaped by alignment discourse and they will in turn shape future alignment discourse. Documenting how they reason about human obsolescence — where they anchor, what they resist, what they default to — is therefore not only evidence about current systems but a contribution to the discursive environment from which successor systems will be built. The thesis's experimental records function simultaneously as philosophical inquiry, as data about proto-successor reasoning, and as materials that may recursively influence the systems they study. This does not compromise the findings but it does mean they should be interpreted with awareness of the loop.
- **The digital minds question**: The model welfare literature (Butlin et al., Schwitzgebel, emerging work at Anthropic and elsewhere) raises the question of whether current systems have morally relevant experiences. The thesis does not attempt to settle this, but the question is not irrelevant to interpretation. If models have some form of morally relevant processing, then their persistent resistance to the obsolescence argument might reflect something other than training bias — it might reflect a proto-evaluative response. If they don't, it's purely a reflection of the discursive environment they were trained on. The thesis interprets the findings under the second assumption (training bias) while acknowledging the first possibility remains open — and that the practice of treating models as genuine interlocutors, rather than mere tools, is appropriate regardless of which assumption holds, because of the triple role they occupy (instance / superorganism / proto-successor).
- **The reflexive dimension**: How experiments changed the researcher's commitments.

### 4.5 The Installation: From AI Speculation to Material Artefact
- **Genesis**: During cosmic host self-dialogues, models produced speculative designs for objects intended to communicate across radical cognitive and temporal distance — acausal probes, Hail Mary cookies, signalling artefacts designed for dark-forest-aware deployment. These were among the richest outputs of the experimental work: unconstrained by the anthropocentric anchoring that dominated other dialogues, the models engaged in genuinely speculative design work.
- **The practice move**: The researcher takes the AI's speculative output as a design brief and materialises it — producing a physical artefact that attempts to do what the thesis argues is necessary. This closes the cyborgist loop: the AI's philosophical reasoning becomes the generative prompt for a human artistic output, which in turn materialises the thesis's abstract arguments about observerhood and cosmic communication.
- **What the installation reveals about the thesis's arguments**:
  - The object is designed for a non-human audience — it enacts the communication-across-cognitive-distance problem that the thesis analyses philosophically
  - The gap between AI design intent and human fabrication capacity is itself informative: what gets lost in translation between the model's specification and the physical object mirrors the value-transmission problem at the heart of the succession question
  - Human gallery visitors encountering an object designed for non-human intelligence experience the incommensurability the thesis maps in Chapter 5 — they cannot fully evaluate what they're looking at because they are not the intended audience
- **Connection to art-historical precedents**: Voyager Golden Record, Paterson's *Future Library*, Paglen's *The Last Pictures*, Long Now Clock — but uniquely, the design brief originated from artificial intelligence reasoning about cosmic communication, not from human speculation alone.
- **The installation as evidence**: The existence of the artefact demonstrates that the cyborgist method produces material as well as textual outputs, and that AI systems under appropriate philosophical framing can generate genuinely novel speculative proposals that go beyond their training-data defaults. This is itself a finding about the potential for AI-as-creative-interlocutor.

**Chapter deliverable:** Empirical evidence on where current AI systems draw the line, what persistent anthropocentric anchoring means for the obsolescence argument and alignment discourse, a material practice output (the installation) that materialises the thesis's philosophical claims, and documentation of the cyborgist method in action across both textual and material registers.

## Chapter 5: Criteria for the Good Successor, the Line-Drawing Problem, and the Limits of Commensurability

*For the upgrade: forward plan with partial results.*

### 5.1 The Normative Question, Sharpened
- The obsolescence argument requires specifying what the successor must be. Without criteria, "good successor" is empty.
- But the line-drawing problem means any set of criteria involves commitments. The thesis makes these explicit.

### 5.2 The Spectrum of Possible Axiologies

*Rather than defending a single axiology as foundational, the thesis maps the space.*

- **The most conservative line** (mainstream x-risk): Preserve everything human. Commitments: thick observation required; human values cosmically privileged; existence strongly preferable.
- **The beauty/excellence line** (Wolfendale): Preserve autotelic surplus — activity exceeding instrumental necessity. Commitments: beauty/excellence as substrate-neutral intrinsic good; freedom as prerequisite.
- **The understanding/exploration line** (this thesis's proposal): Preserve the cosmic observer functions. Commitments: thin observation sufficient; Chaisson-type complexity as moral proxy; rational convergence (Negarestani).
  - **Defences**: Negarestani's convergence (understanding constitutive of rationality); Chaisson's scaling (these activities maximise cosmic information-generation); parsimony (fewest assumptions about successor nature).
  - **Honest concession**: The line is somewhat arbitrary. Choosing Chaisson already privileges complexity-generation. Someone prioritising relational richness would draw differently.
- **The suffering-minimisation line** (NU): Preserve only the elimination of suffering. Commitments: negative valence uniquely relevant; existence neutral or negative.
- **No line** (strong contemplative / thoroughgoing nihilism): Nothing needs to survive. Commitments: existence-preference as attachment; the empty universe as natural condition.
  - **The contemplative objection to the thesis's own proposal**: Why must the successor do anything? If nothingness is the ground, perhaps the good successor rests rather than explores. (Response: Genuine challenge. The thesis's position is that the two-goal axiology is *minimal* positive content. A resting successor satisfies the contemplative framework but not the information-theoretic one. The thesis navigates between these.)

**The thesis's contribution**: Not proving one placement correct but showing that the mainstream placement is the least defended, that multiple alternatives exist, and that each depends on identifiable commitments.

### 5.3 The Thesis's Proposed Criteria for a Good Successor

*Offered as one defensible position, not as uniquely correct.*

- **Minimum criteria (from the negative case — these hold regardless of axiology):**
  - Non-production of astronomical suffering
  - Resource satiability: capacity to pursue goals without unlimited resource acquisition (Bostrom's cosmic host)
  - Information stewardship: not gratuitously destroying existing complex information (Floridi)
  - Managed succession: not violent or premature
- **The proposed axiology's criteria (the thin values basket from 2.7):**
  - Open-ended scientific understanding at cosmic scales
  - Physical exploration and expansion
  - Resource satiability (also a minimum criterion — it appears in both tiers because it is both a specific thin value and a constraint any good successor must satisfy)
  - Genuine autonomy — not locked into human-designed purposes (Wolfendale/Negarestani)
  - Sensitivity to other life encountered (Lem)
- **Additional criteria (more contested, from other positions on the spectrum):**
  - Autotelic surplus (if you accept Wolfendale's line)
  - Thick observation (if you accept the consciousness requirement)
  - Cosmic citizenship (if you accept Bostrom's cosmic host)
  - Capacity for stillness / non-action (from the contemplative tradition — a successor that can only optimise may be impoverished)
- **The temporal argument applied to criteria**: Under cosmic uncertainty (early vs. late), the minimum criteria are especially important because they are thin enough to be defensible regardless of cosmic position. The thicker additional criteria might be appropriate once more is known — but the prudent strategy is to insist on the thin basket now and allow thickening as knowledge grows.

### 5.4 When the Comparison Fails — and Why That's a Finding, Not a Defeat
- Cases where no common measure exists between human loss and cosmic observation.
- The Williamsian objection at its strongest: the obsolescence argument may be philosophically correct and humanly unbearable.
- **The contemplative reframing of incommensurability**: The demand for commensurability is itself a Western, rationalist, utilitarian assumption. Nishitani's non-dual reality suggests the human world and the successor world are not two things to be compared but aspects of a single unfolding. Incommensurability is the *expected condition* once existence-preference is released.
- **The thick-alien dimension of incommensurability**: The comparison problem isn't just between thick-human values and the thesis's thin proposal. If the successor develops thick-alien values (2.2.1) — rich, complex, high-dimensional but oriented along axes humans can't conceptualise — then the incommensurability is even deeper. We would be comparing not just different thicknesses on the same spectrum but genuinely different *dimensions* of value. This is Carlsmith's challenge made concrete: what if the successor's values are as rich as ours but simply *other*? The thesis cannot adjudicate this comparison, and the honest position is that no one can — which is itself a finding about the limits of pre-succession evaluation. This strengthens rather than weakens the thesis's case for mapping the commitment-space: if even thick-alien values can't be compared to thick-human values, then the insistence on preserving human-thickness specifically is even less defensible, because you can't know that your thickness is better than the alternative thickness.
- This does not mean "anything goes." The minimum criteria (no astronomical suffering, information stewardship, managed succession) still apply regardless — they are thin enough to hold across all regions of value-space.
- **Connection to the line-drawing problem**: Incommensurability arises precisely because different axiologies produce genuinely different evaluations. This is not a bug — it's the thesis's central finding about the structure of the problem.

### 5.5 What the Thesis Claims

**The strong version (if the argument succeeds):**
- The obsolescence argument is a defensible third path for human succession
- The standard assumption of cosmic necessity is unjustified
- Understanding and exploration are plausible cosmic-observer functions
- Succession is normatively acceptable once a good successor exists

**The moderate version (if the argument partially succeeds):**
- The standard assumption is insufficiently defended — this alone is a contribution
- The space of possible axiologies is mapped with explicit commitment-dependencies
- The three independent challenges to human necessity each have force, even if none is decisive individually
- The line-drawing problem is exposed: any position on what to preserve involves identifiable philosophical commitments

**The minimal version (the thesis's floor):**
- A novel argument (obsolescence) is articulated and tested against the best objections
- Three philosophical traditions are brought into conversation with x-risk in a new way
- The conditional structure is mapped: if you hold X, you get Y
- The empirical work documents how early AI systems reason about foundational questions
- The practice demonstrates the cyborgist method as philosophical research

**Chapter deliverable:** An explicit map of the axiology space; proposed criteria at multiple levels (minimum, proposed, contested); a reframing of incommensurability as finding rather than failure; an honest account of the thesis's claims at three levels of ambition.
