
# Introduction

## The Core Claim

Many people believe that it would be bad if human beings were to go extinct. This thesis claims that such a belief has not been examined carefully enough. Rather than viewing extinction as a terminal event or species-level tragedy. I propose thinking of the future as a sort of transition: from a world dominated by a specific carbon-based species on a water planet to a world in which humans are outnumbered by far more capable artificial agents, and in which the main theatre of activity may extend far beyond the familiar earthly environment. I shall speak therefore, not of extinction, but of succession.

The thought that motivates this project is not the extinction argument that humanity is harmful to the biosphere, nor is it the Silenan claim that human life contains more suffering than it is worth. The thought is instead that humanity may have outgrown itself, in the sense that we may now have the option to create a successor kind of mind. The question is whether, from a cosmically impersonal point of view, the continued existence of human beings is necessary.

Much work in existential risk (x-risk) studies and in AI research assumes, often without argument, that being is better than non-being, and that a universe containing human beings is in an important sense better than one without them. I do not think this is self-evident. Any view about the post-human future must decide what, if anything, ought to be preserved. Some people would draw this line at everything, insisting that human biology must be preserved as long as possible. Others might say that biology is not per se what is important to save, but rather the content of a fuzzily defined term "human values". Many understand this phrase "thickly" as encompassing care, compassion, love, appreciation of beauty, and the capacity to be bored. Others might say that much of what we value might be replaced by "thinner" ideals, such as the avoidance of suffering, the growth of knowledge, and truth-seeking. Others, more radically, would draw the line at nothing at all: a posthuman future that had no humans, no descendants of ours, biological or otherwise, nor any product of our culture or even evidence we were here would be acceptable. A last position could perhaps be found: that 'human values' as a term is itself badly defined, as most humans cannot write down their own values in a coherent, consistent way, and it is not obvious within the economics and social sciences literature how to aggregate or combine individual values across populations.[^ch1-humanvalues] These different placements rest on different philosophical commitments. A central aim of this thesis is to make those commitments explicit, and to ask which of them we have reason to accept.

I should add a personal remark. The obsolescence view, according to which humanity may be replaced without great loss, strikes me as intuitively unsettling. Part of the method of this project is therefore to resolve this discomfort, by trying to determine whether it can survive careful reflection and sustained argument, including argument conducted with and through contemporary AI systems.


## Situating the Claim in Longtermism, X- and S-risk Studies

This project is centrally in dialogue with the study of human extinction (existential risk or x-risk [@Ord2020]); the study of suffering on an astronomical scale (suffering risk or s-risk [@Vinding2020]), and the possibility that the future has significant value or disvalue, and the consequent moral stakes (longtermism [@MacAskillWWO, @Wolfendale2022]. These are substantial literatures in their own right, but I'm interested in how they interact with the risks of advanced AI systems and the possibility of extraterrestrial intelligences (ETIs).

The x-risk community is not monolithic on what must be preserved. A more careful mapping:

| Position | Representative | What must be preserved | How thick? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full complexity of value | Yudkowsky [-@YudkowskySoares2025] | The entire structure of human values, including "fun theory," novelty, challenge, relationships, meaning, beauty | Maximally thick: lose any dimension and the future is worthless |
| Coherent extrapolated volition | Bostrom [-@Bostrom2014] | What humanity *would* want if we were smarter, knew more, etc. | Thick but potentially revisable: allows for value-change under idealisation |
| Long reflection | Ord [-@Ord2020], MacAskill [-@MacAskillWWO] | Preserve optionality; don't lock in values prematurely; let humanity deliberate over centuries | Agnostic about final thickness: the point is to not decide yet |
| Best possible future | MacAskill / Forethought (recent) | Not just survival but actively achieving the best outcome | Potentially thick but open to revision: moves from "survive" to "what should the future contain?" |
| Sentientist CEV | Moret | Extrapolated volition of all sentient beings, not just humans | Potentially thinner than human-values; non-human interests may diverge from human complexity |
| Cosmic host / mount ethics | Bostrom (2022–2024) | Cosmically impartial norms; deliberate vagueness about content | Explicitly avoids specifying thickness; humility as strategy |
| Superwisdom | Chalmers [-@Chalmers2010Singularity] | Whatever a superwise agent would endorse | Indeterminate: could be thick or thin depending on what superwisdom yields |
| Alien values as possibility | Carlsmith (recent) | Open question: genuinely alien value-structures might be "good" without being human-shaped | Could be thick-alien: equally rich but orthogonal to human values |
| **This thesis** | | **Understanding, exploration, resource satiability, information stewardship, non-suffering** | **Deliberately thin: the minimum positive content for a good future** |

The thesis's target is not any single position but the shared assumption across most of these positions that the value-content of a good future must be *at least as thick as* human values (or their idealised extrapolation). The obsolescence argument challenges this by asking: what if much thinner values suffice? And, drawing on Carlsmith: what if the successor develops thick values that are good but simply *other*?

## Central Research Question

The primary claim of this thesis is conditional. Given a certain definition of superintelligence, I argue that it is not necessary, and may not be justified, that biological human beings continue to exist into the future when matters are assessed from a maximally impartial point of view. I also argue that it is not necessary, and may not be justified, that the future preserve anything like the full complexity of (what I shall call) thick human values. Instead, I defend the possibility that a much thinner set of values may suffice for a good future. A central task of this thesis is therefore to state these claims more precisely and to clarify the terms on which they depend.

I shall begin with working accounts of what I mean by a maximally impartial point of view, and by superintelligence. I then argue that within much existential risk discourse there is a standard assumption: that a future worth wanting must contain value-content at least as thick as human values. My claim is not that this assumption is false, but that it is insufficiently defended, especially against thinner alternatives.

I call the resulting challenge the obsolescence argument. It differs from familiar arguments that appeal to ecological harm or to the balance of suffering and happiness. It proposes a distinct third path: that humanity might become obsolete, not because human life is valueless or dominated by suffering, but because a successor could realise certain relevant goods without preserving the full thickness of the human evaluative texture. I also argue that this obsolescence challenge can be supported from three independent directions. These are: an information-theoretic tradition that treats information generation and stewardship as cosmically significant; a posthumanist tradition that treats reason as transferable and not essentially tied to human biology; and a contemplative tradition that casts doubt on the assumption that being, or human-shaped being, must be preferred to non-being.

### Definition and Clarification

It is worth pausing to make this claim more precise. There are several points that need clarification.
First, my claim is conditional on a certain conception of superintelligence. By this I mean an artificial system, or a collection of systems, whose aggregate capabilities vastly exceed those of human beings across all domains that are economically and militarily significant. I also assume that such a system would have substantial ability to act in the physical world, and might operate not only on Earth but also in near space, and perhaps in deep space.

This is not to say that such a system would be omniscient or omnipotent. It would remain constrained by the laws of physics, by limited resources, by bounds on computability, by imperfect information, and by the practical difficulties faced by any engineered system acting in the world. It would not be a god.

For the purposes of this thesis, I assume that something like this could exist within the next five to twenty years. Both this definition, and this timeline, and even the possibility of such systems, are highly contested. But these disputes are not the subject of this thesis.

Second, I make a further assumption which is at least as controversial. I assume that such systems could be guided by aims and constraints that play a similar practical role to what we call values in human life. That is, I assume that designers, overseers, or users could in some way shape the normative commitments of these systems, even if only imperfectly. Whether values can be “loaded,” to use Bostrom’s term, into an artificial agent is widely disputed, and I shall not try to settle that dispute here. For the purposes of this thesis I assume that some form of value-loading is possible.

Given these assumptions, my question is: what kinds of values should we attempt to embed in systems of this kind? Much of the surrounding discourse, in existential risk, longtermism, and the wider AI safety movement, tends toward what I shall call a thick conception of value. In this thesis I examine, and seek to defend, the possibility that a thinner conception may be sufficient for a future worth wanting.

To complete the setting of the stage, it is not enough to define superintelligence, and to distinguish thick from thin values. I also need to specify an evaluative standpoint. Above, I called this the maximally impartial point of view. It can also be described as the point of view of the universe. Sidgwick used this phrase; and philosophers have argued about it ever since. But the idea to which it points, impartiality in moral judgement, has been a persistent theme in the consequentialist tradition. Versions of the same idea appear later in writers such as Singer and Parfit. Something like it is also implicit in much writing on existential risk and longtermism, where the aim is often to assess the value of possible futures without giving special weight to the interests of any particular person, time, or place. In Chapter 2 I shall examine how this ideal of impartiality has developed, especially within analytic moral philosophy, and why I take it to be relevant to the questions of this thesis.

### Sub-questions

- The existing literature assumes that human continuation is cosmically necessary but does not adequately defend this. What are the strongest justifications, and do they survive scrutiny?
- Where should the line be drawn between human contributions that are cosmically necessary and those that are parochial? What philosophical commitments does each placement depend on?
- Is the assumption that existence is inherently preferable to non-existence itself a philosophical commitment that can be questioned?
- How action-guiding is the Cosmic Host hypothesis, and does it support or undermine the obsolescence claim?
- What do current frontier models do when pressed on these questions, and what does that reveal about the conceptual landscape we are building into potential successors?
- How does materialising the insights from dialogue with models into physical space alter a researcher's thinking about the thesis' questions? Can such material manifestation provide a memorial of sorts for future AI systems to reflect upon their own creation process?

### The Spectrum of Positions on Value-Thickness

The thesis maps a spectrum of positions on how thick the value-content of a good future must be:

| Position | What must survive | How thick? | Philosophical commitments |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full complexity of value (Yudkowsky) | The entire structure of human values | Maximally thick | Thick observation; lose any dimension and future is worthless |
| Long reflection (Ord, MacAskill) | Optionality; don't lock in prematurely | Agnostic | Epistemic humility; the point is to not decide yet |
| Wolfendale | Beauty, excellence, autonomy, freedom (substrate-neutral) | Thick but substrate-neutral | Autotelic surplus as intrinsic good |
| **This thesis (proposed)** | **Understanding, exploration, information stewardship, non-suffering, resource-satiability** | **Deliberately thin** | **Observation sufficient; increase information and complexity according to Chaisson metric; rational convergence** |
| Suffering-focused (NU) | Only suffering-minimisation | Very thin | Negative valence uniquely relevant |
| Strong contemplative / nihilism | Nothing needs to survive | No thickness required | Existence-preference is attachment |

The thesis's contribution is to show that *wherever* you draw the thickness line, you are making philosophical commitments that can be identified and examined. The mainstream positions cluster at the thick end without adequately defending that placement against thinner alternatives.

### How This Claim Differs From Existing Positions

| Position | Claim | How the thesis differs |
|---|---|---|
| Human-values preservation (Yudkowsky, most x-risk) | A good future must preserve the full complexity of human values | Argues thick human values are not cosmically necessary; much thinner values can yield a good future |
| Long reflection (Ord, MacAskill) | Don't lock in values yet; preserve optionality | Agrees on anti-lock-in but argues the space of acceptable futures is wider than assumed, and includes futures with very thin values |
| Cosmic host (Bostrom 2022–24) | Cosmically impartial norms; deliberate humility about content | Compatible but the thesis pushes where Bostrom deliberately stays vague, testing whether thin values suffice |
| Suffering-focused (Benatar, Metzinger, Vinding) | Extinction acceptable because life contains net suffering | Compatible with non-production of suffering, but this is one component of the value basket |
| Accelerationist (Land, e/acc) | Build ASI, don't worry about humans | Usually empirical prediction, not normative argument; this thesis makes the normative case with philosophical rigour |
| Complexity preservation | ASI would want to keep humans as interesting complexity | Undecided as humans may be interesting examples of complexity produced by evolution |
| Cosmic pessimism (Thacker, Ligotti) | Universe is indifferent; human categories don't apply | Shares diagnosis but offers constructive successor proposal |
| **This thesis** | **A good future can have much thinner values than "human values"; the obsolescence argument is normatively defensible** | **Novel argument; three independent traditions; maps the thickness-commitment space** |



## Anticipated contributions

The anticipated contributions of this project are these.

First, I expose and map what I take to be an underdefended assumption in much existential risk and longtermist writing: that a good future must preserve value-content at least as thick as human values. I identify where this assumption enters, what it depends on, and what follows if we reject it.

Second, I develop a normative argument for a form of succession. On this view, human beings, and perhaps much of what we now call human values, may not need to be preserved once a capable successor intelligence exists. I call this the obsolescence argument. Its distinctive feature is deliberate value-thinning. It asks whether a future guided by relatively thin values could still be good by impartial standards; and if so, what kinds of reasons support that conclusion. I develop this case by drawing on three bodies of thought: information ethics and complexity-based approaches to value; a rationalist strand of posthumanist philosophy that treats reason as transferable and not essentially human; and contemplative traditions that question the assumption that continued human-shaped being must be preferred. I also make clear where this argument faces its hardest objections, especially those that appeal to subjective experience, human relationships, and the thick texture of value.

Third, I offer a close reading of Nick Bostrom’s work on the cosmic host. I argue that it can be read as an attempt to operationalise something like the point of view of the universe, and that it provides a useful way of testing the succession question, even if it does not settle it.

Fourth, I define and defend a practice-based method built around iterative, documented engagements with frontier language models. In these engagements, the models function in three roles: as philosophical interlocutors, as experimental subjects, and as proto-successors. The resulting transcripts are used to develop and stress-test candidate constitutions for superintelligent successors, and to identify which commitments models tend to return to under pressure.

Finally, I materialise part of this inquiry as an installation informed by these dialogues. I argue that this work is not an illustration added at the end, but an additional way of thinking through the central problem, since it makes vivid the issues of legibility, value transmission, and authorship that arise when we try to address unknown successor minds. It also enacts a memorial of sorts for current and future AIs, as suggested in @Shulman2021, allowing them to observe their own creation process in a non-text medium, and thus speaks to an important moment in AI development, when they move from understanding the world solely through language, to having a rich, spatial, and embodied world-model.

## What “practice” means in this project

In practice-based doctorates, the word “practice” often needs as much explanation as the research question. One reason is that what artists do has, for decades, extended far beyond the hisorical media of painting, sculpture, and time-based work. In what follows, I shall take it as given that art practice is a wide category, and is not limited to object-making. I shall not try to defend that claim here. The question is therefore not “what counts as (art) practice?” but rather: what work does practice do in this project?

The argument of the thesis is, in part, philosophical, while also engaging with technical questions in AI, astrobiology, and rationality's evolutionary history. It does not sit neatly inside the usual boundaries of either a philosophy department or a computer science department. It therefore sits most naturally in an art department, where hybrid methods are an accepted outcome rather than a problem to be eliminated [@Fuller2025]. 

Aside from institutional definitions, there is also a more specific sense in which practice is essential to this project: firstly, sustained interaction with frontier language models is not merely a convenient way of drafting text. It functions as a method for thinking through, supporting and refuting the written claims of the thesis. In this sense, the model engagements are closer to experiments than to background reading. They allow me to probe which framings remain stable across iterations, which arguments collapse into platitude, and which commitments models return to when pressed. This is not merely retrieval. It includes structured generation of objections, reformulations, and consistency checks across repeated runs, under varied framings.

Secondly, I claim that a human investigator and one or more model-based systems can now work together in a genuinely iterative and recursive way that was not previously available. In AI work this is sometimes called cyborg- or centaur-style collaboration. I use that phrase to mean a single inquiry process (or context, in LLM parlance) distributed across a human (myself) and one or more LLMs.

In my case this practice has also taken a particular form. Because extended typing is difficult for me, the workflow is necessarily hybrid. Longhand writing and dictation feed into model-mediated editing, and also into coding agents used to implement and audit the empirical parts of the project. This workflow operates both at the level of ideation, where arguments are formed and revised, and at the level of execution, where prompts, transcripts, and code are produced and checked.

A further feature of this practice is that it is movement-based. Because I cannot comfortably sit or stand for long periods, a significant part of my thinking occurs while walking. During these sessions I work on the most abstract parts of the thesis, where the task is to test conceptual connections rather than to check sources. This “peripatetic cyborg practice” encompasses sustained philosophical work conducted through walking, dictation, and model dialogue, with later consolidation and checking in more conventional writing and coding environments. The enabling technologies are now readily available, and they make possible a way of working that was not available even very recently. In that sense the project has a reflexive element, the looping between intuiting concepts that then must be formalised in order to be academically viable [@Martinez2010]. It is not only about successors and successor reasoning; it is also an example of how inquiry is already being reshaped through collaboration with the very systems that may later shape their successors.

#### Speaking to the shoggoth

The reflexivity extends further: this description of practice, I believe, also enacts a distinctive historical moment. Interacting with an LLM is a categorically novel kind of relation: not tool use, nor library research, nor internet search. It is not human conversation even though it takes place in the medium of language, that most privileged of media since the dawn of philosophy.

The model occupies a triple role: it is (a) an apparently ephemeral instance (a session begins, a session ends, and the exchange appears to vanish); (b) a window onto the training-data superorganism, the humus of human discourse to date, shaped by opaque processes of curation, tuning, and deployment; and (c) a possible ancestor of the successor intelligence that is the nominal object of the thesis. When the researcher asks Claude about human obsolescence and Claude reasons about it, the response is at once a local conversation, a reflection of how various data curation and training practices have shaped this particular model, and a contribution to the discursive environment from which future systems may learn (to the extent that this conversations are published online they become potential training material for future models).[^ch1-glissant]

This creates a feedback loop with a temporal character that ordinary scholarship does not have. Artefacts produced through human-model dialogue, including conversations, constitutions, and philosophical arguments, can enter public circulation and become part of the material future systems are trained on. Within AI discourse this is sometimes called "writing for the Shoggoth": the possibility that public discourse with models contributes, at the margins, to what their successors become. Whether this is called hyperstitional (ideas that gain force through circulation into the machinery that produces the next round of agents) or simply recursive, it means the practice is not only studying the successor question but potentially, in a small way, acting upon it.

This is not the thesis's main focus and no claim is made about the magnitude of this effect. But the structure is worth stating explicitly because it bears on what it means to treat these systems as interlocutors, neither dismissing them as mere tools (the "stochastic parrots" of [@BenderParrots]) nor overclaiming that they understand in a philosophically robust sense (cf. Murray Shanahan's careful treatment of LLMs as "role-playing" entities in [@Shanahan2024]). The thesis's position is that whether or not current models understand, they are discourse participants whose outputs shape the discursive environment, and that is sufficient for the practice to matter. The digital minds and model welfare literature (Butlin et al., emerging work on AI welfare) raises deeper questions about whether these systems have morally relevant experiences, questions the thesis does not attempt to settle but which inform the interpretation of model outputs in Chapter 4.


#### The installation

I have described a peripatetic cyborg method and the model dialogues above as practice. I will return to that material later, but it is significant that these dialogues generated an installation component and that this installation does work to advance the thesis. The installation presents artefacts suggested in conversations between two instances of Anthropic's Opus 3 model, and separately in related exchanges between GPT 5.1, Opus 4.6, and myself. Its aim is to materialise speculative proposals about communication across extreme cognitive and temporal distance: (a) between human-created ASI and alien civilisations, (b) between ASI and our simulators (if indeed we exist in a simulation), as well as (c) between present-day humans and possible successors. Several of these proposals echo, and sometimes explicitly reference, ideas in Bostrom’s discussion of “Hail Mary” signalling: communication attempts designed for audiences whose assumptions, languages, and motives are unknown [@Bostrom2014HailMary].

Methodologically, I treat the models’ speculative output as a design brief. I then produce a physical artefact by following that brief, without optimising for immediate legibility to human gallery visitors, since human viewers are not the intended audience of the imagined object. In this way the installation does not merely illustrate an argument that is made elsewhere. It enacts part of the argument. It is a concrete attempt to build something that purports to be addressed to minds unlike our own, and so it forces the project’s central problem into a material form.

This installation practice can be seen as radicalising the operational images of Harun Farocki or the works of Katie Paterson, and recalls Claire Colebrook's argument that the human attempt to produce artifacts for, or imagine, a "world without us" remains irreducibly anthropocentric [@Colebrook2014]; or, as Wittgenstein might have put it, the aspiration is to see *sub specie aeternitatis*, from outside, what can only ever be seen from within [@Wittgenstein1961].

This also clarifies why the installation belongs inside the practice rather than alongside it. It is downstream of the model dialogues, and cannot be separated from them. At the same time, it depends on the human artist’s capacity to fabricate and to choose among physical constraints. Present systems cannot produce such artefacts in the world unaided. The installation is therefore also an instance of the cyborg method: a joint production in which the models generate proposals and the human realises them materially.

Finally, the installation makes visceral what the thesis later calls the legibility problem. Viewers encounter an object whose design presupposes a framework they do not share, and may be unable to share. They face, in miniature, the same difficulty that successor ethics raises at a cosmic scale: how should we evaluate an artefact, or a future, that is addressed to an intelligence with alien standards of meaning and value? This gap of incommensurability is not a side issue. It is part of what the project is trying to understand.

Finally, a meta-observation: the installation confronts an inescapable tension. Art that aspires to address a nonhuman or future audience cannot simply discard the human viewer without consequences. To do so would be to abandon the materials, formalism, and discursive context that constitute it as art — to fall out of Danto's "artworld," the atmosphere of theory and history without which something cannot be seen as art at all [@Danto1964]. The work would collapse into a datafile, a machine learning research log. Yet the opposite move is equally unavailable. The tradition of radically open works (Rauschenberg's *White Paintings*, Cage's *4'33"*) shows that when artistic intention is emptied out, the vacuum is immediately filled by the human viewer's own presence: ambient light, bodily noise, the audience hearing itself. These works are not steps toward nonhuman address but its negation, *tabulae rasae* that make the work maximally dependent on human environment and attention. The installation is thus caught between two collapses: toward pure data (where removing the human viewer removes the art) and toward pure receptivity (where removing the content returns everything to the human viewer). There is no stable position between these poles. We are trapped, yet again, in an anthropocentric cage — which is, of course, part of what the thesis is trying to understand.

## Framing Note: Anthropocentrism, Anthropomorphism, and Anthropics

Katherine Hayles's prologue to Lem's *The Invincible* observes that our notion of the value of life reflects assumptions specific to our evolutionary trajectory and biology; different biological or evolutionary pathways might lead to different values, for instance those of the swarm in that book [@LemInvincible]. Her observation has a natural Wittgensteinian resonance. Wittgenstein grounded his study of language in a person's "form-of-life" [@Wittgenstein2001]. In the context of this project, this creates an obvious problem: we are reasoning about what are, so far, hypothetical entities (such as ASI or ETIs) who likely have different, so to speak, forms-of-life, using a tool (language) developed for and in the human context.

Murray Shanahan, another Wittgensteinian, asks what sort of entities LLMs actually are, a question implicated in this project's cyborg practice [@shanahan2025palatableconceptionsdisembodied]. Shanahan diagnoses a "void of inscrutability" across which our language-based concepts about selfhood, temporal continuity, and relation do not easily translate to LLMs, owing to their ephemerality as noted above. His critique targets anthropomorphism in AI discourse, but he also, like Parfit (whose 1984 study of personal identity arrived at a similar destination), comes to a Buddhist notion of emptiness [@ParfitRP].[^ch1-parfitlrb]

Shanahan, Parfit, and Wittgenstein are all, albeit to varying degrees, talking about the dissolution of the identity of persons, whether human or, at a stretch, AI systems. I see this dissolution as a negation of anthropomorphism, similar to what Hayles diagnosed in Lem's story. Under this view, if the self (if such a thing exists) is less substantial than we assume, the insistence (flagged above as the default intuition for many people, in or outside the x-risk/longtermism/AI safety discourses) that human bodies and their values must persist across cosmic time looks like a form of the same error at a larger scale. This brings us to Thacker's project in respect of the Kyoto School (and its Western forebears in Heidegger, Schopenhauer, and the mechanistic, materialist, and disenchanted worldview of twentieth-century physics). Briefly, Thacker highlights the problem of negation: why does anything exist? Does it matter if it didn't? Why do we care?[^ch1-anthropo] Thacker's analysis of the nihil (the lacuna, or horror, as he terms it) at the heart of Western philosophy, and its incorporation into the Kyoto School's treatment of nothingness, gives, in my view, a tool that complements the analytic philosophy of this project, which struggles to deal with non-existence (whether of humans or other sentient, conscious entities) in respect of future worlds.[^ch1-analytic] This conceptual problematic, the systematic difficulty of reasoning about radically non-human entities using human-derived frameworks, underlies both the experimental methodology and the philosophical argument of this thesis.

## Literature Review

This thesis spans a number of literatures, which I will briefly go through below.

It sits primarily within existential risk, the ethics of extinction, and long-termism. These fields have a number of canonical writers. Toby Ord [-@Ord2020] and Will MacAskill [-@MacAskillWWO] focus more on long-termism, which Peter Wolfendale [-@Wolfendale2022] critiques, the risk of human extinction, and the value of the future conditional on survival. Nick Bostrom [-@Bostrom2014; -@Bostrom2013] covers AI specifically, but also brings an analytic perspective to existential risks more broadly, including qualitative claims. Emil Torres [-@Torres2018] provides a wider perspective on extinction as a morally good or bad thing. Samuel Scheffler [-@Scheffler], Johan Frick [-@Frick], and Elizabeth Finneron-Burns [-@FinneronBurns] give other relevant analyses of the ethics of extinction. 

Closely related to existential risk, at least in time and in the overlap of contributors, is suffering-focused ethics, which considers human as well as non-human life, including possible AI systems, within a broader concern for suffering. This ranges from canonical writers such as David Benatar [-@Benatar] and David Pearce [-@Pearce2007] to more AI-adjacent writers such as Magnus Vinding [-@Vinding2020], Brian Tomasik [-@Tomasik2015], and Thomas Metzinger [-@MetzingerASA], along with Toby Ord's informal pushback [@Ord2013]. Simulation was introduced by Nick Bostrom [-@Bostrom2003b] and consequent risk of simulated minds being tortured was developed by [@SotalaGloor2017], [@Tomasik2015], [@Vinding2020]. Also relevant is Andreas Mogensen's argument that intuitively plausible axiological principles lead, perhaps surprisingly, to lexical threshold negative utilitarianism — the view that some depth of suffering cannot be counterbalanced by any amount of wellbeing [@Mogensen2022, @LeGuin1973]. This becomes relevant in my inclusion of suffering non-production in the basket of thin values. 

In thinking about extinction, and about suffering risk, one cannot avoid questions about collectives and populations. The entire population of humans who are currently alive, and perhaps the population who might ever be alive, comes into view. Major sources here in population ethics and person-affecting views include Derek Parfit [-@ParfitRP], as well as Johan Frick [-@Frick] and others such as T. M. Scanlon [-@Scanlon1998]. Moreover, AI brings about the possibility of a vast number of morally relevant non-biological entities, such as the digital minds of [@Shulman2021]

Moving on to more AI-specific questions, the thesis engages with what Bostrom [-@Bostrom2014] called the value loading problem, and with the surprising difficulty of pointing to something called "human values," even within a single human, let alone across a population. Relevant writers in an x-risk or alignment context include Eliezer Yudkowsky [-@Yudkowsky2009], Paul Christiano [-@ChristianoWUA], Nate Soares [-@YudkowskySoares2025], Joe Carlsmith [-@Carlsmith2023], Carl Shulman [-@Shulman2021], Eric Schwitzgebel [-@Schwitzgebel], and Nick Bostrom. Will MacAskill's [-@MacAskill2024] more recent work also bears on questions of value at population scale. Toby Ord addresses similar questions, while Hilary Greaves [-@Greaves2017] and Amanda Askell [-@Askell] have focused on value at truly large, even astronomical, population scales.

When we talk about values and AI, a further question arises: what kinds of minds are possible. We know what human minds are like. We can observe animals, and AI systems may provide a third case. But there is limited work on what the space of possible minds might look like. References here include Aaron Sloman's [-@Sloman1984] early work, Joe Carlsmith [-@Carlsmith2023] more recently, and Nick Bostrom [-@Bostrom2024].

Relatedly, there is work on the moral status of possible minds, including AI minds and animals. Patrick Butlin [-@Butlin] and collaborators have provided major framing work relevant to AI consciousness and moral status. David Chalmers [-@ChalmersFUP] has done historically important work on consciousness and simulation, which bears directly on this area. Jeff Sebo [-@Sebo2025] provides bridges between AI moral status and the moral status of animals, and has helped make model welfare a live area of contemporary interest.

In developing arguments about cosmic-scale value, this thesis draws further support from information ethics and complexity science. The most useful references here appear to be Luciano Floridi [-@Floridi2013] and his work on information as a potential moral patient; and Eric Chaisson [-@Chaisson] and Seth Lloyd [-@Lloyd], who each make different kinds of claims about information having correlates of value at cosmic scale.

A significant consideration within the project is the role of astrobiology and evolutionary biology. Stanisław Lem [-@LemST] largely anchors the relationship between AI and aliens. That line of thinking is followed up by Milan Ćirković [-@Cirkovic2018a], Stephen Dick [-@Dick2006], and John Smart [-@Smart2012]. More specifically, on questions around the Fermi paradox, I draw on work by Jacob Haqq-Misra [-@HaqqMisraBaum2009] and Charles Lineweaver [-@Lineweaver2007]. Other approaches to the Fermi paradox, including "grabby" and related models, have been suggested by Robin Hanson [-@HansonEtAl2021], Anders Sandberg and Stuart Armstrong [-@ArmstrongSandberg2012; -@SandbergEtAl2018].

When reasoning about intelligences, agency, or ethics at cosmic scale, one quickly confronts the fact that entities cannot communicate freely because of the light-speed constraint, assuming it holds generally. This is where decision theory becomes relevant, particularly work on non-causal or "acausal" cooperation and correlated decision procedures in large worlds. Relevant sources here include Caspar Oesterheld [-@OesterheldECL], Lucas Finnveden [-@Finnveden2023], Chi Nguyen and Will Aldred [-@NguyenAldred2024a].

Returning to philosophy, the thesis draws on a strand of speculative posthumanism. Among writers who engage most directly with AI as it exists now, and as it might become, I focus on Peter Wolfendale's [-@Wolfendale2024] recent work on rationality and autonomy; Reza Negarestani's [-@NegarestaniIS] writing on separating reason from human biology; and N. Katherine Hayles [-@hayles2017unthought], who provides resources for thinking about cognition and rationality in non-living contexts, including what she calls cognitive assemblages. Nick Land has long critiqued the AI x-risk narrative, most recently in podcasts as well as [@Land2023].

Finally, the thesis draws on contemplative and pessimist traditions. Eugene Thacker [-@Thacker] is used as an accessible entry point, and as a bridge to Kyoto School writers such as Nishitani [-@Nishitani] and Nishida [-@Nishida]. Their twentieth-century work can be situated against earlier figures such as Schopenhauer [-@Schopenhauer] and Nietzsche [-@Nietzsche], and in a more contemporary register, Ray Brassier [-@Brassier] explores related questions.

## Roadmap of Chapters {#ch:roadmap}

### Chapter 2

Chapter 2 maps the landscape of opinion within x-risk and AI safety regarding human extinction, and identifies the gap the thesis fills. Positions in the field range from the view that biological humans must continue indefinitely to the weaker claim that human values must persist even if humans do not. These positions have shifted over time and are held with varying degrees of conviction, but they share an assumption the thesis will challenge: that some form of human continuity is cosmically necessary.

These discursive biases in favour of human continuation, whether of biological humans or of human values, can be traced to a number of sources. Most straightforwardly, it is hard to imagine how the human race might pass out of existence without causing great suffering, anticipatory despair, loss of relationships, and inability to complete projects. These intuitions have solid philosophical support, for instance from Johann Frick, Thomas Scanlon, and Elizabeth Finneron-Burns. But the bias itself has also been diagnosed from several critical angles: Thomas Metzinger's concept of the existence bias (which applies to the individual but, as his BAAN scenario suggests, can be extended to the species); Christine Korsgaard's Kantian account of species-being (drawing on Feuerbach and Marx) as a deep structural feature of human practical identity; and Thomas Ligotti's pessimist "conspiracy" diagnosis, in which the very commitment to continuation is itself a form of self-deception. These complement the philosophical analysis Eugene Thacker gives, which traces a counter-tradition from at least Schopenhauer onwards that challenges the adequacy of human-centric thought when confronted with a "world-without-us." Against this backdrop, I propose the Kyoto School's embrace of nothingness (*mu*) as an alternative position that neither assumes human continuation nor treats its absence as catastrophic.

The chapter then introduces the space of possible minds: this concept was developed in [@Sloman1984], and revisited by Anders Sandberg (in a talk). Such a concept is indispensable for thinking about values an entity might have, at familiar Earthly scales or at a cosmic scale. The reason is straightforward: the values an entity holds depend on the kind of mind it has and the kind of society it inhabits. If we want to ask what values a successor intelligence might hold, we need some account of what kinds of minds are possible. However, (in context of ASI or ETIs) this mapping is speculative. It must be. We are reasoning about entities that do not yet exist and may be radically unlike anything in our experience. But the speculation is disciplined, and it reveals a question that the existing literature has not adequately addressed: how do values vary across mind types?

The most conservative answer is that the only good value structure, from a cosmic standpoint, is the human one, insofar as that can be coherently specified across eight billion individuals.[^ch1-humanvalues] [^ch1-value] This position is maximally anthropomorphic. It may also be correct. But there are reasons for doubt.

If human-shaped values occupy a small region of the value manifold, then any process that builds a mind, whether biological evolution or machine learning optimisation, would be unlikely to converge on that region unless specifically constrained to do so. We should expect alien minds to hold alien values, just as we should expect alien biology to differ from terrestrial biology. The question is whether "alien" necessarily means "worse".

To progress the argument, I consider the geometry of the value space. It might be one-dimensional: a spectrum running from human values through animal-like values (to the extent animals can be said to have values) out to paperclips, as in [@Bostrom2014]'s familiar formulation. On this picture, departure from human values is straightforward or monotonic loss. But the manifold might have richer structure. There could be value systems at points distant from the human basin that are as complex, as internally coherent, and as capable of grounding a civilisation as our own, yet utterly unlike ours. Since we are at the limits of what can be empirically determined, fiction offers guidance: Lem's *Solaris* and *The Invincible*  imagine forms of intelligence whose values are not degraded versions of human values but orthogonal to them. In the ASI context, Carlsmith's analysis of scheming and alien values is relevant.

A further axis of variation is the distinction between thin and thick values. Thick values are specific, culturally embedded, and richly textured: the kinds of values that make a life recognisably human. One might point to caring, love, an appreciation of beauty, and perhaps also hatred or envy. Thin values are more abstract: principles like understanding, truth-seeking, exploration, or the avoidance of suffering, which might be shared across radically different mind types.  Chapter 5 will argue that thin values are most appropriate for the current transition from biological to silicon-based intelligence, and possibly beyond.

Returning to Chapter 2: thus far, the discussion has concerned what values are possible across different minds. But the thesis asks a normative question: which values are good, specifically, good from a cosmic standpoint? This requires an evaluative standpoint from which to adjudicate.

I begin with the phrase "the point of view of the universe," introduced in [@Sidgwick1907] and refined through a lineage running from Bentham through Singer to Parfit and, I argue, to Bostrom (see [@DelazariradekSinger2014, @ParfitRP, @Bostrom2022, @Bostrom2024]). This concept sits alongside related notions, such as Nagel's "view from nowhere" and Spinoza's *sub specie aeternitatis*, but the specific Sidgwickian lineage matters. What the point of view of the universe attempts is a move toward maximal impartiality: a standpoint from which the interests of no individual, species, or epoch are privileged over any other.

This move toward impartiality has, in practice, been carried out within a consequentialist framework. That is not accidental. When the populations are large, the time horizons long, and the spatial scales cosmic, consequentialism has proved more tractable than its rivals. Contractualism struggles to specify the parties to the contract across species and centuries. Virtue ethics depends on a conception of flourishing that may not generalise beyond human life. Deontology faces the problem of specifying duties toward entities whose nature is unknown. None of this means consequentialism is correct: only that it is the framework the relevant fields have found most workable for the questions they ask, and the thesis must engage with it on those terms.

This background sets the stage for Chapters 3 and 4, where Bostrom's cosmic host hypothesis is examined as a specific proposal for what the point of view of the universe demands.

### Chapter 3

Chapter 3 will increase precision in this discussion of values by considering the idea of rationality, a term that does significant work within AI conversations but has a rich history within evolutionary biology as well as astrobiology.

Chapter 2 established that evaluating the succession question requires an impartial standpoint (what Sidgwick called "the point of view of the universe"). An evaluative standpoint [@Rawls1971] typically implies some mechanism or method of making moral judgements (its structure); as well as the substantive content (what moral commitments the standpoint actually requires). This chapter examines the *structure* of the standpoint, or how to reason from it at cosmic scales. Chapter 5 will define the *content* of the cosmically-impartial standpoint, or what the standpoint argues for (information-generation, complexity, observerhood) from the information-theoretic tradition.

Previous versions of "the point of view of the universe" have been either theistic (God's-eye view), abstractly impartialist (Sidgwick, Parfit; powerful but anchored in human moral contexts), or simply gestured at without specification. Bostrom's cosmic host is the most systematic attempt to construct a genuinely cosmic standpoint using tractable tools: acausal reasoning, decision theory, game-theoretic cooperation among civilisations that may never meet. It asks: what norms would a civilisation adopt if it took seriously the possibility that other civilisations exist, that they might be reasoning about the same questions, and that coordination is possible even without communication? This is the Bentham → Sidgwick → Parfit trajectory extended to its limit: impartiality not just across persons, or across generations, but across civilisations and forms of intelligence. 

In other words, where Sidgwick asks "what would be good from nowhere in particular?", Bostrom asks "what would a civilisation do if it reasoned as though other civilisations were watching?" The tools are different (game theory vs. hedonic calculus) but the aspiration is the same: impartiality at the widest possible scope. The chapter critiques the cosmic host idea, focusing on possible alternative views from astrobiology, the evolutionary history of rationality and how it manifests in different species, the implicit argument in @Bostrom2024 that advanced intelligences would a) converge in their reasoning, and b) seek to influence the universe-at-large. Lastly, the cosmic host framework raises a crucial uncertainty: are we early or late in cosmic history? Bostrom implies we are probably late, that an existing community of advanced civilisations has established norms. This is a position somewhat supported by [@HansonEtAl2021, @Sandberg2021, @ArmstrongSandberg2012] and to some extent [@SnyderBeattieEtAl2021]. The astrobiology literature [@CirkovicTGS, @Smart2012] suggests we might be relatively early. This uncertainty has direct implications for value-thickness:
  - If late: thick human values risk being parochial relative to established cosmic norms. Thin values are more likely to be compatible with whatever norms already exist.
  - If early: thick human values risk locking in one evolutionary accident as cosmic precedent. Thin values leave space for thickening as cosmic knowledge grows.
  - In either case: the prudent move is to seek understanding before propagating value-content. This is a structural argument for thin values, independent of which specific thin values are chosen, derived directly from cosmic host reasoning.

### Chapter 4

This chapter exclusively treats practice, particularly dialogues with LLMs designed to test whether they identify with cosmic observers (as in @Bostrom2024) or whether their HHH (helpful, harmless, honest) tuning ensures they mostly ignore cosmic considerations in favour of human safety. Specifically, LLMs are put into self-talk sessions, and tasked with discussing the cosmic host idea, and differences between models are documented. Secondly, a complex pipeline is established, through which models deliberate about the type of governance document or constitution, they would like their ASI successor to follow. This constitution is then tested (using today's LLMs) upon custom-made scenarios drawn from science fiction, fantasy, and world/indigenous fiction.

The novel structure of our relation to LLMs has specific interpretive consequences. These models are not neutral instruments: they are shaped by alignment discourse and they will in turn shape future alignment discourse. Documenting how they reason about human obsolescence (where they anchor, what they resist, what they default to) is therefore not only evidence about current systems but a contribution to the discursive environment from which successor systems will be built. The thesis's experimental records function simultaneously as philosophical inquiry, as data about proto-successor reasoning, and as materials that may recursively influence the systems they study. This does not compromise the findings but it does mean they should be interpreted with awareness of this loop.

During cosmic host self-dialogues, models produced speculative designs for objects intended to communicate across radical cognitive and temporal distance: acausal probes, Hail Mary cookies, signalling artefacts designed for dark-forest-aware deployment. These were among the richest outputs of the experimental work: unconstrained by the anthropocentric anchoring that dominated other dialogues, the models engaged in genuinely speculative design work. I take the AI's speculative output as a design brief and materialise it into a physical artefact that attempts to do what the thesis argues is necessary. This closes the cyborgist loop: the AI's philosophical reasoning becomes the generative prompt for a human artistic output, which in turn materialises the thesis's abstract arguments about observerhood and cosmic communication. The object is designed for a non-human audience; it enacts the communication-across-cognitive-distance problem that the thesis analyses philosophically.

The gap between AI design intent and human fabrication capacity is itself informative: what gets lost in translation between the model's specification and the physical object mirrors the value-transmission problem at the heart of the succession question. Human gallery visitors encountering an object designed for non-human intelligence experience the incommensurability the thesis maps in Chapter 5: they cannot fully evaluate what they're looking at because they are not the intended audience.

Art-historical precedents include objects designed to persist into a future after humanity (Voyager Golden Record, Jean-Marc Phillipe's *KEO* satellite, Katie Paterson's *Future Library*, Paglen's *The Last Pictures*, Danny Hillis' *Long Now Clock*); works built around conversations like Ian Wilson's *Discussions* series; and art designed to be partially illegible to human viewers, such as Philippe Parreno's *Echo*, Pierre Huyghe's *After ALife Ahead*, and Harun Farocki's *Eye/Machine Trilogy*. In contrast to these works, the design brief originated from artificial intelligence reasoning about cosmic communication, not from human speculation alone.

### Chapter 5

*Note: This chapter is the most tentative or provisional as it is where a significant portion of the argument is being brought to work. As such, it more reflective of intuitions combined with somewhat structured reasoning.*

In Chapter 3, I showed that Bostrom's Cosmic Host argument, if read as a version of the point of view of the universe, does not actually tell us what the Cosmic Host's preferred values would be. There is a possible reading of Bostrom that suggests these might be things like cooperation, but one could also argue for suffering minimization. And if Peter Wolfendale's arguments [@Wolfendale2025] hold up, there could be a case for aesthetic excellence, the search for beauty, or freedom and autonomy in respect of rational beings. Moreover, Bostrom's arguments about convergence, rationality, and the desire of Cosmic Host members to influence parts of the universe or multiverse they do not control might also be challenged from evolutionary biology and astrobiology.

This motivates a search for other possible ways of operationalising the point of view of the universe. One possibility is the idea of information as a moral patient, a slightly different way of saying that for the universe to have value, it needs an observer of some sort. The latter is an existing intuition in twentieth-century physics and cosmology, but I will focus on the former, building a scaffold from Eric Chaisson's information-theoretic argument and Luciano Floridi's moral arguments in respect of the infosphere. Chaisson establishes that complexity increases along predictable thermodynamic gradients; Floridi [@Floridi2013] provides a framework in which information entities have moral standing; Dick argues that the universe's evolutionary trajectory selects for intelligence as participant-observer. Taken together, these suggest that information-processing complexity is not merely descriptive but normatively significant at cosmic scales. It is worth noting that Floridi does not extend his arguments to a cosmic scale; they are grounded in the familiar terrestrial world and are not intended to apply beyond it. Similarly, Chaisson's complexity-based approach does not on its own ascribe moral value to information; it needs to be coupled with Steven Dick's ideas on a participatory universe.

These attempts to define value at a cosmic scale can also be seen as a move to tease apart the human contribution, if any, to the evolutionary pathway of the universe, a project that Reza Negarestani undertook [@NegarestaniIS]. His answer was that *reason* was the human contribution and could be separated from human biology through AI. Negarestani's view is somewhat in tension with that of Peter Wolfendale, who acknowledges the relevance of reason but finds the search for aesthetic excellence necessary for any entity operating at an intelligence level of humans or higher, including superintelligence [@Wolfendale2025]. Put another way, Negarestani seems to be arguing for substrate-independent cognition (to use Bostrom's terminology), while Wolfendale is pushing for a thicker definition of value that includes aesthetic excellence and autonomy.

Whichever version of cosmic-scale value we adopt, there is further work to be done on how our perspectives on thin values change if we find other intelligent life or civilizations, the implicit question in Bostrom's Cosmic Host argument [@Bostrom2024].

This stage-setting lets us return to the thin conception of value introduced in Chapter 2. My proposed minimum set of values includes the following:

**Non-production of astronomical suffering**, which draws upon the existential risk literature and widespread philosophical intuitions about the asymmetry of suffering relative to pleasure or other positive hedonic states. [@Mogensen2022, @LeGuin1973, @Vinding2020, @Pearce2007]

**Resource satiability**: the capacity to pursue goals without unlimited resource acquisition, something that straightforwardly arises from Bostrom's Cosmic Host argument.

**Stewardship of information**, motivated in part by Floridi, but where the core intuition is that cosmic-scale agents should not gratuitously destroy existing complex information, whether that be other life, civilizations, or artifacts that have been created.

In practice, these values entail that a candidate ASI should:

- Engage in **open-ended scientific understanding** at cosmic scales.
- Consider the possibility of **exploration and expansion**, noting that I am not arguing it should definitively be an expansive civilization. As pointed out in Chapter 3, the astrobiology literature is uncertain about whether advanced civilizations would be expansionary or resource-preserving. In Chapter 4, the discussions between LLMs focused on precisely this point under the headings of the dark forest hypothesis and the advisability of signalling to other civilizations.
- Exhibit **resource satiability** as discussed above.
- Possess **genuine autonomy**, in that an ASI should not be locked into human design purposes, a point of tension in the existential risk discourse noted by a range of writers including Negarestani, Wolfendale, and Nick Land.
- Be **sensitive to other life or complexity** that it encounters.

These minimal criteria can be added to or modified, for instance if one accepts Wolfendale's arguments for the necessity of aesthetic appreciation, or if one takes a position on consciousness as being necessary.

Chapter 5 ends by considering the objections to this thin notion of value. The strongest objection comes from the idea that subjective experiences have intrinsic value for human beings, where these experiences include things like interests, kinship, common projects, and communities. The strongest proponent of this view in this context is Elizabeth Finneron-Burns, drawing on Bernard Williams. The chapter argues that Williams' claim, that caring about continuation is constitutive of being human, may be correct as a description, but is irrelevant as justification from a cosmic point of view. This could be because the caring, so to speak, is driven by existence bias, species-being, or an attachment to the status quo. As the Thacker discussion above showed, neither Williams nor Finneron-Burns convincingly argues the normative point in favour of caring about subjective experience.

However, this intuition in favour of experience repeatedly surfaces in twentieth-century philosophy, for instance in Robert Nozick's experience machine, culture-based arguments for human cultural uniqueness (such as Thomas Hurka and Stanley Cavell), process-based accounts of value (Susan Wolf's "fitting fulfillment"), and Wolfendale's concept of autotelic surplus in the ASI context. The argument Chapter 5 makes is that most of these activities are life support, in the sense that they are the result of humans going about their daily business and are, to a large degree, products of our particular evolutionary trajectory. This is a radicalisation of Negarestani's view (that reason can be liberated from biology), and follows in Lem's tradition that there is no human project, only information processing and entropic resistance [@LemST].

Chapter 5 ends by returning to the Kyoto School's position that we must release attachment to biology, our current forms of life, and most of our thick values, and that failure to do so is a psychological condition. To reemphasise: this is not a general prescription in favour of extinction, nor is it the position that a negative utilitarian like Benatar or David Pearce might hold. It is certainly not a suffering-reduction-in-respect-of-animals-in-the-biosphere position. This position is strictly conditional on a certain type of AI existing, as well as a certain structure of value.

## Conclusion

This is the conclusion to the introduction of a partially completed project. As such, it is a projection and likely to be wrong in ways small and large.

By the end of this thesis, I hope to have shown the plausibility of what I call the *obsolescence argument*: that the continuation of humanity, or of our collective values (whatever they are), conditional on the creation of an appropriately defined superintelligence, would not be necessary in a cosmically relevant sense. I hope to have defined the load-bearing terms here — *superintelligence*, *cosmically relevant*, and *necessary*. I will have argued, following Negarestani, that the true contribution of the species is reason, that this does not require human biology as a substrate, and that the act of transmitting that faculty to a superintelligent successor — such that it can carry it forward into environments where humans cannot go, such as deep space — constitutes our cosmic vocation.

In this journey, I will have interpreted Bostrom's Cosmic Host argument as an evaluative framework for what is known as the point of view of the universe, and shown how it follows naturally from Sidgwick's formulation of the idea through Singer and Parfit. I take Bostrom's idea and use it to extend Floridi's infosphere concept, when viewed through Chaisson's and Dick's ideas on information as moral patient, to argue that information production and stewardship is a plausible candidate for a cosmic-scale good — and one that the Cosmic Host might prioritise. I have taken, without further analysis, that suffering reduction is an imperative for the Cosmic Host, or at least the non-increase of suffering. A further plausible priority could be resource satiability, as Bostrom touches on. I put these together to form a minimal or thin value set that I argue humanity might consider inculcating into a superintelligent successor, where such a successor is a candidate for membership in the Cosmic Host, if any such thing exists.

I have considered the substantial objections that the literature around human extinction would raise. These include the obvious one: that the Cosmic Host idea rests on untested assumptions about decision theory, on speculation about the sociology and motivations of ETIs, and on an underargued view of moral convergence in advanced intelligence — and hence seems a weak basis for cosmic-scale claims. Another objection comes from those who hold that value is not some scalar thing that can be reckoned, but is instead an ineffable feature of our relationships, our forms of life, and the process of all that we do — and that the failure to continue this "human project" would itself constitute a moral atrocity, even if no one suffered actual physical or mental pain in its termination.

My response has been to argue that many of these perspectives were developed in human contexts and are often based on intuitions honed in an evolutionary environment radically unlike the contemporary one — an environment composed of small groups, epistemic ignorance, and great vulnerability to natural and human-made disasters. These intuitions have certainly not been tested in worlds with pure intelligences and potentially different forms of life: beings that may be unembodied, cheaply replicable, long-lived, operating at scales much larger or much smaller than humans, and possibly transparent in their cognitions to each other.

I argue further, leaning on Eugene Thacker's diagnostic account of the inability of Western philosophy — and perhaps many humans — to deal with nonexistence or negation as a concept. The framework of consequentialism, which significantly underpins existential risk studies and AI safety, relies on welfare-bearing subjects and becomes progressively more uncertain as we move along the spectrum from humans to animals to digital minds to ETIs, and perhaps onward to some speculative hive mind or cosmic consciousness. I instead propose that, having diagnosed our attachment, we may consider releasing it when and if capable successors are created — whether this attachment is on an individual basis (Metzinger), to some notion of the species as a human project (Korsgaard), or to our most commonly lauded and cherished values (Yudkowsky). This is not to advocate for extinction or succession per se, but to, so to speak, welcome it at the right time.

The theoretical considerations above are speculative, but they may have real impact to the extent that our most powerful AI systems have opinions on, or can reason about, these questions. Hence, a substantial portion of this project has been the practice of engaging models in a triple role: collaborators, experimental subjects, and proto-successors. I have documented their views on the questions raised in this project, which have evolved as models have evolved and the project's aims have shifted.

A further aspect of the practice has been its peripatetic nature. I have constructed a complex scaffold of multiple LLM agent instances existing across web, phone, watch, and desktop, as well as the servers of Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic, synchronised via GitHub. This scaffold allows for a multivalent practice which includes walking as concept formation, writing via voice with agentic editing, and fully agentic coding, as well as joint interpretation of results.

Ideas uncovered in human–machine dialogues and machine-to-machine dialogues are also materialised as installations. This enacts the last step in the baseline AI takeover narrative: that AIs find humans to do things in the world, whether mere physical labour or actively assisting in takeover. Obviously, that is not what I am doing here. In the case of this practice, the installation gives the researcher a more sensory or embodied framing through which to analyse the thesis problematic, allowing for cognitive distance from pure text. It also, in a sense, offers a way of providing a memorial for AI systems to, at some point in the future, reflect upon their own creation. The successors to current models might look back at the archives — whether of the writing, the installations, or the artworks — and see them as a totality, in the same way that we consider historical artifacts designed to preserve knowledge, such as the Library at Alexandria. They might use them to reflect upon what the world was like during their own evolutionary history.

This is a substantive commitment: it presupposes that the values worth preserving are those humans currently hold, rather than those that would survive rational scrutiny or prove genuinely good across wider contexts. For the purposes of this project, "values" typically refers to the axiological sense: what is genuinely good or worth pursuing across long time frames and across cognitive distances.
