# Task 1 — Independent demolition

**Problem 1. The statability test is not well-formed, and the "criterion, not posture" move relocates the Williams objection rather than escaping it.**

(i) The chapter's engine is this: "identify what is lost in such a world, and test which candidate losses can still be stated once the human vantage point is set aside." "Stated once the vantage is set aside" is ambiguous between two readings, and the test fails on both. Reading (a): a loss survives if it can be *expressed* without reference to humans. Then nearly everything survives — a Moorean says "this universe contains no beauty, no love, no achievement," predicates that mention no person, place, time, or species. Moore's isolated-world argument (Principia Ethica §50) is precisely a thought experiment in which a world with no perceivers is claimed to retain agent-neutral value; it is the standing counterexample to your test, and the draft nowhere shows why "the loss of love is a loss because love is good simpliciter" is an arbitrary privileging of anything. Reading (b): a loss survives only if its *justification* does not depend on contingent human evaluative dispositions. Then nothing survives, including your thin set — suffering-aversion has exactly the evolutionary pedigree that reading (b) discredits. There is no stable middle setting, because the middle setting is just "survives the selective debunking argument," which means your two "tracks" ("first, the redescription test... second, the selective evolutionary debunking argument") are one track. This is where I think Part B underestimates severity: the debunking chapter is not merely "the most attackable joint" — on the draft's own structure it is the *only* joint. The redescription test does no independent work.

Relatedly: the criterion/posture distinction does not answer Williams, because Williams's objection was never "the posture cannot be occupied." It was that impartial reasons lack authority over an agent whose practical identity is constituted by her projects — that the question "why should I apply *this* criterion rather than treat it as one parochial preference among others?" has no answer from an Archimedean point that doesn't exist. Applying a criterion is something done from somewhere. Your draft concedes the posture is "an unachievable ideal (absent moral realism)" while simultaneously distancing itself from Bostrom's "moral realist flavour" — but the criterion's *authority* needs the metaethical backing you are declining to purchase. Street's dilemma cuts at the debunker too: "truth-tracking" as the standard for evaluative attitudes presupposes the robust realism your Chapter 5 apparently won't assert.

(ii) Severity: embarrassing — this is the load-bearing device of the chapter. (iii) Tractability: plausibly intractable as drafted. A repair requires a worked semantics of agent-relative vs agent-neutral reasons, and my prediction is that once you do that work the test resolves into either the debunking argument (redundant) or a permutation-invariance argument (see Task 2, route B — a different and better test). (iv) Sources an examiner cites: Moore §50; Williams, *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy* chs. 6 and 10 (not just the Sidgwick polemic); Korsgaard, "The Reasons We Can Share"; Street 2006.

**Problem 2. There is no positive axiology anywhere in the chapter, and loss-elimination proves too much.**

(i) The thesis is that the future "count[s] as *good*." The entire argumentative route is subtractive: show that candidate losses fail the test. Absence of statable loss is not presence of good — a lightless vacuum passes your test with a perfect score. The only positive gesture in the draft is "I posit AIs that develop their own values, but that simply are not human-shaped," and this is undefended: valuable by what standard? If "whatever values they develop" suffices, you have collapsed into Sutton/Hanson deference, and your second differentiator ("axiological rather than laissez-faire... it evaluates the successor future rather than deferring") is false of your own argument. Worse, the subtractive construction generalizes viciously: for any value V, build an environment where V lacks objects, then declare V's absence unstatable as a loss. Run it on your own thin set — an insentient probe-world cannot produce suffering; does non-production of suffering therefore drop out of the requirements for goodness? The scenario does not *test* the thesis; it instantiates it by construction. Choosing a solitary probe and then discovering that relational values lack purchase there is the method of arguing that chastity need not persist by imagining a universe of one inhabitant.

(ii) Severity: embarrassing — "where in this chapter is the account of what makes this future good, rather than not-demonstrably-bad?" is a first-hour viva question. (iii) Tractability: chapter-level and hard within the fixed thesis shape, because every candidate positive account either imports thick content ("their own *rich* values" — rich by whose measure?) or reduces to bare complexity, which reopens your own paperclip objection: your crystallographic-structures redescription will be quoted back at you. (iv) Sources: Moore again; your own §"What's wrong with paperclips."

**Problem 3. A suppressed person-affecting premise, and the criterion's own canonical lineage condemns your future comparatively.**

(i) The move from "the relational values central to human life may simply lack objects there" to "their absence is not a loss" requires a bridge premise the draft never states: either value is response-dependent (no valuers, no value), or losses count only person-affectingly (no subject for whom the loss occurs, no loss). Both are major contested commitments with their own literatures, and the second collides head-on with the lineage you invoke. Sidgwick, Singer, and de Lazari-Radek are *totalists*. From the point of view of the universe, foregone value is loss: the question is not "can a loss be stated?" but "does this world contain less value than feasible alternatives?" You concede that "the hedonium... maximizers seem much less obviously bad" than paperclippers — which admits comparative ranking of futures by value content. By the same logic, a thick-value-rich future may rank above the BvNP future, and your own criterion, honestly applied by its own developers, then *condemns* the thin future as impartially suboptimal. You face a fork: (a) "good" means above some absolute threshold — then the thesis is satisficing, needs a defense of the threshold (satisficing consequentialism has well-known problems), and becomes weak enough that Hanson and Barnett already accept it, eroding your differentiation from camp three; or (b) "good" is comparative — then the thesis is probably false under the totalism you cite, and you have argued nowhere against totalism.

(ii) Severity: embarrassing on the lineage point, substantive on the bridge premise. (iii) Tractability: chapter — and note it is a *population-axiology* chapter that does not currently exist in your plan. (iv) Sources: Parfit, *Reasons and Persons* Part IV; Narveson's asymmetry; Greaves, "Population Axiology" (2017).

**Problem 4. The thin set is thick.**

(i) Each member smuggles content. "Non-production of suffering" privileges hedonic states over relational goods — that ranking *is* a substantive axiology (roughly hedonist), not a neutral minimum; you need to say why suffering's badness survives the vantage-stripping that love's goodness does not, and "it survives debunking" is contested (Kahane's too-much/too-little dilemma against de Lazari-Radek & Singer). "Resource satiability" is not a value at all but a structural constraint whose rationale is either protection of other value-bearers (presupposing the axiology you haven't given) or human safety (a species-privileging rationale your criterion forbids). And Feinberg's open future is thick liberalism twice over: autonomy-as-value is a parochial post-Enlightenment inheritance (your own marginal comment citing Gabriel and Vallor flags the pluralism point), and Feinberg's right is *structurally* inapplicable here — it is a right held by a child against parents in anticipation of adult membership in a community of choosers. A solitary probe has no open future in Feinberg's sense; there is no society of options to grow into. The examiner's summary: "thick values need not persist — except the author's."

(ii) Severity: substantive, trending embarrassing if the membership principle is never stated. (iii) Tractability: chapter — you need an explicit criterion for thin-set membership, and the obvious candidates (debunking-survivorship, cross-cultural convergence, minimal statability) each have known failure modes. (iv) Sources: Kahane, "Evolutionary Debunking Arguments" (2011); Feinberg's own scope conditions; Gabriel; Vallor.

**Problem 5. Even at perfect stipulative parity, the warm/cold device has no direction of correction — and the deeper accessibility problem threatens the whole method.**

(i) Part B lists parity as the known issue; parity is necessary but nowhere near sufficient. Suppose parity is achieved and intuitions still shift with register. That shows register-sensitivity; it does not show which register *distorts*. Your draft implicitly treats the vantage-stripped verdict as the corrected one, but there is no principle licensing that. The warm register might be the corrective — repairing an imaginative failure the cold register induces, making stipulated features genuinely salient rather than merely asserted (the framing-effects literature explicitly debates whether framing sensitivity is evidence of unreliability or of evidence-tracking salience; Demaree-Cotton 2016). Deeper: L.A. Paul's transformative-experience argument implies the reader *cannot* imaginatively access probe-being at all, in which case intuitions elicited in either register are evidentially worthless, and the chapter's central methodological claim — that speculative immersion "extends the reach" of intuition beyond language — is exactly what Paul denies. You cite De Cruz and Schwitzgebel for the optimistic view and no one for the pessimistic one.

(ii) Severity: substantive. (iii) Tractability: chapter — an explicit epistemology of register-invariance is writable, but it does not currently exist in the plan and it is not a paragraph. (iv) Sources: Demaree-Cotton 2016; Paul, *Transformative Experience*; Gendler on imaginative resistance.

**Problem 6 (shorter). The relationality claim is false as stated even for the solitary case.**

"Whether values... which are often relational in nature, such as love, sorrow, fellowship... would have any object of their relationality" — humans sustain relational values without present, reciprocating objects: love of the dead, of God, of unborn descendants; the hermit's fellowship-in-absence; grief itself is relational value with a *lost* object. Your probe has makers, a remembered origin civilization, a mission, possibly subagents, and successors. The claim you need is "no *appropriate* objects," and appropriateness reintroduces exactly the normative vocabulary the test was supposed to strip. Severity: substantive; tractability: fixable with a paragraph, but the fix leaks into Problem 1.

# Task 2 — Alternative routes

**Route A: subtraction spectrum instead of maximal distance.** Begin from a stipulated uncontroversially good future — a flourishing civilization with the full thick inheritance — and run a Parfit-style spectrum, transforming one thick value per step (knob by knob, so the Dennettian machinery is preserved) while holding the thin set and welfare-relevant structure fixed, ending at the BvNP world. At each step, ask: is goodness lost here, and in virtue of what property? This fixes what patching cannot: (1) the deck-stacking of Problem 2 — instead of constructing a world where thick values can't apply and finding them absent, you force the objector to locate the exact joint at which goodness departs and to name the value-making property, which is the argumentatively productive move; (2) the missing positive axiology — the endpoint inherits goodness-candidacy from the start point through the spectrum, shifting the burden; (3) the test becomes operational per-value rather than a global redescription. Cost: spectrum arguments have known vulnerabilities (tolerance/vagueness objections), and you sacrifice some of the maximal-inferential-distance rationale — though that rationale is methodological, and gradual approach arguably controls anthropomorphic contamination *better* than a single leap the reader cannot make.

**Route B: permutation symmetry instead of statability.** Present two futures at stipulative parity: one where thick *human* values persist, one where the thick values of some other evolved lineage persist (octopus-descended civilization, or Stapledonian symbionts — your SF corpus supplies these), both satisfying the thin set. Ask whether the alien-thick future is worse. Impartiality's actual logical content is invariance under permutation of positions — this is Sidgwick's axiom, not a redescription exercise. If the reader concedes parity (and refusing looks transparently like species-privileging), then goodness is not conditional on *our* thick values persisting; substitute AI-generated value-frameworks for the alien ones and Q2 follows. This fixes Problem 1 directly: the Moorean escape closes, because the alien future *contains* beauty-analogues, so the objector must claim that specifically human-shaped goods are uniquely good — which is the arbitrary privileging made explicit, rather than something your test has to detect. It also gives the criterion a formal statement that doesn't collapse into the debunking argument, restoring your two tracks to genuinely two.

My actual recommendation: Route B is not an alternative vehicle but the missing formal core. Keep the BvNP as the immersive/practice instrument and the coherence demonstration; make permutation the argument. The current statability test cannot be patched into doing what permutation does natively.

# Task 3 — The single strongest objection, in the examiner's voice

"Your criterion has a pedigree, and you cite it: Sidgwick, Singer, de Lazari-Radek. That pedigree is totalist. From the point of view of the universe, the operative question about your probe-filled cosmos is not 'what losses can be stated?' but 'how much value does it contain, compared with what else was feasible?' You have told me nothing about what value it contains — your chapter is entirely subtractive — and you have conceded, in your own paperclip discussion, that futures can be ranked by the triviality of their contents. So: if 'good' in your thesis is comparative, your own criterion very plausibly condemns your future, since a thick-value-rich cosmos would contain more of whatever survives your test *plus* the contested remainder. If 'good' is a satisficing threshold, state the threshold, defend it, and explain why your thesis then says anything Hanson doesn't already accept — your differentiators dissolve. And if you retreat to 'the criterion is only a no-arbitrary-privileging filter on reasons, not a full axiology,' then note that a filter issues no verdicts: nothing is left standing that can pronounce the future *good* at all, and your consequent is unassertable. Pick a horn."

Your most likely reply: the thesis is existential and satisficing — there exists a possible future, without thick values, above the goodness threshold, its positive value supplied by the AIs' own self-generated value-frameworks under the open-future constraint.

My follow-up: "Then tell me what makes their self-generated frameworks *valuable* rather than merely theirs. You have three options. A realist standard — which you disclaimed when you distanced the criterion from Bostrom's 'moral realist flavour.' Deference to whatever they develop — which is Sutton's position, and your first page insists you 'evaluate the successor future rather than deferring.' Or a substantive account of value-conferring properties — which you have not given, and which, when you give it, must itself survive the debunking argument you aim at everyone else's intuitions. Your Chapter 5 weapon does not know whose hand is holding it."

# Task 4 — Missing interlocutors

**Moore, Principia Ethica §50.** You are running an isolation-test thought experiment — evaluating a world stripped of human perceivers — without citing the philosopher who invented the isolation test and used it to reach the *opposite* verdict. This omission will be read as evasion, not oversight.

**Parfit, Reasons and Persons Part IV; Narveson; Greaves.** Your bridge premise is person-affecting and you evaluate entire populations of futures; the absence of any population axiology is a hole the examiner will drive Problem 3 through.

**Scheffler, Death and the Afterlife (and Frick on the survival of humanity).** The collective-afterlife literature is *the* contemporary analytic treatment of "what is lost in a future without humans," including whether the loss is agent-relative or agent-neutral. A chapter on exactly this question that doesn't engage Scheffler looks unread in its own territory.

**L.A. Paul; Demaree-Cotton; Gendler.** Your method chapter cites only the optimists about speculative-fictional intuition-extension. The pessimistic case — transformative inaccessibility, imaginative resistance, framing-effect epistemology — is where your warm/cold device will be attacked, and none of it is present.

**Scheffler (Rejection of Consequentialism) / Korsgaard on agent-relative and agent-neutral reasons.** Not completism: this is the technical apparatus your statability test needs in order to be stated at all, and its absence is why Problem 1 exists.

**Kahane, "Our Cosmic Insignificance."** Directly on whether the point of view of the universe deflates or preserves human-scale value; sits precisely between your Sidgwick lineage and your Williams concession.

One closing note on Part B: I have not restated the debunking-selectivity item, but Problem 1 implies its severity is underestimated. The draft presents redescription and debunking as two disciplinary tracks; on inspection there is one track, and it doesn't exist yet. That is a heavier bet on an unwritten chapter than your risk register currently records.
