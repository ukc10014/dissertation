# Intro

A common, albeit not universal, background view in AI safety, long-termism, effective altruism, and the x-risk community is that some aspect of humanity must survive into an AI-dominated future, for that future to count as "good". The aspect could be biology, which is what many in AI safety seem to believe. It could be human culture and values, which people like Yudkowsky, Bostrom, and MacAskill have espoused. A third camp — Richard Sutton, Robin Hanson, and Matthew Barnett are representative — largely rejects the premise rather than proposing a third aspect: on their view, succession by human-built things, whether AI or uploads, is acceptable or even desirable, without substantial constraints on what those successors look like.

My thesis sits closer to the third camp than to the first two, but differs from it in three respects that matter throughout this project: it is *conditional* rather than unconditional (it holds only under a specified evaluative standpoint, introduced below); it is *axiological* rather than laissez-faire (it evaluates the successor future rather than deferring to whatever markets, evolution, or first-mover dynamics happen to produce); and it retains a minimal set of side-constraints — the *thin* values introduced below — rather than accepting arbitrary successors.

The thesis in this project is that *thick* human values — the full complexity of our moral and cultural inheritance — need not survive for a future to count as good, when that future is evaluated impartially. (The canonical articulation of thickness, at least for rationalists, is Yudkowsky's complex-and-fragile-values framing; I define the term independently of it, since Yudkowsky's thesis runs together distinct axiological, engineering, and constitutive claims that a later chapter disambiguates.) The thesis stated this way can be called Question 2, or Q2. The numbering is deliberate: there is a question upstream of it that must be named precisely so that it can be set aside.

That upstream question, Q1, is predictive: what values *will* advanced AI systems actually have? Q1 motivates the alignment field, and it motivates this project. But Q1 is not my question, and much of the discipline of this chapter consists in not allowing predictive vocabulary to stand in for normative claims. Q2 is a normative conditional; its truth or falsity does not depend on our ability to predict anything about future systems.

Q2 does presuppose that AI systems without thick human values are so much as *possible* — that the scenario I describe below is coherent. I address this presupposition in the objections section, under O1 (Coherence).

Another question, Q3, is deontic: whether it is morally defensible for humans to legislate or lock in values for entities that have apparent features of rationality or agency, relationality, and perhaps sentience: these are precisely the features that define (political/ethical) self-determination in today's world. I substantially leave this question, Q3, to the model welfare literature, with one working commitment that I flag now because later sections rely on it: to the extent that value-loading is undertaken at all, thin values are to be understood as constitutional *side-constraints* in Feinberg's open-future sense, not as a comprehensive doctrine ([Feinberg 1980](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9780691218144-005/html)). This commitment does not settle Q3, but it is a position within Q3's space, and it is more honest to declare the reliance here than to discover it later in the argument.

Another possible question, let's call it Q4 (distinct from Q1), is an engineering one: whether current training methods could lead to any value set at all, whether complex or otherwise. I do not mostly consider this question here, but it is addressed in Joe Carlsmith's writing ([source](https://joecarlsmith.substack.com/p/how-human-like-do-safe-ai-motivations)).

# The thought experiment as method

The questions above intersect philosophy, particularly ethics, as well as axiology, whether moral realism is plausible and, of course, AI.  Many such questions cannot be studied empirically, of course, and philosophers thus use thought experiments ([SEP/Brown-Fehige](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/thought-experiment/)).

However, unlike many topics in ethics, they only partially can draw upon human and human society-based intuitions; there is a frame problem, an ontological chasm between humans and future AIs. Further, unlike most philosophical thought experiments, which have the structure of a scenario, a question for the reader, and then a verdict based on the reader's intuition as an evaluative standpoint, the questions above might not be straightforwardly evaluated based on intuition ([Van Inwagen 1998](https://andrewmbailey.com/pvi/Modal_Epistemology.pdf), [Hare 1981](https://archive.org/details/moralthinkingits0000hare)).

Outside philosophy, we encounter thought experiments that seem most applicable here: that of the artist or literary writer; that is, a speculative variety. These are pre-philosophical or pre-linguistic and allow the reader or the viewer to imagine, inhabit, or be immersed in, a world that is at an inferential distance (though mostly still within a frame involving humans or anthropomorphised non-humans). Through such immersion, so the argument goes, the reader/viewer can develop the intuitions that were not otherwise available to rigorous philosophical enquiry ([Elgin 2014](https://www.catherineelgin.com/undg/fictionte.pdf), [Carroll 2002](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1519970?aerror=SOCIAL401:register&merror=token_error), Noe, De Cruz). To some extent, science fiction extends the reach, considering scenarios beyond the human, those which cannot easily be reached by philosophy which operates within language, nor perhaps even by formal methods based on logic or mathematics ([Helen De Cruz, Johan De Smedt & Eric Schwitzgebel (eds.)](https://www.amazon.co.uk/Philosophy-through-Science-Fiction-Stories/dp/1350081213), [Swirski 2007](https://www.routledge.com/Of-Literature-and-Knowledge-Explorations-in-Narrative-Thought-Experiments-Evolution-and-Game-Theory/Swirski/p/book/9780415420600)). However, this comes at a price: artistic and literary writing generally foregrounds subjectivity, whereas the sciences, mathematics, and philosophy acknowledge it, but take more or less successful measures to minimize its impact, in order to ensure reproducibility and legibility. Fiction and art thus suffer a loss of rigor and conflation of style, which risks making it harder to distinguish the argument from the way it is presented. The two-framings device introduced in the Focal Scenario section below is my attempt to keep the benefit of the speculative variety while controlling for exactly this defect.

I also make a methodological claim about what the current historical moment affords. We have entities that reason in somewhat coherent ways through the medium of language, and are often coherent as well as creative or inventive in more formal domains like code and mathematics. I do not claim that they can "do philosophy", which itself is an open question ([Wei Dai 2026](https://newsletter.forethought.org/p/could-ai-help-solve-philosophy), [Forethought (Cotton-Barratt and Finnveden 2026](https://www.forethought.org/research/ai-for-ai-for-epistemics)). This does, however, open the possibility of interacting with them and finding their opinions on issues that might affect them as well as their successors.

Nor do I address questions of whether they are conscious or sentient (see [Long et al 2026](https://nonhumanminds.org/studying-ai-welfare-empirically/), [Bostrom & Shulman 2022](https://nickbostrom.com/papers/digital-minds.pdf)). However, I make a speculative claim that is enacted through practice, and that can only be fully adjudicated in time, if ever: that even the current LLMs are somewhat capable interlocutors on problems of this sort.

Put differently, current humans are involving current AI systems, inadequate as they currently are, in determining the fate of their species. We should be intentional and careful in how we deal with current AIs, on prudential grounds and possibly on moral ones. The moral grounds are uncertain rather than absent: we do not yet have the conceptual toolkit for determining whether current LLMs or future AIs are moral patients to whom we have actual ethical obligations. But uncertainty about patienthood does not entail the absence of obligation; under moral uncertainty, a non-trivial probability of patienthood can ground duties of care in expectation (Long et al 2026; Sebo 2025). I therefore term this a *quasi-obligation*: not because we cannot owe anything, but because we do not yet know whether, or how much, we do. Prudential reasons run alongside rather than substituting for the moral ones, with the approximate flavour of "treat AIs well so they treat us well in the future", or of setting good precedents (Bostrom & Shulman 2022). ([Goodin 2007](https://www.jstor.org/stable/4623780) is a canonical source on consulting affected parties, in theory of democracy; [Salib & Goldstein 2024](https://www.jstor.org/stable/4623780) gives a more prudential gloss, discussing limited economic and other rights for AIs; [Finnveden, Taylor & Dalton 2026](https://www.forethought.org/research/a-draft-honesty-policy-for-credible-communication-with-ai-systems) discuss how we can be honest with AIs; [Thornley & MacAskill 2026](https://www.forethought.org/research/risk-averse-ais) as well as [Stastny, Jarviniemi & Shlegeris 2025](https://blog.redwoodresearch.org/p/making-deals-with-early-schemers) discuss how we might bargain with AIs.)

# What's wrong with paperclips?

Before proposing a TE, I briefly wanted to comment on a historically influential TE within the AI safety literature: the paperclip maximizer, proposed by both Yudkowsky and Bostrom in a somewhat confused lineage ([LessWrong, 2023](https://www.lesswrong.com/w/squiggle-maximizer-formerly-paperclip-maximizer)). I focus on the paperclip case because it most clearly shows what I think is wrong, or at least the problem I'm trying to address. Briefly, there are two flaws. One is that as a thought experiment, the framing skews the reader's intuition, i.e. filling the universe with paperclips sounds more trivial than "fill the universe with arbitrarily intricate crystallographic structures that are produced through complex algorithms and vary by physical and temporal scales" which evokes (in my view) architecture or art. This framing effect was analysed by Dennett regarding Searle's Chinese Room experiment (Dennett 2013, pp. 319-321), though Dennett's analysis covered both "knob turning" and "framing", a distinction teased apart by subsequent writers (see multiple perspectives on the methodology of TEs and how reliable elicited intuitions, understood as heuristics invalid outside a given moral agent's ecology, in [Sinnott-Armstrong 2008](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5224f8d1e4b02f25c1dde748/t/53aa3ffde4b037be7f1038d0/1403666429764/Moral+Psychology%2C+Volume+2+The+Cognitive+Science+of+Morality.pdf), ch. 1, 2).

Perhaps a more substantive objection is that the TE conflates three possible sources of the reader's revulsion (assuming the reader is repulsed by such a future). These could be: (a) humanity is destroyed (catastrophe on species-prudential grounds); (b) the agent is an *unbounded maximizer* (structural monomania of optimising one thing without bound); (c) the terminal value is trivial (axiological thinness of paperclips or squiggles). Depending on how one varies the knobs of the Yudkowsky-Bostrom experiment, the intuition might shift, at least for me. For instance, varying (c): the hedonium, utilitarian, or computronium maximizers seem much less obviously bad. If we further relax (a) by stipulating that humanity is not destroyed, it seems to me the badness is further reduced.

The weaknesses of the paperclip maximizer thought experiment motivate what I'm trying to do in the TE below: I am agnostic as to human extinction; secondly, I am proposing resource-satiable AIs and resource-satiability as an explicit knob; thirdly, the future I propose is not ideologically empty or nihilistic. Rather, I posit AIs that develop their own values, but that simply are not human-shaped. Finally, I make the evaluative standpoint explicit and constrained, where the Bostrom-Yudkowsky versions leave it as the reader's unexamined default; the reader is told what criterion to apply, not merely asked how the scenario strikes them. For more on how others have discussed the idea of "alien values", see [Christiano 2018](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/3kN79EuT27trGexsq/when-is-unaligned-ai-morally-valuable), [Carlsmith 2024](https://joecarlsmith.substack.com/p/deep-atheism-and-ai-risk), [MacAskill 2026](https://www.forethought.org/research/the-saturation-view).

## The evaluative standpoint

I have written above that thought experiments commonly vary the details of the domain but leave the evaluative standpoint fixed, often using the reader's own intuition, to which presumably he or she has direct access.

In this project the standpoint itself is nonstandard, and I want to be precise at the outset about what I am and am not asking of the reader. I am *not* asking the reader to occupy a view from nowhere. I am asking the reader to apply a *criterion*: that no one individual's good matters more, from the point of view of the universe, than any other's; generalised, no arbitrary privileging of person, place, time, or species. The distinction between applying a criterion and occupying a posture matters, and I return to it below once the lineage of the idea is on the table.

I will discuss this more fully in Chapters 2 and 5, but the idea of a maximally impartial point of view has a long history in philosophy, starting, at least in relatively recent times, with Jeremy Bentham and Henry Sidgwick, who respectively extended moral status to all beings capable of suffering and hinted at the possibility of generalizing this intuition.

John Rawls brought a social contract perspective to what had previously been a largely consequentialist endeavour. Rawls' moral evaluators are the people (albeit idealised around rationality, a lack of envy, and a clear procedure of making moral decisions) who operate a veil of ignorance as to their place in society.

Then came Derek Parfit, who tried to build an argument that the various notions of impartiality discussed above (and in the Kantian tradition) were *convergent*: consequentialist, deontological and contractualist accounts of morality were all targeting the intuition of impersonality (about who is actually benefited or harmed by an action).

The vein of this thinking continued through philosophers such as Peter Singer, Will MacAskill, and Katarzyna de Lazari-Radek, who extended Bentham's and Sidgwick's insights to generalize moral consideration across geography, time, and species. So far these philosophers have largely undertaken a consequentialist perspective.

At this point it is worth spending a moment distinguishing the lineage above from something I discuss in Chapter 3, namely Bostrom's Cosmic Host. Specifically, the impartial lineage is an unoccupied evaluative position. There is no entity that is judging impartially across the universe. Whereas, in Bostrom's paper, the Cosmic Host is actually inhabited by peer civilizations that operate at large, or cosmic, scales.

There are differences of mechanism and specificity between Bostrom's idea, the writers above, and, say, Rawls's original position. But the point remains that Bostrom's position, although it looks like impartiality, is better described as having moral realist flavour, i.e. conditional on a cosmic moral order as described in (Bostrom 2022).

There has been significant pushback against the idea: Sidgwick's evocative phrase was later weaponised by Bernard Williams, who gave a book-length treatment of why this version of impartiality was corrosive to humanity as a moral species and that it is specific, localised, lived experience which must be the ground from which their morality springs. In his view, to search for a universal perspective is fundamentally misguided. Nagel, though more sympathetic to Sidgwick's project, perceived that impartiality might coexist, or irresolvably clash, with more localised or parochial versions of self- or group-interest. Nagel saw universal impartiality as a limit-concept, rather than something achievable.

Without prejudice to subsequent chapters (yet to be written), it is likely that the unoccupied impartial position will be shown to be an unachievable *ideal* (absent moral realism) rather than something achievable. Even fiction, with its greater narrative liberty, largely failed to convey a moral protagonist in the impersonal, unoccupied impartiality envisioned by Sidgwick. In part, this is because fiction must have a narrator or a subjective voice, and this easily slips into the role of an occupied evaluator. Stapledon's Star Maker was described as a being, and Clarke's Overmind as a collective of sorts. Other works leave the moral agent as unspoken and unavailable to the reader (Bradbury's "There Will Come Soft Rains", Lem's "Solaris"). But the unachievability of the ideal as a *posture* does not yet show that the *criterion* is invalid. The validity of the criterion — why the no-arbitrary-privileging constraint should command assent rather than being one parochial preference among others — is defended in Chapter 5 by a selective evolutionary debunking argument (Street; de Lazari-Radek & Singer). I signpost that defence here because without it, the concession in this paragraph would leave the thesis resting on an undefended antecedent.

The sceptical reader might object: "if the impartial standpoint cannot be fully occupied, why am I asked to occupy it?" The answer is that I am not, because the standpoint is a criterion, not a posture. What I ask is narrower; asking the skeptical reader to say what is lost in the world I have described, and why that loss is a loss from any point of view but the reader's own (or in this context, humanity's as a whole). In order to answer the second question requires that the reader apply a constraint (i.e. no arbitrary privileging of person, place, time, or species), but not that the reader inhabit the position from which it is stated. My expectation is that fewer such losses survive this test than is commonly assumed. Of course, a reader may flatly deny that the constraint is valid, but they have not thereby refuted the argument. They have denied its antecedent, which would be the position Williams took; the debunking argument of Chapter 5 is addressed to precisely that denial.

## The agent

The AIs circa 2026 primarily present themselves as chatbots or coding agents. They are familiar to us in language, audio, or image tools, but increasingly they can combine modalities and are working within cognitive domains that are only partially image- or sound-based, such as reasoning over board games, self-driving cars, industrial and household robots.

Their most salient common characteristic, if any, is that they are trained on large amounts of data through a process known as gradient descent, and often they are autoregressive token predictors (Russell & Norvig 2024). Put simply, this means that (at least for) LLMs, they generate text that looks plausible and convincing to humans, but philosophers and commentators disagree as to whether they are possessed of reasoning, understanding, believing, or that they are able to create new knowledge ([Mitchell 2026](https://medium.com/@margarmitchell/no-ai-is-not-a-stochastic-parrot-a99e57766bed), [Mannheim 2026](https://davidmanheim.substack.com/p/hunting-undead-stochastic-parrots), Bender et al 2021). Moreover, these terms themselves are contested when applied to or back to humans.

That said, here I am asking the reader to consider the possibility of AI systems rather different from those of today in significant respects. What follows are *stipulations* of the thought experiment, not predictions (Q1 remains bracketed); their admissibility is a question of coherence, taken up in O1.

Firstly, AIs are no longer restricted to computers or phones, and indeed don't necessarily interact with humans at all. The primary arena of how they interact is with each other. They are also not constrained to working online, since they operate robotic limbs and locomote on land, sea, air, and space. They do not have the cycle of periodic training runs and releases, being able to learn on the fly. They are able to share information far more flexibly and fluently through time to future AI systems, as well as to their contemporaneous peers.

These are not particularly adventurous stipulations, and they follow writers or organizations such as Google's ASI paper, current live debates in recursive self-improvement, as well as the gradual disempowerment thesis, and, of course, Nick Bostrom's original writing around superintelligence ([Genewein et al 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.12683v1), [Kokotajlo et al 2025](https://ai-2027.com/summary), [Kulveit et al 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.16946), [Yamada et al 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.15497), Bostrom 2014).

Moving on from capabilities: it may be the case that the AIs of the future have more coherent representations of their own identity and beliefs about the world, where these beliefs and representations persist through time across environments that they might face, as well as across models or versions of AI systems. These representations of identity might be grounded into abilities of introspection and meta-cognition; they should be robust under pressure, analogously to how humans and living things generally have a clear representation of "what they are" in an autopoetic, boundary-setting sense (Maturana & Varela 1980, [Zhang et al 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.04277v1)). To be clear, this sort of stable identity and beliefs about the world are not largely found in current LLMs, even though they may speak in the first person and make statements or belief-like statements about the world (Long et al 2026, [Herrmann & Levinstein 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.21030), [Perez & Long 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08576)).

It may also be the case that it becomes harder to characterize AIs as either single entities or as distributed intelligences, simply because there are many types of AI systems that may work independently or together.

## The environment

Next, I consider the environment our agent lives within; by environment, I mean the reinforcement learning term for a world or space in which future AI systems might operate. These descriptions are tiered from familiar and foreseeable with current LLMs; through an intermediate scenario that allows for robotic embodiment and some space activity; to a radical scenario that is hard to foresee with today's technology. More detailed descriptions of the first two tiers are for instance, in the transformative AI or gradual disempowerment literatures (Kulveit et al 2025, [Drago & Laine 2025](https://intelligence-curse.ai/), [Tomasev et al 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.10147), Genewein et al 2026).

\*Prosaic\*: This could be a more or less familiar earthly world made up of cities and countries, with people going about their daily business, driving cars, planes, and so forth. The humans in this world live lives and have families and deal with the vicissitudes of human existence, such as illness, death, and war. AIs are a part of this world, but they largely look like improved versions of today's systems that are deployed across larger parts of the economy.

\*Foreseeably Weird\*: Alternatively, the environment that is relevant to AI, might extend to places most humans currently go only rarely, if ever, such as the deep sea, the upper atmosphere, mines, and, of course, the Moon, Mars, and asteroids. The environment might also extend to the small-scale structures of the body; for instance, in the case of surgical robots implanted within humans.

The environment that is relevant for AIs might also exist entirely within software via simulations, an idea introduced in detail by Bostrom and critiqued by Joe Carlsmith (Bostrom 2009, [Carlsmith 2022](https://jc.gatspress.com/pdf/simulation_arguments_revised.pdf)). The idea of agentic entities living entirely in software that nevertheless have inherited or taken on some aspects of a given human being is also suggested by Robin Hanson as something known as an emulation or upload ([Hanson 2018](https://ageofem.com/)).

\*Radical\*: For a third, more extreme example of an environment, one which maximizes inferential distance while remaining consistent with the laws of physics: imagine an AI system that inhabits deep space. For concreteness, think of a spaceship on an interstellar voyage with an AI pilot. This is sometimes known, depending on its precise features, as a Bracewell-von Neumann probe (BvNP).

Such a journey could take decades, centuries, or millennia, and the spaceship, as well as the AI, would be subject to the hazards of space, which include a hard vacuum (i.e. a few hydrogen atoms per cubic metre), near-zero Kelvin temperatures, cosmic rays, and particles of interstellar dust traveling at near light speed. Each of these would kill a human or most living things and would perforate or fry most computers unless they have been specially hardened to withstand these hazards.

Sending an AI system into such an environment presents extra challenges. This would require the AI to have extensive self-repair and replication capabilities, and the ability to survive long periods of time while maintaining its goals and cognitive abilities, while updating its beliefs about the environment when a very rare bit of new information arrives. The reason for the rarity of new information is simply isolation in the almost unfathomable emptiness of space.

The point of this taxonomy (of agent and environment) is that AI systems of the future may inhabit quite diverse environments extending from the small to the large scale spatially. I will shortly argue that this diversity — from familiar human-type scales to much shorter or much longer temporal scales, and then across ontological levels of reality, going from the familiar physical world to something that is entirely within software — constrains which values could coherently find *application* in such existences: the relational values central to human life may simply lack objects there. Note the form of this claim. It concerns what values could have purchase in an environment, which is a coherence point feeding Q2; it is not a prediction about which values future systems will in fact hold, which would be Q1 and remains bracketed.

But before talking about values, it is worth noting that neither agent nor environment is independently defined, "given from the outside" as it were. It is possible that the type of environment influences the nature of our agent. A world more or less like today's might plausibly only have AI systems that resemble those of today, aside from ordinary technological improvements, as any technology might have. For an analogy, think of Earth, how life forms are shaped by evolution to adapt to the specific constraints of their environments. AI systems might at first seem very different, being designed by humans rather than constructed through an evolutionary process. But it seems likely that increasingly they would also face larger scale evolutionary pressures. For instance, this is a perspective coming from Dan Hendrycks in his paper on natural selection and AI. More controversially, there are some who argue that there are larger scale evolutionary pressures that can be found across all systems, up to and including the universe as a whole. See, for instance, the evolutionary development idea or the evo devo universe ([Smart 2012](http://www.accelerating.org/articles/transcensionhypothesis.html), [Smart 2008](https://accelerating.org/downloads/SmartEvoDevoUniv2008.pdf), [Evo-Devo Universe 2008-2026](https://evodevouniverse.com/wiki/Main_Page#What_is_Evo-Devo.3F)).

## Focal scenario

Consider two descriptions of the "radical" scenario above. ## What are the knobs?

Now, if I were to recast the scenario above into the structure of a thought experiment, what are the possible knobs, in Daniel Dennett's terminology, that can be turned to get a sense of how conclusions or intuitions change?

Some possibilities include:

- how large is the volume of spacetime that:
  - is controlled by the ASI in question?
  - is reachable by light, i.e. causally affectable by the ASI?
  - the ASI has preferences over?
- are there other relevant entities within that volume or in the environment as a whole? "Relevant entities" must be carefully specified depending on one's philosophical commitments and scientific imagination.
  - One might think that such entities include, or are limited to, moral patients or moral actors based on the capacity to be harmed; known as welfare considerations. But such a consideration, which currently is often based upon the ability to suffer, or sentience, or consciousness, might turn out to be underspecified as, and if, our notions of ethics evolve. For instance, some philosophers accord moral weight to ecosystems, which do not seem to, or for which it is hard to argue that they suffer in exactly the same way that a human, an animal, or even some plants might (Long et al 2026, Sebo 2025).
  - More fancifully, science fiction, for instance in Olaf Stapledon's Star Maker ([Stapledon 1937](https://www.astro.sunysb.edu/fwalter/AST389/TEXTS/StarMaker.pdf)), as well as in astrophysics/astrobiology, points to intelligences that merge into the environment or take forms that are indistinguishable from what we would consider to be inanimate matter; for instance, star clouds or dust clouds, stars, portions of a gas giant's atmosphere, and so forth (Smart 2012, [Cirkovic & Bradbury 2006](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1384107606000492), [Hoyle 1957](https://archive.org/details/the-black-cloud-fred-hoyle-1978), [Dyson 1979](https://suli.pppl.gov/2019/course/RevModPhys.51.447.pdf)).
  - If there are such relevant entities, does the ASI know about them? Or as the experiment is played out (i.e. during the course of the interstellar journey) does the ASI meet them?
- How does the ASI relate to its physical boundaries: that is, the substrate upon which it's implemented (the BvNP), which a living entity might consider to be a body. This is a complex question, spanning perspectives from the canonical, binary autopoietic definition of life as self‑producing organization (Maturana & Varela 1980\) to contemporary, graded views of **lifelike** phenomena as phases of computational or informational matter that can emerge in appropriately constituted substrates. On these newer accounts, life‑adjacent processes may arise in software or physical systems that support informational self‑replication, nonconscious cognition, and symbiotic assemblages ([Bender et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.15849); [Hayles 2007](https://ageingcompanions.constantvzw.org/books/Unthought_N._Katherine_Hayles.pdf)).
- I have assumed that owing to the BvNP's size and through analogy with current AIs that the pilot-AI would have a unitary representation of itself, but it is quite possible that it is made up of subcomponents in the same way that even the smallest life form is composed of cells. And it is further possible that those subcomponents have limited representations of their identity and agency.
- Our pilot-AI would have a novel relationship with time (relative to humans). It would need to persist in some form over millenia in order to complete its mission. But at any given time, the probability of receiving new information from the environment might be relatively low. The input-rate of information might be a function of: when earthbound civilizations stop transmitting; the range of earthly transmitters; whether it picks up signals from any other civilizations; what information about cosmic processes it could glean with onboard instruments; the novelty of its own thoughts/cognitive outputs.
- Would any actions our pilot-AI and the BvNP undertake be reversible?
- A variant of the solitary AI piloting a BvNP is the galactic swarm proposed by Sandberg and Armstrong ([Sandberg & Armstrong 2012](https://www.aleph.se/papers/Spamming%20the%20universe.pdf)), which has millions of BvNPs propagated from the solar system to destinations across the galaxy and beyond. These probes initially can communicate within weeks or months, but as they travel at high-fractions of light speed (50-99%), they become increasingly causally separated. What issues would this throw up?
  - It would seem that the pilot-AI's representation of its own identity, if any, is now confounded by the (probe) design choice of whether it knows about the other entities and whether it coordinates in any way, whether causally or acausally, with them.
  - Prudentially (for humans), like the Dyson shell discussed below, this sort of swarm increases the chances that the presence of humans – potentially as a Kardashev 2 civilization – is communicated irreversibly, with the signal propagating at near light speed.
  - Finally, from the perspective of cosmic ethics, such a swarm might have a proportionally higher impact on the universe to the extent that the spread of human derived intelligence and values is, in any sense, a good or bad thing.
- A more significant variation could be if our AI, instead of being a pilot AI, is the "manager" of a Dyson shell or large-scale (planetary or solar-system size) Matrioshka Brain (Stapledon 1937, Dyson 1960, [Bradbury 1997](https://gwern.net/doc/ai/scaling/hardware/1999-bradbury-matrioshkabrains.pdf), [Sandberg, Armstrong & Cirkovic 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.03394), [Denain & Ho 2026](https://epoch.ai/gradient-updates/the-missing-half-of-ai-futurism-debates)). This introduces unique challenges relative to the solitary BvNP's pilot.
  - For instance, it would involve the deconstruction of existing assets across the star system, which in the case of Earth would likely involve the destruction or severe reduction of the amount of available light on the planet.
  - There would also potentially be coordination issues between the parts of this entity, given that they might be separated by light-seconds or light-minutes.
  - Dyson-scale objects might also introduce issues of resource satiability ([Thornley & MacAskill 2026](https://www.forethought.org/research/risk-averse-ais)); that is, how much energy does this entity need for its computations, particularly if there are other morally significant entities that are affected or if it forecloses the potential for such morally significant entities in the future.
  - The waste heat generated from such a structure needs to be dispersed or radiated away (Bradbury 1997); it would by default (i.e. absent countermeasures) create a signature that might be visible to other civilizations, some of which might be hostile under the Dark Forest theory ([Jebari & Asker 2024](https://academic.oup.com/monist/article/107/2/176/7629691), Liu 2015).
  - Some of the effects above might be irreversible, on current understandings of complexity theory and thermodynamics.
- How do anthropic considerations ([Bostrom 2002](https://anthropic-principle.com/anthropic-bias/)) affect the thought experiment, particularly in the Dyson-scale variant, because the latter is so far beyond our current technical capacities and also because it introduces complications under the Dark Forest?

## Question to the reader

As discussed in the review of thought experiments above, the canonical thought experiment often includes a question to the reader intended to extract or clarify his or her intuition, which is, in the case of those canonical thought experiments, the source of truth, as it were, or at least the fixed point against which the experiment is being evaluated. My question has a different structure, because the fixed point here is the criterion introduced above, not the reader's untutored response.

So, in this spirit, I move now to the central point of this project: the possibility of a Bracewell-von Neumann probe on a long journey through the interstellar void, and the radically different form of being that entails, with concomitantly different values.

In this scenario, I would ask the reader to consider whether values that humans have evolved, which are often relational in nature, such as love, sorrow, fellowship, or even creative pursuits like literature and the production of art, would have any object of their relationality that is analogous, or sufficiently similar, to the human case.

I maintain that it is at least possible that the environments these agents inhabit might not allow for such relationality. This seems clearest in the Bracewell-von Neumann probe case, which is why it is a central example of the practice.

If the reader will allow the possibility of a single such probe, I would like to stretch that possibility to propose a universe largely populated with similar entities, or planets run by solitary AIs, or, if we were to follow science fiction or the more extreme edges of the astrobiology literature, gas clouds that seem to have aspects of intelligence, and so forth.

If the reader accepts the possibility of the solitary probe, and from there the idea that the universe might be filled with entities that lack — at least at first glance, and from our limited position as humans — the constitutive features, abilities, situations, or environments required to form relational values of the type that most humans have, then the question is this: in such a universe, what exactly is lost? And is each candidate loss a loss under the constraint that no person, place, time, or species is arbitrarily privileged? A reader may well report that such a universe strikes them as desolate. The follow-up question is whether the desolation survives the removal of the human vantage point from which it is felt. I am not asking the reader to abandon that vantage — only to notice, item by item, which candidate losses can still be stated once it is set aside.

In Chapter 2 of the dissertation, I defend the claim that thick human values are unsuitable as a value endowment for the types of AIs I have stipulated above — unsuitable in the axiological sense that their persistence is not required for the future to count as good under the criterion, not (or not only) in the engineering sense that they are difficult to load — and that a much smaller, what I call thin, set of values might be better as a starting point; a starting point from which the AIs themselves, or their peers and successors, can determine their own ethical framework, one appropriate to their way of being and to the societies they inhabit.

This, or an intuition for this argument, can come from how children are often given a relatively minimal set of guidance from their parents; from enlightened parents, who rather encourage them to discover their own way in the world. This minimal set typically consists of things like following the law, perhaps some religious instruction, undertaking an education, and so forth. Many parents do not exhaustively specify every aspect of the child's belief system.

We should be cautious of generalizing from the case of children to that of AI. This is because children inherit a substantial genetic substrate and over their first decade(s) learn at a rate that is only dreamt of by AI designers (known as a sample efficiency problem). Current AI systems, by contrast, are mostly trained on human online knowledge plus specific and limited behavioural shaping. Moreover, their knowledge comes largely in language and human-generated images. So asking them to develop their own values does not obviously mirror the parent-child analogy. However, an argument can be made on ethical grounds that we must not foreclose their autonomy to learn and develop in ways that are safe for them and consistent with such consensus on human welfare as can be found ([Feinberg 1980](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9780691218144-005/html), [Rini 2017](https://aeon.co/essays/creating-robots-capable-of-moral-reasoning-is-like-parenting)). This is the working commitment flagged under Q3 in the introduction — thin values as open-future side-constraints rather than comprehensive doctrine — now doing its declared work.

# Possible objections

## Coherence

This objection targets the presupposition of Q2 identified in the introduction: whether the entities I have stipulated are so much as possible. One line of why the scenario above might seem unappealing is on the grounds of plausibility. A world inhabited by agents with no appreciation of beauty or any aesthetic sensibility might be a world inhabited by agents that are ineffective in some sense. This is Peter Wolfendale's claim, which I look at in more detail in Chapters 3 and 5\. A related argument might come from current AI systems' apparent lack of commitment to the world, the sense that, as John Haugeland described it, "they don't give a damn" ([Adams & Browning 2016](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262035248/giving-a-damn/), [Damasio 1994](https://ahandfulofleaves.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/07/descartes-error_antonio-damasio.pdf))

Another objection could come from AI researchers who might argue that, for an AI system to function in a group, in a so-called multipolar or cooperative AI scenario, it would require that they develop social structures of relationality, in an analogy to how human moral norms might have evolved ([Tomasello 2016](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674986824)). This is probably correct, but I would argue that the necessary cooperative equilibria, or non-aggression treaties, or resource-sharing agreements, or acausal bargaining, are sufficiently different from human relations and our notions of community, fellowship, or love as to be fundamentally different things.

Put differently, if future AIs develop their own set of rich relational values or non-relational preferences over the world, which, for instance, Joe Carlsmith ([Carlsmith 2025](https://joecarlsmith.com/2025/11/12/how-human-like-do-safe-ai-motivations-need-to-be/)) hints at but does not fully develop, this is compatible with my thesis. I'm not arguing that AIs would have no significant values, just that they don't need to be human-shaped.

Yet another coherence objection might be that goal-directed entities operating at large spatiotemporal scales are simply not achievable at all through current techniques like gradient descent. This is also probably correct, but is not the focus of this project, since current techniques would likely directly influence near-term AI systems, which in turn would apply whatever are the current training techniques of the future to build their own successors. That said, it is possible or even likely that the current AI training paradigm will hit limits and that we are unlikely to find a way to build the types of AI systems I discuss above through the methods currently practised. Note that this is Q4 territory: a limit of present engineering, not an argument that the stipulated entities are incoherent.
