# Task 1 — Demolition

Four problems, ordered by how likely they are to still be standing in two years.

**Problem 1. The criterion is empty, and every way of filling it either trivializes Q2 or refutes it.**

(i) "No arbitrary privileging of person, place, time, or species" is a *formal* constraint on how goods are weighed. It contains no account of what the goods are. But the test you build on it — "identify what is lost in such a world, and test which candidate losses can still be stated once the human vantage point is set aside" — requires substantive verdicts about which absences are losses. A formal constraint cannot generate those verdicts. So something else must, and there are only two candidates. If you complete the criterion the way your entire cited lineage does (Bentham → Sidgwick → Singer → de Lazari-Radek: welfarist consequentialism), Q2 is *trivially* true and was proved in 1874: Sidgwick's point of view of the universe never required thick human values, and your own draft concedes the shortcut — "the hedonium, utilitarian, or computronium maximizers seem much less obviously bad." On that horn the BvNP is decoration; a hedonist needs no probe. If instead you complete it with any axiology on which complex goods are agent-neutrally valuable — Moore's organic unities, Hurka's perfectionism — then love, achievement, and aesthetic engagement are impartial goods, their absence is an impartial loss stated in fully vantage-free vocabulary ("achievement is good" privileges no person, place, time, or species), and Q2 is false or at best undefended. The fight you actually have to win is *against the agent-neutral value of thick goods*, and the introduction shows no awareness that this is where the fight is. Note also that "arbitrary" is itself criterion-relative: privileging sentient beings over rocks is a privileging — is it arbitrary? You need a theory of principled-vs-arbitrary distinctions, and that theory is the smuggled axiology again.

(ii) Severity: embarrassing if raised cold at a viva, because the examiner can run both horns in two minutes.

(iii) Tractability: plausibly intractable *within the stated architecture*. "The thesis holds under the criterion" presupposes the criterion determines verdicts; it doesn't. Fixing this means naming the completing axiology, at which point the conditional structure is really "conditional on impartial welfarism," and you should say so.

(iv) The examiner cites: Sidgwick (whose impartial point of view was always paired with hedonism doing the axiological work), Moore *Principia Ethica*, Hurka *Perfectionism*.

**Problem 2. The redescription test is rigged, and its verdicts equivocate between "not a loss" and "not sayable."**

(i) You ask which candidate losses "can still be stated once the human vantage point is set aside." But on the standard analysis — Williams's own — thick concepts are *essentially* perspectival: world-guided and action-guiding from within a form of life, non-detachable (McDowell), entangled (Putnam). So "losses stated in thick vocabulary fail the test" is analytic. Your prediction that "fewer such losses survive this test than is commonly assumed" is not a discovery about the world; it is a restatement of the definition of thickness. Worse, the test equivocates. Suppose "love is lost" cannot be stated vantage-free. Two readings: (a) metaphysical — the loss isn't real; (b) epistemic — the loss is real but our articulation of it is perspectival. The inference from (b) to (a) requires an anti-realist or verificationist premise about value ("only what can be stated from nowhere is evaluatively real") that you never state, and — this is the trap — that the Chapter 5 debunking argument *cannot* supply, because the debunking argument needs enough realism to spare the impartiality criterion itself. You can't be anti-realist about thick value and realist about the criterion using one argument.

(ii) Severity: this is exactly the category you asked about — looks fine in an introduction, collapses when the supporting chapter is written. Substantive now, embarrassing at thesis.

(iii) Tractability: intractable *as an eliminative one-directional filter*. Convertible into something defensible only by making it symmetric (see Task 2, Route C).

(iv) Williams, *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy* ch. 8; Putnam, *The Collapse of the Fact/Value Dichotomy*; McDowell, "Non-Cognitivism and Rule-Following."

**Problem 3. Your own lineage condemns your focal scenario. Astronomical waste.**

(i) A satiable, solitary probe drifting through the void, converting a rounding error of cosmic resources into whatever it values, is — from the impartial consequentialist standpoint you inherit from Sidgwick through Singer — a *catastrophe of foregone value*. Bostrom made this argument famous ("Astronomical Waste," 2003), and Bostrom is cited a dozen times in this draft while this paper is absent. The problem is structural, not bibliographic: resource satiability appears in your thin set as a virtue, but under total impartial consequentialism unbounded resource acquisition for value-production is closer to *mandatory*. Your criterion, cashed the only way your lineage cashes it, may deliver the verdict that the probe world is bad — not because thick values are missing but because almost everything is missing. Escape via person-affecting axiology imports the non-identity problem and the full instability of Parfit Part IV. And this compounds the known debunking-selectivity issue in a way I think you're underestimating: de Lazari-Radek & Singer's selectivity move spares *impartial maximizing benevolence* specifically (on the ground that it's fitness-costly and hence not debunkable). It does not spare side-constraints, and it certainly does not spare satiability, which has an obvious prudential-evolutionary pedigree. So the one selective-debunking tool on your shelf, if it works, yields Sidgwickian maximization — which condemns the satiable probe. The debunking chapter isn't just unwritten; its most defensible version is an argument *against* your thin set's structure.

(ii) Severity: embarrassing — it's an internal contradiction with the cited tradition, and Bostrom 2003 is the first thing a hostile examiner reaches for.

(iii) Tractability: a chapter at minimum, and probably a reformulated criterion. Not patchable.

(iv) Bostrom 2003; Parfit, *Reasons and Persons* Part IV; Greaves on population axiology.

**Problem 4. The brackets are load-bearing: whether the probe world is good turns on exactly the questions you've bracketed.**

(i) "I posit AIs that develop their own values, but that simply are not human-shaped." Values *held* do not generate value *realized* unless the holder is the kind of thing whose states matter — a welfare subject, or at minimum a valuer in Korsgaard's sense. If the probe-AI is a welfare subject with rich satisfied non-human values, the world is good under impartial welfarism — but then welfarism does all the work and Q2 is Problem 1's trivial horn. If it is not a welfare subject (and your cold register flirts with stipulating exactly this), then what makes the world good *at all* under any impartial criterion? You have bracketed consciousness/sentience as "not my question," but Q2's truth value is not independent of it: a world's goodness under impartiality depends on whether it contains subjects of good. Bracketing patienthood is legitimate for Q1, Q3, Q4; for Q2 it removes the truthmaker. Related modal scope error: "the relational values central to human life may simply lack objects there" is offered as neutralizing those values, but on standard axiology a world in which friendship is *impossible* is pro tanto worse than one where it's realized — unrealizability is a form of the deficit, not an exemption from evaluation. A barren universe lacks objects for every value; that doesn't make it no-worse than a flourishing one. You need the premise "V applies evaluatively to W only if W contains possible objects of V," and that premise is false on both totalist and perfectionist views.

(ii) Severity: substantive, rising to embarrassing when combined with Problem 1.

(iii) Tractability: requires unbracketing — you must stipulate the probe's patienthood status and accept the consequences either way. Not a paragraph fix.

(iv) Korsgaard ("value enters with valuers"); Moore *Principia* §50 for the opposite pole (see Task 4).

**Problem 5 (escalation of a known issue). Register/stipulation parity may be impossible in principle, not just hard in drafting.**

Part B treats the experiencelessness slip as a correctable violation. I think the audit condition itself may be unsatisfiable: warmth in prose just *is* the implicature of conative and phenomenal predicates ("yearning," "patience," "loneliness" attribute mental states whether or not you stipulate them). A warm register with genuinely identical stipulations and identical implicatures is not a distinct register. To run the Dennett audit you need a principled stipulation/implicature boundary for literary prose, and no such theory exists. Severity: substantive; tractability: unknown, and since the two-framings device is your methodological centrepiece, "unknown" is bad. At minimum the chapter needs to say what counts as content and what counts as register, before the paragraphs are commissioned.

# Task 2 — Alternative routes

**Route C (recommended): make the redescription test symmetric — cold-frame humanity.** Add a third passage: the *human* world rendered at the same inferential distance the probe gets — pair-bonding chemistry, grooming coalitions, status hierarchies, mortality-salience rituals, described in the register of an indifferent xenoethologist. The question to the reader becomes comparative: *state what the human world has that the probe world lacks, in vocabulary that survives cold-framing of both.* This fixes what patching cannot: (a) it converts the rigged one-directional filter of Problem 2 into a parity test — if cold-framing doesn't annihilate the value of human life, it can't annihilate the probe world's, and if it does annihilate both, the reader has learned that the desolation was register all along, which is your actual claim; (b) it uses your Dennett-audit machinery bidirectionally, so the device earns its keep; (c) it partially answers the knobs-cut-both-ways worry, since the reader's warm attachment to humanity is now explicitly on the table as a framing effect. Cost: you must be willing to let the reader conclude "both worlds survive cold-framing," which is fine — Q2 only needs the probe world to pass, not humanity to fail.

**Route B: demote the thought experiment and promote the premises.** State the argument as two explicit premises — (P1) impartial welfarism (or whatever completes the criterion after Problem 1 is resolved); (P2) non-human welfare subjects with satisfied non-human-shaped values are possible — and let Q2 follow deductively. The probe then does one honest job: defeating *imaginative resistance* to P2, i.e., it is a de-biasing instrument against paperclip-shaped intuitions, not evidence. This fixes Problem 1 (the axiology is named), Problem 4 (P2 stipulates patienthood), and disarms the "your TE is rhetoric" attack by agreeing with it in advance and relocating the argumentative weight. Cost: the philosophy becomes less novel — it is Sidgwick plus digital minds — and the chapter must own that the contribution is the *methodological* apparatus (the audit, the symmetric framing) rather than the axiological conclusion. Given a practice-based PhD, that trade may be favourable.

The current route — eliminative test, unnamed axiology, solitary probe as evidence — is the weakest of the three, because Problems 1–4 all attack it at joints that patching can't reach. C keeps your scenario central; B keeps your conclusion safe. They combine.

# Task 3 — The single strongest objection

*Examiner:* "Your criterion has a lineage — Bentham, Sidgwick, Singer, de Lazari-Radek — and that lineage is impartial maximizing welfarism. Take the criterion in that, its only articulated form, and a pincer closes. First jaw: your thesis is trivially true and roughly 150 years old. Sidgwick's point of view of the universe never had any use for the full complexity of human cultural inheritance; your own draft concedes hedonium worlds 'seem much less obviously bad.' No probe required. Second jaw: your exhibit is condemned by your own criterion. A satiable, solitary system idling through the void while the accessible universe's resources go unconverted into welfare is, from the point of view of the universe, astronomical waste — Bostrom, 2003, the one Bostrom paper you don't cite. So the criterion, taken substantively, makes Q2 boring and the BvNP world *bad*. Your escape is to retreat to the criterion as purely formal — 'no arbitrary privileging' with no axiology attached. But a formal constraint returns no verdicts, and your central test — what is lost? — becomes unanswerable. So: name the axiology that completes your criterion, and then tell me what the probe adds to an argument that goes through, or fails, on that axiology alone."

*Author's most likely reply:* the criterion is formal, and the test is eliminative — I don't need a positive axiology, only the filter: candidate losses that cannot be stated without the human vantage don't survive.

*Examiner's follow-up:* "Then apply your filter symmetrically. State the *goods* of the probe world without any vantage. 'It has values of its own' — held by what kind of subject, mattering to whom, from where? If inarticulability-from-nowhere disqualifies losses, it disqualifies gains by the same clause, and your filtered world contains neither. Your thesis then reads: a world evacuated of statable value is not bad. That is not impartiality. That is nihilism wearing Sidgwick's coat — and Williams, whom you think you've conditionalized away, told you this is where the road ends."

# Task 4 — Missing interlocutors

**Moore, *Principia Ethica* §50.** The beautiful-uninhabited-world thought experiment is the canonical treatment of whether a world without experiencers can bear value — which is precisely the question your cold register and your experiencelessness wobble raise. Its absence from a chapter about the value of a possibly-subjectless probe world will read as a gap, not an omission of completism. Moore is also the sharpest version of Problem 1's second horn.

**Samuel Scheffler, *Death and the Afterlife* / *Why Worry About Future Generations?*** The strongest contemporary opponent of Q2 who is *not* Williams: his argument that the "collective afterlife" — humanity's continuation specifically — is a condition of much present valuing, and his conservatism about value, is the position an examiner will assume you've read. Engaging him also sharpens your successionism differentiation, since Scheffler is the anti-successionist your third camp never answers.

**Bostrom, "Astronomical Waste" (2003).** See Problem 3. Not citing it while citing five other Bostroms looks evasive even if it isn't.

**Population axiology — Parfit *Reasons and Persons* Part IV; Arrhenius; Greaves.** "What is lost?" is a comparison between worlds with disjoint populations. Loss relative to which baseline — the actual world, the best feasible continuation, a person-affecting comparison? Comparative evaluation of futures with different inhabitants is population ethics' home terrain, and you currently run such comparisons with no machinery at all. Parfit appears in your draft only for the convergence thesis, which is the least relevant Parfit.

**Railton, "Alienation, Consequentialism, and the Demands of Morality."** Not a threat — a resource you're missing. The sophisticated-consequentialist answer to Williams is the closest existing model for your criterion-not-posture move, and an examiner who knows it will ask why you reinvented a weaker version.

One meta-point, since you asked what fails in two years: Problems 1, 2, and 4 are the same problem seen from three sides — the chapter defers the substantive axiology to a criterion that cannot hold it. Everything else is repairable. That one is a decision, not a repair: name the axiology, restructure around Route B/C, and accept that the conditional becomes "conditional on impartial welfarism" rather than "conditional on impartiality." The draft's current form postpones that decision in a way an upgrade panel may allow and a final viva will not.
