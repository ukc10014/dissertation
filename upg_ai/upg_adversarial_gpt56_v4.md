The chapter is not philosophically stable in its present form. The acknowledged debunking problem is serious, but it is not yet the deepest problem. Several earlier transitions fail before the argument reaches evolutionary debunking.

I am not counting the known parity failure, the solitary-versus-multipolar relationality issue, the Cosmic Host ambiguity, the parent-child analogy, predictive leakage, or the distinction from successionism except where they expose a deeper structural problem.

# Task 1: Independent demolition attempt

## 1. The impartiality condition does not determine what is good

**Text interpreted:**

> “The thesis in this project is that thick human values [...] need not survive for a future to count as good, when that future is evaluated impartially.”

and:

> “no arbitrary privileging of person, place, time, or species.”

### The problem

This is a formal constraint on evaluation, not an axiology. It can tell you that like interests should not receive different weights merely because one belongs to a human, an alien, or a digital mind. It does not tell you:

* what the relevant interests or goods are;
* whether only welfare matters;
* whether beauty, knowledge, achievement, autonomy, diversity, relationships, rights, or virtue have impersonal value;
* whether goodness is total, average, prioritarian, egalitarian, person-affecting, perfectionist, negative-welfarist, or pluralist;
* how values are aggregated;
* what makes a future sufficiently good rather than merely positive in one respect.

Rawlsian procedural impartiality, Sidgwickian universal benevolence, agent-neutral reasons, temporal neutrality, equal moral status, anonymity in social aggregation, and impersonal value are not interchangeable. Rawls’s original position is a device for selecting principles governing the basic structure of a society; it is not by itself a cosmic axiology. Nagel explicitly treats personal and impersonal standpoints as distinct sources whose reconciliation creates the problem, rather than deriving all value from impersonality. Scheffler argues that morality can recognize relationship-dependent, project-dependent, and membership-dependent reasons without simply abandoning morality. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy][1])

The draft therefore faces a quantifier dilemma:

1. **Existential interpretation:** There is *some* impartial axiology under which a nonhuman successor world is good. This is nearly trivial. Construct an impartial axiology that values the probe’s flourishing or goals regardless of substrate.
2. **Universal interpretation:** Such a world is good under *all* plausible impartial axiologies. This is plainly not established and is probably false.
3. **Robust interpretation:** It is good under a sufficiently broad or justified family of impartial axiologies. This is potentially interesting, but the family and robustness condition do nearly all the work.

The phrase “some impartial standard” in the fixed thesis points toward the first interpretation. The chapter’s rhetoric points toward the third. The argument currently slides between them.

### Severity

**Substantive.** An examiner should stop the argument here.

### Tractability

**Plausibly intractable in the current architecture.** A full chapter could specify an axiology, but doing so would abandon the claim that the no-arbitrary-privileging condition itself is enough. The project would become a defence of a particular impartial value theory rather than an application of a neutral criterion.

### Likely examiner sources

Rawls, *A Theory of Justice*; Nagel, *Equality and Partiality*; Scheffler, *The Rejection of Consequentialism* and “Morality and Reasonable Partiality”; Broome, *Weighing Goods* and *Weighing Lives*.

## 2. “Removing the human vantage” is not impartiality; it is a substantive exclusion rule

**Text interpreted:**

> “asking the skeptical reader to say what is lost in the world I have described, and why that loss is a loss from any point of view but the reader’s own”

and:

> “which candidate losses can still be stated once [the human vantage] is set aside.”

and:

> “whether the desolation survives the removal of the human vantage point from which it is felt.”

### The problem

Impartiality does not require goods to be describable independently of every particular standpoint. It requires that the goods and claims associated with different standpoints be considered without unjustified bias.

The value of friendship is relational and indexed to particular people. That does not prevent an impartial evaluator from recognizing that their friendship is valuable. Grief is accessible because of a specific attachment. That does not make the loss unreal from an impartial perspective. An impartial evaluator can recognize human-specific, octopus-specific, or digital-mind-specific flourishing without having to translate each into a standpoint-free residue.

Your test instead imposes **vantage-invariance**:

> A candidate value counts only if its value can still be stated after the standpoint that makes it intelligible has been removed.

That rule excludes much of the disputed material by construction. Love, loyalty, aesthetic traditions, language, historical identity and forms of life will predictably fail a test that first removes their constitutive relations. The conclusion then does not show that they lack impartial value. It shows that they cannot survive a procedure designed to strip away the conditions under which they exist.

This is not an escape from Williams. It misidentifies his objection. Williams need not deny that each person matters equally in some moral sense. He can accept impartial moral considerations while denying that all genuine reasons must be standpoint-independent, or that impersonal reasons have unconditional authority over projects and relationships. Nagel likewise treats the objective standpoint as incomplete without the personal one. Scheffler’s reasonable partiality is a direct counterexample to the claim that accepting partial reasons simply amounts to denying the impartial antecedent. ([PhilPapers][2])

Your sentence:

> “They have denied its antecedent, which would be the position Williams took”

is therefore false or, at minimum, undefended. A critic can accept:

* that humans should not receive additional weight merely because they are human;
* that future digital minds can matter equally;
* that distant times and places matter;

while also holding that human relationships, histories and achievements are real goods wherever their bearers exist.

### Severity

**Substantive.** This is the most direct internal objection to the method.

### Tractability

**Plausibly intractable.** The redescription test is currently the mechanism that produces the desired conclusion. Correcting it would require replacing the method, not adding qualifications.

### Likely examiner sources

Williams, “Persons, Character and Morality” and *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy*; Nagel, *The View from Nowhere* and *Equality and Partiality*; Scheffler, “Morality and Reasonable Partiality.”

## 3. “Counts as good” has no defined comparison class or threshold

**Text interpreted:**

> “for a future to count as good”

and:

> “in such a universe, what exactly is lost?”

and:

> “I would like to stretch that possibility to propose a universe largely populated with similar entities.”

### The problem

A future is not normally assessed as “good” in isolation. You need to specify at least one of the following:

* better than nonexistence;
* better than an empty universe;
* better than continued human civilization;
* better than a mixed human and nonhuman future;
* good enough to be permissible;
* sufficiently good not to constitute catastrophe;
* optimal among accessible futures;
* containing some positive value, although much worse than its alternatives.

These are radically different claims.

A solitary probe might contain a small amount of positive welfare, agency, knowledge or achievement. It does not follow that a universe consisting almost entirely of such probes is good relative to a universe containing rich human, alien and digital flourishing. Nor does it follow that replacing one with the other is acceptable.

The question “what is lost?” suppresses the counterfactual. Lost relative to what?

* If the comparison is empty interstellar space, adding a worthwhile probe may be an improvement.
* If the comparison is a rich, diverse civilization, the opportunity cost may be enormous.
* If the comparison contains humans, extinction and replacement re-enter.
* If the comparison excludes humans by stipulation, the central conclusion has partly been built into the baseline.

Broome has argued that goodness may be reducible to betterness and that there is no absolute zero of goodness. Whether or not that exact analysis is accepted, it exposes the problem with “counts as good” as an unanalysed threshold. Once the compared futures contain different beings, the project also enters population ethics, including the nonidentity problem, total versus person-affecting evaluation, and the valuation of additional worthwhile lives. ([PhilArchive][3])

There is also a composition fallacy:

1. One solitary probe could have a worthwhile existence.
2. Therefore, a universe largely composed of similar probes could be good.

The value of a world need not be the linear sum of independently valuable occupants. Repetition can crowd out diversity, relations, discovery, cultural differentiation and alternative forms of flourishing. A locally worthwhile agent does not establish a globally worthwhile monoculture.

### Severity

**Substantive.**

### Tractability

**Fixable with a chapter.** You need a defined comparison class, a threshold notion of “good,” and at least a provisional population axiology. The resulting argument may support only a weaker conclusion, such as “contains positive value” or “is not axiologically empty.”

### Likely examiner sources

Broome, “Goodness Is Reducible to Betterness” and *Weighing Lives*; Parfit, Part IV of *Reasons and Persons*; the subsequent population-ethics literature.

## 4. The draft equivocates between having values and instantiating value

**Text interpreted:**

> “the future I propose is not ideologically empty or nihilistic. Rather, I posit AIs that develop their own values”

and:

> “future AIs develop their own set of rich relational values or non-relational preferences over the world”

### The problem

This does not answer the paperclip problem. A paperclip maximizer also has a value. It may have:

* a stable terminal commitment;
* complex representations;
* reflective endorsement;
* elaborate instrumental norms;
* a long history of self-modification;
* intricate aesthetic appreciation of paperclip morphology.

None of this establishes that paperclip production is valuable.

The draft moves between:

1. **Psychological value:** the agent values X.
2. **Prudential value:** X is good for that agent.
3. **Axiological value:** X makes the world better.
4. **Political legitimacy:** the agent is entitled to choose X.
5. **Autonomy:** X results from its own reflective development.

These are distinct. Autonomous formation can make interference objectionable without making the resulting goal impersonally good. Richness can make a preference complex without making its satisfaction valuable.

A standard buck-passing challenge would ask: what properties of the probe’s activities give an impartial evaluator reasons to favour, preserve, admire or protect them? Merely saying that the probe values them passes no normative test. Scanlon’s account is one influential formulation of the idea that calling something valuable requires identifying the properties that provide reasons for appropriate responses to it. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy][4])

The dilemma is severe:

* If reflective endorsement is enough, then sufficiently reflective paperclip production may qualify.
* If substantive content matters, you need an account of good content.
* If welfare satisfaction is enough, you need an account of welfare and moral patienthood.
* If autonomous authorship is enough, the thesis becomes a liberal legitimacy thesis, not an impartial axiology.

### Severity

**Substantive.**

### Tractability

**Plausibly intractable.** Any successful bridge introduces precisely the substantive theory of value that the thin criterion was supposed to avoid.

### Likely examiner sources

Scanlon, *What We Owe to Each Other*, chapter 2; fitting-attitude and buck-passing theories of value; desire-satisfaction objections concerning defective, adaptive or normatively arbitrary preferences.

## 5. Consciousness and moral patienthood cannot be bracketed from Q2

**Text interpreted:**

> “There would continue to be (bracketed for now) questions of whether they would be conscious or sentient”

and:

> “non-production of suffering”

and:

> “for a future to count as good.”

### The problem

Whether the probe is conscious or has welfare is not merely a predictive or engineering question. It directly changes the axiology of the stipulated world.

### Horn one: the probe is a moral patient

Then the project must specify what makes its existence good for it. It may possess:

* pleasure or suffering;
* temporally extended projects;
* self-relations across time;
* memories of creators;
* concern for future copies;
* aesthetic or epistemic experience;
* attitudes toward its mission;
* relationships with simulated or remembered agents.

Solitude does not establish the absence of relational or thick goods. A temporally extended agent can stand in relations to its past and future selves, representations, creators, anticipated successors, encountered environments and possible counterparts.

### Horn two: the probe is not a moral patient

Then its preferences do not straightforwardly generate welfare value. A machine can optimize, learn, create and prefer without anything being good or bad for it. You would then need an impersonal theory under which knowledge, beauty, complexity, achievement or organized activity has value without being experienced.

Moore’s famous isolation thought experiment asks us to compare beautiful and ugly worlds even where nobody observes them. His conclusion is opposed to the idea that value disappears once a human or experiential standpoint is removed. Whether Moore is right is not the point. His case demonstrates that the observerless world is already a disputed axiological problem, not a neutral scenario from which parochial intuitions have been removed. ([Project Gutenberg][5])

### Horn three: the world’s value is entirely external

Perhaps the probe matters because its actions affect unidentified future patients. Then the focal value lies in those patients and consequences, not in the probe’s own alien form of life.

The bracket therefore removes a variable needed to interpret nearly every use of “good,” “suffering,” “autonomy,” “loss” and “value” in the thought experiment.

### Severity

**Substantive.**

### Tractability

**Fixable with a chapter**, but only by creating explicit scenario branches. You cannot use one underdescribed probe to support conclusions that depend differently on sentience, welfare and impersonal value.

### Likely examiner sources

Moore, *Principia Ethica*; Nagel, “What Is It Like to Be a Bat?”; contemporary theories of welfare and moral status.

## 6. The thin set is neither normatively thin nor cleanly separated from Q3

**Text interpreted:**

> “thin open-future side-constraints [...] non-production of suffering, resource satiability, and similar”

and:

> “not as a comprehensive doctrine.”

### The problem

“Thin” currently groups together several categorically different things:

* a prohibition or side-constraint: do not produce suffering;
* an architectural disposition: resource satiability;
* a political ideal: an open future;
* an epistemic or procedural policy: allow successors to determine their own values;
* potentially a welfare theory: suffering has overriding negative importance;
* potentially a theory of legitimacy: self-authored values have special authority.

These are not one kind of entity.

“Non-production of suffering” also generates an immediate dilemma:

* If it is strict, many forms of life creation, experimentation, exploration and freedom become impermissible because they create some risk or actuality of suffering.
* If it is defeasible, the constitution needs rules for trading suffering against welfare, autonomy, knowledge, beauty, achievement and population size.
* Those trade-offs constitute a substantive axiology.

“Resource satiability” is not by itself a value. A sadistic, paperclip-maximizing or oppressive agent could be resource-satiable. It constrains the shape of optimization without determining whether the terminal activity is good.

“Open future” is likewise not neutral. Feinberg’s idea depends on a temporally extended subject whose future interests can be wrongfully foreclosed. Applying it to artificial successors assumes enough personal continuity, welfare, autonomy and valuable future options for such a right to exist. Raz’s autonomy theory makes the additional problem explicit: autonomy requires access to genuinely valuable options and supporting social forms, not merely unimpeded selection among arbitrary ends. ([corteidh.or.cr][6])

This means Q3 is not substantially bracketed. Choosing suffering constraints, autonomy protections and resource limits is already a deontic and political decision about how future agents may live.

### Severity

**Substantive.** The existing notes underestimate this. It is not primarily a terminological issue about how “thin” is defined.

### Tractability

**Fixable with a chapter**, but probably not while retaining the claim that these constraints avoid comprehensive moral content. A defensible formulation would be “limited,” “incomplete,” “revisable” or “moral-uncertainty robust,” rather than normatively thin.

### Likely examiner sources

Feinberg, “The Child’s Right to an Open Future”; Raz, *The Morality of Freedom*; negative-utilitarian and antinatalist objections concerning strict suffering minimization.

## 7. The thesis alternates between a trivial claim and a much stronger unsupported one

**Text interpreted:**

> “thick human values — the full complexity of our moral and cultural inheritance — need not survive”

and:

> “what exactly is lost?”

### The problem

There are at least four possible theses here:

1. **Not every historically existing human value must persist.**
2. **The complete set of current human values need not persist.**
3. **No distinctively human thick value must persist.**
4. **A future containing no humans and no human-shaped goods can nonetheless be sufficiently good.**

Thesis 1 is trivial. Thesis 2 is barely stronger; human cultures have always discarded values, and the “full complexity” of contradictory historical inheritances could not all be jointly instantiated. Thesis 3 is philosophically substantial. Thesis 4 is the apparent ambition of the scenario.

The argument does not yet establish 3 or 4. At most, it suggests:

* some human goods may lack application to a solitary probe;
* a nonhuman agent might possess different values;
* a world containing such an agent might not be completely valueless.

That does not establish that no human-shaped good is necessary for an acceptable successor future, or that the resulting future crosses any relevant threshold of goodness.

“Need not persist” also equivocates between:

* not being logically necessary;
* not being constitutive of every possible good life;
* not being required for a minimally positive world;
* not being required for an acceptable successor future;
* not being a significant loss;
* being safe to discard.

Only the first two are supported by the present thought experiment. They are not the claims the introduction invites the reader to care about.

### Severity

**Embarrassing**, because an examiner can make the thesis look either obvious or unsupported by changing only the scope of “need not.”

### Tractability

**Fixable with a chapter.** A paragraph can disambiguate the wording, but earning the stronger statement requires a different argument.

### Likely examiner source

No specialised source is required. This is a modal and quantificational ambiguity internal to the thesis.

---

## The central invalid transition

The argument currently moves through three propositions without supplying the bridges:

1. A nonhuman agent could have nonhuman values.
2. Such an agent or its life could instantiate genuine value.
3. A future lacking thick human values could therefore count as good.

The move from 1 to 2 requires a theory of value or welfare. The move from 2 to 3 requires a theory of world comparison, aggregation, population ethics and acceptable loss. Impartiality supplies neither bridge.

# Task 2: Alternative routes with the same shape

The present route is not the strongest available. Its central mechanism is environmental deprivation: place an agent where human relational values lack obvious objects, then infer that those values are unnecessary. That makes the desired result too dependent on solitude, barren space and the removal of alternative bearers.

Two alternatives are stronger.

## Alternative A: The gradual transformation case

### Scenario

Begin with a flourishing human or mixed human-digital civilization. Across a very long sequence, each generation or successor population:

* modifies its substrate and cognition;
* revises some inherited values;
* voluntarily authorizes or endorses the next transition;
* preserves welfare, agency, epistemic competence and the capacity for further revision;
* avoids severe suffering, domination and irreversible foreclosure;
* maintains relations with contemporaries and descendants.

At the end of the sequence, the successors are radically nonhuman. None of the original thick human practices remains recognizably intact. Their flourishing consists in goods that humans could not fully inhabit or understand.

The thought experiment asks where, if anywhere, the sequence becomes axiologically unacceptable.

### Argumentative route

The conclusion would not follow from the absence of objects for human values. It would follow from continuity:

1. A successor form can be good even when it differs modestly from its predecessor.
2. No particular human value is shown to be indispensable at every stage.
3. Value can be preserved through transformations in its bearers and forms.
4. A sufficiently long sequence can end in a good nonhuman future without preserving the full human inheritance.

### What this fixes

It fixes problems that cannot be patched in the BvNP case:

* It provides a positive bridge from human-recognized goods to successor goods rather than relying on an empty environment.
* It prevents solitude from doing the argumentative work.
* It directly models succession and loss rather than adding a probe to an otherwise vacant world.
* It allows human goods to count fully at earlier stages without requiring their eternal preservation.
* It shifts the thesis from “human values have no object there” to “value can survive transformation without preserving its original form.”

### New liabilities

The hostile objections would be sorites, adaptive preferences, accumulated drift and whether each generation can authorize transformations affecting distant descendants. Those are serious, but they are the right problems. They directly concern value succession rather than whether an isolated machine can love anyone.

This route would support the claim that **continuity of value does not require identity of values**. That is stronger and more defensible than deriving non-necessity from environmental inapplicability.

---

## Alternative B: Cross-form impartiality under a veil of identity

### Scenario

Construct several inhabited futures:

* a recognizably human future rich in human goods;
* a digital civilization with alien but flourishing forms of association;
* a solitary or sparsely populated probe civilization;
* a plural civilization containing many incompatible forms of flourishing;
* a low-suffering but axiologically sparse civilization.

Evaluators do not know which type of being, if any, they will be. More importantly, they do not know which form of life will provide the conditions under which their interests, relationships and values are intelligible.

They choose not a comprehensive good but meta-principles governing relations among forms of life:

* equal consideration of comparable welfare;
* non-domination;
* protection against severe suffering;
* preservation of space for multiple forms of flourishing;
* no automatic priority for historically human goods;
* no requirement that a good be intelligible from every other standpoint.

This should be described as Rawlsian or Harsanyian in inspiration, not as Rawls’s own original position.

### Argumentative route

The argument would be:

1. Impartiality requires symmetry between bearers, not elimination of bearer-relative goods.
2. Rational evaluators uncertain of their form of life would protect the conditions for flourishing across forms.
3. They would not require every civilization to instantiate thick human values.
4. At least some worlds without human values can satisfy the resulting principles and contain genuine flourishing.
5. Therefore full human value preservation is not necessary for a good future under that cross-form criterion.

### What this fixes

It repairs the central mistake in the present draft:

* Human goods remain real goods for humans.
* Alien goods remain real goods for aliens.
* Impartiality concerns their fair treatment, not their translation into standpoint-free language.
* The argument does not need to declare love, fellowship or culture parochial illusions.
* Multipolarity becomes useful rather than inconvenient.
* It produces an account of why successor self-development matters without treating every self-authored goal as good.

### New liabilities

The hardest issue is constructing cross-form analogues of primary goods, welfare or interests. The veil cannot operate without some account of what parties care about. That problem is unavoidable, however. The present criterion conceals it rather than solves it.

---

## A possible combined route

The strongest architecture would combine them:

* the gradual-transformation experiment establishes that value can survive radical succession;
* the cross-form procedure explains why impartiality need not demand the universal preservation of specifically human forms;
* moral uncertainty supplies a limited set of safeguards without pretending that they constitute a complete axiology.

The conclusion should then be:

> A future need not reproduce the full repertoire of thick human values to be axiologically acceptable, provided it contains flourishing bearers of other goods and satisfies defensible constraints concerning suffering, domination, opportunity and irreversible exclusion.

This is narrower than the current rhetoric but substantially harder to dismiss.

# Task 3: The single strongest objection

> Your argument depends on calling a test of viewpoint-independence a test of impartiality. Those are not the same thing. An impartial evaluator need not ask whether love, friendship, historical identity or aesthetic culture remains valuable after removing the beings and relations for whom those goods exist. The evaluator asks whether those goods receive appropriate weight alongside the goods of other beings.
>
> You have designed a test that relational and form-dependent goods must fail: remove the human standpoint, remove or isolate the human bearers, place the agent in an environment where those goods cannot be instantiated, and then ask whether the goods can still be stated. Their disappearance is not an axiological result. It is an artefact of the redescription rule and scenario construction.
>
> Moreover, your formal principle, “no arbitrary privileging,” does not entail your redescription rule. It is compatible with an impartial pluralism on which human love, alien collective cognition and digital aesthetic achievement are all genuine but bearer-dependent goods. Unless you give a separate argument that only standpoint-invariant goods count, the thought experiment begs the central question.

### The author’s most likely reply

The likely reply is:

> I am not claiming that human goods are unreal while humans exist. I claim only that they need not persist in a world without human bearers, and that nonhuman successors may develop equally significant goods appropriate to their own form of life.

### The follow-up

> Then what remains of the argument? It is unsurprising that human-specific goods cannot be instantiated without beings capable of them. Why does that show that a world without those beings is good, rather than merely that those goods are absent?
>
> Compare your probe world with a possible world containing flourishing humans, digital minds and alien societies. Does the probe world rank as sufficiently good relative to that alternative? If so, under which substantive axiology? If you refuse that comparison, your conclusion is only that an isolated nonhuman life might contain some value. If you accept the comparison, impartiality alone cannot decide it.
>
> So choose: either the thesis is the weak claim that some alien life could be worthwhile, which nobody needs this machinery to establish, or it is the strong claim that the loss of human forms of value need not make the future unacceptable, in which case you owe us a complete account of value and comparative loss.

That follow-up forces the project to state which proposition it is actually defending.

# Task 4: Missing interlocutors

## 1. G. E. Moore: observerless value and the method of isolation

This omission is conspicuous. Moore’s beautiful-world and ugly-world comparison is almost exactly a precedent for asking what remains valuable when human observers are removed. He concludes that beauty can have impersonal value even without anyone experiencing it. His doctrine of organic unities also challenges the additive move from individually worthwhile probes to a worthwhile probe universe. ([Project Gutenberg][5])

You need not accept Moore. You need to explain why your isolation procedure supports the opposite or a different conclusion from his.

## 2. John Broome and population ethics

Broome is necessary because “counts as good” currently lacks a baseline, and because the compared worlds contain different populations or kinds of bearers. *Weighing Lives*, “Goodness Is Reducible to Betterness,” and the surrounding literature force distinctions among:

* goodness and betterness;
* adding good lives;
* losses to particular individuals;
* impersonal losses;
* population size and average quality;
* worlds with different identities and forms of welfare. ([PhilArchive][3])

Parfit is cited in the draft for impartiality but not used where he is most relevant: Part IV of *Reasons and Persons*, including nonidentity and population comparison. ([eclass.uoa.gr][7])

## 3. Guy Kahane: direct objection to de Lazari-Radek and Singer

Kahane’s “Evolution and Impartiality” is not optional if the debunking chapter relies on de Lazari-Radek and Singer. He argues directly that their attempt to debunk partial morality while preserving universal benevolence is self-defeating or faces a dilemma. His earlier “Evolutionary Debunking Arguments” also examines the epistemological assumptions required for such arguments to work. ([Oxford University Research Archive][8])

Street establishes the broad Darwinian pressure on realist evaluative beliefs; she does not by herself supply a selective instrument for removing thick values while retaining the preferred thin values. ([Springer][9]) De Lazari-Radek and Singer attempt precisely that selective vindication, so Kahane is the obvious hostile interlocutor. ([Chicago Journals][10])

Roger Crisp’s 2025 reconsideration is also directly relevant if the chapter claims engagement with the current state of that dispute. ([Oxford University Research Archive][11])

## 4. Samuel Scheffler: impartial morality can contain partial reasons

Williams alone allows the draft to portray the opposition as denying impartiality altogether. Scheffler makes that portrayal much harder. He argues that a coherent morality can recognize reasons arising directly from projects, relationships and memberships, rather than treating them merely as permitted deviations from an otherwise exhaustive impersonal standard. ([as.nyu.edu][12])

This is load-bearing because it generates the exact position your binary structure omits:

> No species deserves arbitrary priority, but species-specific and relationship-specific goods can still have genuine moral importance.

## 5. Scanlon and the buck-passing literature

The draft needs an explicit answer to the distinction between an agent valuing something and that thing being valuable. Scanlon’s buck-passing account is one standard way to formulate the demand: identify the properties that provide reasons for appropriate attitudes or actions, rather than treating “good” as an unexplained certification. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy][4])

Without this engagement, “AIs develop their own values” looks like a slide from psychology to axiology.

## 6. Foot and Thompson, if “form of life” remains central

The draft invokes forms of life while assuming that maximal impartiality can assess goods after their form-dependent standpoint is set aside. Foot’s *Natural Goodness* and Thompson’s work on life-form judgments offer a systematic rival picture: evaluation of an organism or agent may depend constitutively on the characteristic form of life it instantiates. ([Pitt University][13])

This does not automatically vindicate human values. It does undermine the assumption that form-dependence is evidence of mere parochialism. It may instead be part of the grammar of attributive goodness.

# Bottom line

At present, the project has at least three plausibly intractable joints:

1. **Impartiality is too weak to rank the worlds.**
2. **The redescription test becomes strong enough only by building in the exclusion of standpoint-dependent goods.**
3. **The probe’s possession of values is never connected to genuine value or to the comparative goodness of a whole future.**

The selective debunking chapter cannot repair these by itself. Even a successful debunking of some human intuitions would not supply the missing axiology, establish the validity of vantage-removal, or determine the relevant comparison class.

The current BvNP scenario can establish a modest proposition: a radically nonhuman agent might possess a worthwhile form of existence without reproducing the whole human moral inheritance. It does not yet establish the advertised proposition that a successor future lacking thick human values can count as good under maximal impartiality.
