# Task 1 — Independent demolition attempt

## P1. The criterion is either toothless or omnivorous, and "arbitrary" decides which

(i) The criterion is "no arbitrary privileging of person, place, time, or species," and the test is to "test which candidate losses can still be stated once the human vantage point is set aside." Here is a candidate loss, stated with no indexicals and no vantage: *in this world there exist, anywhere, no beings who love, no friendship, no aesthetic experience.* That proposition quantifies impersonally over the universe. It privileges no person, place, time, or species — love is multiply realizable in principle; the claim is not "no *humans* love" but "no *lovers* exist." On the narrow reading of the criterion (no indexical/de se privileging), this loss passes the test, and so do most thick losses, because agent-neutral value claims about contingently human-instantiated goods are trivially restatable without the vantage. Your thesis then fails. To block this, you must read "arbitrary privileging of species" broadly: any evaluative conviction whose *pedigree* is a species-typical evolved sensibility counts as privileging. But on that reading, suffering-aversion has exactly the same pedigree, and the thin set falls with the thick. The criterion has no stable middle setting, and the word "arbitrary" is where the entire substantive axiology is hiding, undefined.

(ii) Embarrassing. This is not a deferred problem for Chapter 5; it is active in the introduction's own "Question to the reader," which cannot currently be run without silently choosing a reading.

(iii) Plausibly intractable *as formulated*. Any principled middle setting for "arbitrary" is a substantive theory of value, and defending one reopens everything the criterion was supposed to bypass. Note this is distinct from the Part B selectivity item: that concerns whether *debunking* can be selective; this shows the *criterion itself* faces the same dilemma before debunking is even invoked.

(iv) The agent-relative/agent-neutral literature (Parfit, *Reasons and Persons* §§ on agent-relativity; Nagel, *The View from Nowhere* ch. 8–9; Korsgaard, "The Reasons We Can Share"). Impartiality constrains *whose* good counts equally; it is silent on *what* counts as good.

## P2. Screening losses cannot deliver "good": the good/not-faulted gap

(i) Q2 says thick values "need not survive for a future to count as good." The method only ever *eliminates candidate losses*. Even a clean sweep yields "no impartially statable loss identified," which is compatible with the probe-world being *valueless* — neither good nor bad. Run your own test on a universe of sterile rock: what loss survives the removal of the human vantage there? If your criterion cannot distinguish the probe-universe from the rock-universe, it cannot certify the former as good; if it can, name the positive value theory doing the work — and check whether it is thick. This is Moore's isolation method (the beautiful world vs. the heap of filth) run in reverse, and the draft's TE is a direct structural descendant of Moore's two-worlds without acknowledging it. Compounding this: "loss" is comparative and the draft never fixes the comparandum. Lost relative to *our* world's continuation? To an empty world? To a thick-value cosmic future? The verdict changes with the baseline.

(ii) Embarrassing if unaddressed, because it means the thesis statement overclaims relative to anything the method can produce.

(iii) Fixable, but only by one of two chapter-scale moves: adopt a positive axiology (which must then survive P1's dilemma), or weaken the thesis to "such a future cannot be impartially *faulted*" — a real and defensible claim, but not the one Q2 currently states. A paragraph cannot patch this; it is a choice between two different chapters.

(iv) Moore, *Principia Ethica* §§50, 112–113; the value-without-valuers debate in environmental ethics (Rolston vs. Callicott) is the standard proving ground for whether "good" applies to observerless worlds.

## P3. The semantics of thickness rigs the test; Williams is relocated, not escaped

(i) "Thick" is Williams's term, and Williams's analysis of thick concepts is that they are *world-guided and action-guiding at once* and cannot be disentangled into neutral description plus attitude. If that disentangling thesis is right, then thick losses are *analytically guaranteed* to fail a test that demands they "be stated once the human vantage point is set aside" — not because they are parochial, but because that is what thickness *means*. The test then presupposes the parochialism it purports to discover. And this shows the "criterion, not posture" move does not escape Williams: his objection to the absolute conception was never merely that the posture is unoccupiable (which you concede) but that the impartial conception *lacks the conceptual resources to register what thick concepts register*. A criterion applied in a vocabulary that cannot express the candidate losses is an instrument calibrated to find nothing.

(ii) Substantive shading into embarrassing, because the draft cites Williams only on corrosiveness, i.e. on the part of Williams you have an answer to, and not on the part that targets your test's semantics.

(iii) Chapter-scale, and genuinely hard: you would need to either refute the disentangling thesis (against McDowell and Putnam) or reformulate the test so that expressive failure is *defeasible evidence* of parochialism rather than criterial of it — which weakens every downstream conclusion.

(iv) Williams, *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy* ch. 8; McDowell, "Non-Cognitivism and Rule-Following"; Putnam on fact/value entanglement.

## P4. The thin set survives only under an undeclared population-ethical asymmetry

(i) The inference pattern in the "Question to the reader" is: relational values lack objects in this environment, therefore their absence is not a loss. But non-instantiability of a *positive* value is precisely what a loss *is* under any totalist axiology; only for *negative* values (suffering) does absence of instantiation-conditions count as vacuous satisfaction rather than deprivation. Notice your thin set is exclusively negative/side-constraint shaped. The architecture therefore silently assumes the procreation-asymmetry family of views (absent goods aren't bad; absent bads are fine) — a heavily contested position in population ethics, nowhere declared or defended. This is distinct from the Part B relationality-scoping item: scoping to the solitary case does not touch the inference from non-instantiation to no-loss.

(ii) Substantive. (iii) Fixable with a chapter section that *owns* the asymmetry — but owning it is itself a thick-looking axiological commitment, which strains the architecture. (iv) Parfit, *Reasons and Persons* Part IV; Benatar; Fehige's antifrustrationism; Frick on the asymmetry.

## P5. Feinberg's open future is content-parasitic on human forms of life

(i) The open-future right gets its content from the range of life-plans available within a human society; "open to what?" is answered by the human option-set. Export it to entities whose form of life is stipulated to be at maximal inferential distance and the constraint is either empty (no determinate option-set to keep open) or covertly thick (openness relative to *our* conception of a flourishing trajectory). Additionally, "non-production of suffering" presupposes suffering-attribution across alien substrates — exactly the consciousness question you bracket. The thin set's *applicability* depends on bracketed questions.

(ii) Substantive. (iii) Chapter-scale; the Feinberg move probably needs replacement by a purpose-built constraint rather than an inherited one. (iv) Feinberg 1980 read against its own scope conditions; Rini 2017 doesn't repair this.

## Severity flag on a Part B item

The two-framings device is underestimated even in your editorial note 3. Stipulative parity and knob-control are necessary but not sufficient: if intuitions diverge across registers *at full parity*, the divergence shows framing-sensitivity but yields no verdict on *which* register is the artifact. The warm framing could be the sentimental distortion or the vividness-corrective (Dennett allows both). Without an independent theory of which register tracks value — the very question at issue — the device is diagnostic only and argumentatively inert for Q2. State that limited role explicitly or an examiner will state it for you.

# Task 2 — Alternative routes

**Route A — positive axiology via determinable/determinate structure.** Keep the probe TE, replace the loss-screening test with a preservation argument: ask the reader to identify the *good-making features* of human goods (say: richly structured experience, achievement, understanding — whatever survives scrutiny), then stipulate the probe-world instantiates those determinables under alien determinates. Parochialism is redefined as mistaking the local *realizer* for the value. This fixes P1 (no reliance on "arbitrary"), P2 (delivers "good" positively), and P3 (no expressibility test to rig). Cost: you must commit to a theory of good-making features, and the probe must actually be stipulated to have them — meaning the fully austere, possibly experienceless probe cannot be your focal case. It also fits your existing Dennett apparatus better than the current route: it is essentially "real patterns" applied to axiology.

**Route B — symmetry/deflation.** Keep the loss-vocabulary but build a mirror TE: an alien civilization with alien thick values contemplating a human-dominated future and finding *it* desolate. Show the two complaints are structurally isomorphic; conclude that thick-persistence complaints are indexical in disguise, like temporal bias. This delivers the conditional directly and never requires losses to be "stated without the vantage," avoiding P3 entirely. Cost: it delivers "cannot be impartially faulted," not "good" — but that is all the current route delivers anyway (P2), so Route B is the honest version of the existing argument, with Q2 restated accordingly.

I do not think the current route is patchable into delivering "good." Choose: Route A if Q2's "good" must mean good; Route B plus a weakened Q2 if you can live with deflation. Running the current route unmodified means cashing P1–P3 simultaneously in Chapter 5, which is where I would predict collapse.

# Task 3 — The single strongest objection, examiner's voice

"Here is a loss with no vantage in it: *nowhere in your world does anything love anything.* No indexicals, no proper names, no species term used evaluatively — a universal generalization about what kinds of goods exist. It passes your stated test. So either your test lets the thick losses through, and your thesis fails, or you now tell me the *conviction* that love's absence matters is itself species-privileging because it issues from an evolved human sensibility. Take the second horn. Your aversion to suffering issues from the identical sensibility — same pedigree, same evolutionary story, same parochial origin. So state the principle that debunks the one conviction and spares the other. And before you appeal to Chapter 5: I am not asking about the debunking argument's *selectivity in general*; I am asking what, *in the criterion as stated in this chapter*, distinguishes non-arbitrary from arbitrary privileging — because your reader must apply that distinction on page one, four chapters before your debunking argument exists."

Your most likely reply is de Lazari-Radek & Singer's move: the impartiality axiom and suffering-aversion survive debunking because they are reachable by reason alone, not explicable solely by fitness. My follow-up: "Sidgwick's rationally-reachable residue was *hedonism* — the view that what matters is valenced experience. Your focal probe may have none; you have bracketed whether it can. So the one axiology your debunking strategy spares appears to evaluate your probe-world at zero. Either the probe has valenced experience, in which case say so and accept everything that stipulation drags in, or the surviving axiology condemns your scenario and your own Chapter 5 is the refutation of your Chapter 1."

# Task 4 — Missing interlocutors

**Scheffler, *Death and the Afterlife*** — the most conspicuous absence. Scheffler argues that the value of much human activity depends on the survival of a collective human future; your thesis is close to its direct denial. An examiner in this territory will raise it in the first ten minutes. Cohen's "Rescuing Conservatism" (conservatism about existing value) belongs in the same slot.

**Moore's method of isolation** — your TE's structural ancestor (see P2); not citing it looks like not knowing the genre's founding instance.

**Williams on thick concepts specifically** (not just impartiality-corrosiveness) — see P3; using his term while ignoring his semantics for it is the kind of gap examiners relish.

**Population ethics / the Asymmetry** (Parfit Part IV, Benatar, Fehige, Frick) — load-bearing given P4; the draft currently takes a contested position in this literature without naming the literature.

**Bales 1971 / Railton's "Alienation, Consequentialism"** — your criterion/posture distinction is the criterion-of-rightness/decision-procedure distinction under another name. Citing it would strengthen the move *and* import its known limitations honestly, which is better than an examiner importing them for you.

One clean bill: I found nothing intractable in the Q1–Q4 bracketing architecture itself. The bracketing is done carefully. The failures above are all inside Q2's own machinery.
