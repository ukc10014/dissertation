## The most convergent findings

### 1. Impartiality is not yet an axiology

This is the strongest convergence by some distance. Nearly every critique argues that:

> “No arbitrary privileging of person, place, time, or species”

is a **symmetry or anti-discrimination constraint**. It can tell you not to discount something merely because it is nonhuman, distant or future. It cannot by itself tell you:

* what things are valuable;
* whether the probe’s activities instantiate value;
* how much value a world contains;
* whether that world is positively good.

The word “arbitrary” also threatens to conceal the missing substantive standard. A distinction is arbitrary only relative to some prior account of which properties are morally relevant.

This bears directly on the movement in the chapter from the impartiality criterion to the question “what exactly is lost?” and then to the claim that the future might count as good. 

### 2. The argument currently moves from “no decisive loss” to “good”

The redescription or statability test is subtractive. It attempts to eliminate objections based on human parochialism. Even if completely successful, that would establish something like:

* no specifically human loss has been shown to make the future bad; or
* the future is not objectionable merely because it lacks human continuity.

It would not yet establish that the future is positively valuable.

Fable-v4’s particularly effective version is the **sterile-rock control**: if the criterion cannot distinguish the probe world from a universe containing only sterile rocks, then it is not yet supplying the positive value-maker required by “good.”

This is the central semantic fork:

1. weaken the consequent to “not impartially faultable,” “not catastrophic,” or “not bad merely because nonhuman”; or
2. retain “good,” but identify positive nonhuman goods that the successor world instantiates.

I think the second route is better for your project.

### 3. Non-instantiability does not entail non-loss

The solitary environment may make human love, fellowship, artistic community and some relational goods impossible to instantiate. But:

> There is nobody there who can love, therefore the absence of love is not a loss

requires a substantive bridge premise. It resembles a person-affecting or procreation-asymmetry position: absent possible goods do not count negatively unless there is an existing bearer who is deprived of them.

That is not supplied by impartiality. It is a controversial population-ethical commitment.

This is why so many responses recommend a **matched-world or permutation argument**. Rather than deleting the conditions under which rival goods could exist, compare:

* a flourishing human-continuity future; and
* a flourishing nonhuman successor future;

while holding the independently relevant positive dimensions fixed and varying human genealogy and cultural realization.

The resulting argument would be: if the second world is downgraded solely because its goods are nonhuman in origin or form, that looks like precisely the arbitrary species or genealogy privilege your criterion excludes.

### 4. “Thin values” currently perform too many different jobs

The evaluations repeatedly observe that suffering prevention, resource satiability, an open future, constitutional limitations and autonomy are not one homogeneous category. They include:

* axiological commitments;
* side-constraints;
* procedural principles;
* rights or protections;
* properties of agent architecture.

More importantly, thin values cannot simultaneously be described as neutral constitutional safeguards and treated as what makes the world positively good.

The cleanest separation is:

* **Q2:** historically or culturally human thick values are not necessary for goodness, because nonhuman successors could instantiate other genuine goods.
* **Q3:** humans should perhaps impose only limited constitutional constraints, leaving successors room to develop their own richer values.

Your draft already contains the resources for this distinction when it says future AIs may develop “their own set of rich relational values or non-relational preferences.” The problem is that other passages describe the target as a “world of thin values,” which makes the more radical and less defensible claim appear to be doing the work. 

### 5. Consciousness cannot be completely bracketed for Q2

It can remain bracketed for prediction and perhaps for some questions about agency. But whether the probe is conscious affects:

* whether it has welfare;
* whether suffering prevention is applicable;
* whether its experiences contribute positive value;
* whether the world contains a subject for whom anything is good.

The minimal repair is not to solve consciousness. It is to distinguish three versions:

1. conscious probe;
2. nonconscious probe;
3. uncertain patienthood.

Then state which version carries the primary axiological argument.

### 6. The warm/cold pair is diagnostic, not verdict-producing

Different responses to two literary framings show that reactions are framing-sensitive. They do not tell you which framing is epistemically superior. The warm version might distort through sentimentality; equally, the cold version might distort through imaginative under-description.

Call it a **framing-robustness diagnostic**, not a control that identifies the unbiased judgment.

## My object-level recommendation

Do **not** immediately weaken Q2 to “not impartially faultable.” Your draft already gestures toward the stronger and more defensible route: successors may develop their own rich, nonhuman values.

The upgrade-safe thesis would be approximately:

> There are possible successor futures that lack historically and culturally human thick values but instantiate genuine nonhuman goods sufficient for positive value; impartiality forbids discounting those goods merely because of their species, genealogy or unfamiliar realization.

Then:

* use matched worlds or permutation symmetry as the central philosophical argument;
* retain the BvNP as the severe limiting case and artistic vehicle;
* treat thin values as initial constitutional constraints under Q3, not as a complete account of what makes the future good;
* state provisionally what positive goods the nonhuman world contains;
* acknowledge that cross-form value individuation is a task for the full thesis.

This retains the core thesis while removing the inference that the judges consistently found least defensible. It also satisfies the rubric’s emphasis on changes that remain tractable and survive development over the next one to two years.
