## Task 1: Independent demolition attempt

The first three problems can sink the central chapter. They are not drafting defects. They concern whether the conclusion follows from the proposed criterion at all.

### 1. Impartiality does not supply an axiology

> “I am asking the reader to apply a criterion: that no one individual’s good matters more, from the point of view of the universe, than any other’s; generalised, no arbitrary privileging of person, place, time, or species.”

This constrains **whose good receives consideration**. It does not tell us:

* what constitutes anyone’s good;
* whether impersonal goods exist;
* whether relationships, knowledge, beauty, achievement or cultural complexity have final value;
* how different goods should be aggregated;
* whether value is person-affecting;
* whether creating valuable lives or valuable practices improves a world.

An impartial theory can be hedonistic, perfectionist, pluralist, contractualist, prioritarian or rights-based. Several such theories would treat art, knowledge, relationships and achievements as impartially valuable. The fact that humans happen to instantiate them does not make their recognition species-partial.

The word **“arbitrary”** conceals the missing argument. Privileging human culture merely because it is human would be arbitrary. Valuing it because it instantiates knowledge, beauty, agency, achievement or unique forms of relationship would not obviously be arbitrary. The criterion therefore cannot tell you whether protecting human culture is arbitrary until you already know whether that culture has value. That is the question the criterion is supposed to answer.

The Rawls-Sidgwick-Parfit genealogy cannot fill the gap. Rawls’s original position represents a particular contractual construction of justice; it does not merely formalise generic impartiality. Harsanyi derives utilitarian conclusions from a superficially similar veil by giving the parties different rational-choice assumptions. The dispute shows that ignorance of identity does not determine a substantive moral theory. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy][1])

**Severity:** Substantive and potentially fatal.

**Tractability:** Plausibly intractable in the current form. It becomes fixable with a chapter only if you select and defend a substantive axiology in addition to impartiality. Merely elaborating “no arbitrary privileging” will not do it.

**Likely examiner references:** Rawls, *A Theory of Justice*; Harsanyi, “Can the Maximin Principle Serve as a Basis for Morality?”; Nagel, *The View from Nowhere*; Scheffler, *The Rejection of Consequentialism*; Scanlon on value and reasons.

### 2. The “criterion, not posture” move relocates the Williams problem

> “I am not asking the reader to occupy a view from nowhere. I am asking the reader to apply a criterion.”

And:

> “I am not asking the reader to abandon that vantage — only to notice, item by item, which candidate losses can still be stated once it is set aside.”

The distinction removes an impossible psychological instruction but not the philosophical problem. Someone still has to determine:

* which descriptions count as setting aside the human vantage;
* which reasons are genuinely impartial;
* which apparently particular goods are merely parochial;
* what “arbitrary” means;
* how the criterion has authority over reasons generated by a form of life.

There are two interpretations of the proposed test, and both fail.

**Epistemic interpretation:** A loss counts only if humans can formulate it after attempting to abstract from their standpoint. This makes our inability to describe a value evidence that no such value exists. That is an argument from epistemic limitation to axiological absence. Inferential distance makes this inference less reliable, not more reliable.

**Metaphysical interpretation:** Only goods describable without reference to any particular form of life are genuine goods. This is no longer a modest impartiality constraint. It is a highly controversial anti-particularist theory of value. It may exclude love, friendship, aesthetic traditions and social achievement by definition rather than evaluation.

Williams’s objection was not simply that humans find the impartial posture psychologically difficult. It concerned the authority and alienating structure of a morality that requires agents to treat their projects and attachments as normatively incidental. Converting the standpoint into an algorithmic test leaves that authority question intact.

The draft is also entering ideal-observer territory without acknowledging it. Ideal-observer theories have to specify the observer’s information, affective capacities, interests and standards of relevance; these specifications determine the verdict. An “unoccupied criterion” faces the same problem in less explicit form. Firth is the obvious historical interlocutor. ([JSTOR][2])

**Severity:** Substantive and potentially fatal.

**Tractability:** Plausibly intractable if the redescription test remains the main route from scenario to verdict. It needs replacement by an explicit account of agent-neutral reasons or world-ranking value.

**Likely examiner references:** Williams, *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy*; Nagel, *The View from Nowhere*; Firth, “Ethical Absolutism and the Ideal Observer”; Rawlsian constructivism.

### 3. The argument moves illicitly from non-instantiability to non-loss

> “The relational values central to human life may simply lack objects there.”

Then:

> “In such a universe, what exactly is lost?”

The absence of objects for a value does not show that the value is dispensable. It may be precisely what makes the world deprived of that value.

A world has no friendship because it contains no plurality of agents. It does not follow that friendship has no value there. A world has no music because none of its inhabitants can hear or produce organised sound. It does not follow that the absence of music is axiologically neutral. The scenario removes the conditions under which goods can be instantiated, then risks treating their resulting inapplicability as evidence against their value.

To get the desired inference, you need something like:

> A possible good makes no contribution to the value of a world unless it benefits, fulfils the desires of, or figures in the projects of an entity who actually exists in that world.

That is a substantive person-affecting, desire-based or good-for theory. It is not supplied by impartiality. An impersonal or objective-list theory can say that worlds are better when they contain more friendship, knowledge, achievement or aesthetic value, even if no one in the poorer world is deprived in the person-affecting sense. Population axiology is largely structured around precisely this disagreement. ([PhilPapers][3])

The solitary probe is therefore not neutral scenery. It is a mechanism for deleting the possible bearers and objects of rival goods. That makes it a favourable construction for the conclusion.

**Severity:** Substantive and potentially fatal.

**Tractability:** Plausibly intractable for the current scenario-to-thesis inference. It can be repaired only by defending a person-affecting theory or by replacing the deprivation scenario with one containing substantial alien goods.

**Likely examiner references:** Parfit on impersonal and person-affecting principles; Broome on population value; Greaves, “Population Axiology”; contemporary person-affecting literature.

### 4. The official thesis is nearly trivial, while the chapter repeatedly argues stronger theses

The official formulation is:

> “Thick human values — the full complexity of our moral and cultural inheritance — need not survive for a future to count as good.”

“Full complexity” makes the claim easy to establish. No remotely plausible future will preserve every human value, cultural form, contradiction, religious practice, aesthetic tradition and historical conception of the good. Ordinary cultural change already shows that a future can remain good without preserving the full inheritance.

But later claims are substantially stronger:

> “Thick human values are unsuitable as a value endowment.”

> “A much smaller, what I call thin, set of values might be better as a starting point.”

> “What exactly is lost?”

These correspond to different propositions:

1. **Existential non-necessity:** At least one good future lacks the complete human inheritance.
2. **Axiological replaceability:** Alien goods can replace the value supplied by human goods.
3. **No-loss:** The disappearance of thick human goods is not itself a loss.
4. **Comparative superiority:** A thin-value future can be better than a thick-value future.
5. **Design recommendation:** We should load only thin constraints.
6. **Permissibility:** It would be permissible to bring about the thin future.

Only the first follows from the official thesis. It is also the least interesting. The thought experiment regularly invites conclusions 2 through 5.

“Counts as good” is additionally too weak to support the rhetoric of succession. A future could be good in an absolute sense while being catastrophically worse than an available human future. A village surviving a disaster may contain good lives while the disaster remains an immense loss. Q3 can bracket the permissibility of causing the transition, but it cannot bracket the comparative axiology if the argument asks “what is lost?”

**Severity:** Embarrassing if left unclarified; substantive once the nontrivial thesis is selected.

**Tractability:** The quantifiers can be corrected in a paragraph. Defending the corrected, stronger proposition requires a chapter.

**Likely examiner references:** Parfit and the population-axiology literature; Greaves’s taxonomy of theories of world-ranking. ([PhilPapers][4])

### 5. The argument has no criterion for individuating values

> “I’m not arguing that AIs would have no significant values, just that they don’t need to be human-shaped.”

This produces a dilemma.

Suppose alien AIs possess reciprocal attachment, shared projects, mutual recognition, aesthetic discrimination, collective memory and forms of creation. These may be radically unlike human friendship, love, art or culture phenomenologically. But they may instantiate the same abstract goods. In that case, the chapter has shown that **human implementations** of values are unnecessary, not that the values are unnecessary.

Suppose instead that the AIs possess nothing structurally analogous to relationship, creation, knowledge, play, appreciation or achievement. The world is then much more plausibly axiologically impoverished. Calling its remaining preferences “their own rich values” does not establish that they are goods rather than merely terminal aims.

You therefore need identity conditions for values. Are two values the same because they share:

* historical genealogy;
* phenomenology;
* functional role;
* objects;
* constitutive reasons;
* social practice;
* evaluative structure;
* behavioural consequences?

Without an answer, “human-shaped” expands or contracts opportunistically. Defined genealogically, the thesis is easy but primarily about cultural continuity. Defined functionally or structurally, many supposed alien values may turn out to preserve thick human goods at a higher level of abstraction.

Raz is particularly relevant because his pluralism connects many valuable activities to historically contingent social forms without concluding that their value is merely subjective or parochial. ([scholarship.law.columbia.edu][5])

**Severity:** Substantive.

**Tractability:** Fixable with a chapter, but only by making the thesis narrower and more exact.

**Likely examiner references:** Raz, *The Morality of Freedom*; Scanlon on reasons and value; Hurka on perfectionism; literature on relational goods.

### 6. Consciousness and welfare cannot remain bracketed

The chapter brackets whether the probe is conscious or sentient while retaining “non-production of suffering” as a thin value and asking whether its world is good.

Those branches are axiologically different worlds.

**If the probe is nonconscious:**

* it cannot suffer;
* the anti-suffering constraint is vacuously satisfied;
* its “values” may be no more welfare-generating than the dispositions of an automated factory;
* the chapter must defend the impersonal value of computation, achievement, complexity or goal fulfilment without experience.

**If the probe is conscious:**

* it may possess welfare, phenomenology and identity;
* isolation may be harmful;
* aesthetic, epistemic or relational goods may become relevant;
* its long-term self-relation, memory and internal multiplicity may instantiate forms of relationship.

**If its status is indeterminate:**

* the evaluation must incorporate moral uncertainty;
* the verdict cannot be derived from a single clean scenario.

This is broader than the known warm/cold parity defect. Even perfect stipulative parity would leave the focal world axiologically underspecified.

**Severity:** Substantive.

**Tractability:** Fixable with a chapter or explicit case split. It cannot be solved by continuing to bracket the issue.

### 7. The “thin set” is neither thin nor a set of one kind of thing

The proposed examples include:

* non-production of suffering;
* resource satiability;
* open-future constraints;
* autonomy or self-determination;
* perhaps reversibility and non-domination.

These belong to different normative and architectural categories.

“Non-production of suffering” is a substantive rule with potentially extreme consequences. Read literally, it may prohibit creating any sentient life that has a nonzero risk of suffering. It can conflict directly with autonomy, experimentation and the open future.

“Resource satiability” is not itself a moral value. It is a property of preferences or an agent architecture. A satiable agent could still pursue appalling terminal ends within its resource threshold.

“Open future” is not neutral. It embeds a liberal ideal of self-authorship, option preservation and opposition to comprehensive value control. That may be defensible, but it is a thick political commitment from one historical tradition. Rawlsian neutrality among comprehensive doctrines is itself supported by a substantive conception of free and equal persons; it is not normlessness. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy][6])

The problem is not merely terminological. Calling these commitments “thin” makes it look as though they survive the anti-parochial test without needing the defence required by love, art or fellowship. That asymmetry has not been earned.

**Severity:** Substantive.

**Tractability:** Fixable with a chapter if you separate moral side-constraints, architectural properties and procedural values. Potentially intractable if the argument requires them to be uniquely non-parochial.

**Likely examiner references:** Rawls on political liberalism; Raz on autonomy and perfectionism; Feinberg’s original open-future argument rather than its metaphorical extension.

### 8. The selective debunking problem is more severe than the draft admits

Part B identifies this as the most attackable joint. That understates the problem. It is not just a vulnerable supporting argument. It is currently being asked to manufacture the project’s positive normativity.

An evolutionary genealogy can potentially reduce confidence in an intuition. It does not by itself establish:

* which intuitions survive;
* why impartiality survives;
* why suffering aversion survives;
* why autonomy survives;
* why resource satiability has moral significance;
* why the surviving commitments constitute a sufficient axiology.

To debunk thick-value intuitions selectively, the chapter needs an independent epistemic criterion distinguishing tracking from distorting influences. But that criterion will already contain substantial metaethical commitments.

Street and de Lazari-Radek/Singer do not combine into an obvious selective instrument. Street’s dilemma pressures realist accounts of moral knowledge and offers a constructivist direction grounded in evaluative starting points. That does not automatically yield a species-transcendent impartial criterion. De Lazari-Radek and Singer seek to preserve impartial principles through a more realist epistemology. Following them risks reintroducing the moral realism that the “unoccupied” criterion appeared designed to avoid.

The post-Street literature also attacks both the epistemic inference and the empirical evolutionary story. Kahane argues that evolutionary debunking arguments carry demanding and often hidden presuppositions; Vavova distinguishes several different debunking structures rather than treating causal influence as automatically defeating; FitzPatrick argues that some versions require supplementary philosophical premises that risk begging the question. ([Wiley Online Library][7])

There is also a boomerang problem. Aversion to suffering, preference for autonomy, opposition to domination and equal consideration all have plausible evolutionary and cultural genealogies. The more powerful the debunking instrument becomes, the harder it is to stop it from attacking the thin set.

**Severity:** Substantive and potentially fatal.

**Tractability:** Plausibly intractable. A chapter may expose the problem more clearly without solving it. The project needs a positive moral epistemology, not merely selective genealogical suspicion.

**Likely examiner references:** Kahane, “Evolutionary Debunking Arguments”; Vavova, “Debunking Evolutionary Debunking” and “Evolutionary Debunking of Moral Realism”; Shafer-Landau; FitzPatrick; Arnon Levy on the empirical evolutionary premises. ([PhilPapers][8])

## Task 2: Alternative routes with the same thesis shape

### Alternative route 1: The matched alien-flourishing worlds

Replace the solitary-probe deprivation case with two closely matched successor worlds.

#### World H

The inhabitants preserve recognisably human cultural and moral forms:

* human languages or descendants of them;
* human artistic canons;
* family-like relationships;
* recognisably human virtues;
* human historical memory;
* human religious and philosophical traditions.

#### World X

The inhabitants preserve none of those historically specific forms. Nevertheless, stipulate that they possess:

* robust positive welfare;
* autonomous projects;
* reciprocal recognition;
* social cooperation;
* intellectual discovery;
* aesthetic or creative activity that humans cannot interpret;
* meaningful temporal identity;
* diverse and revisable conceptions of the good;
* low suffering and non-domination.

The two worlds are matched as far as possible for welfare, agency, diversity, achievement, epistemic success and relational richness. The difference is historical, cultural and species-specific realisation.

The question becomes:

> Does genealogical continuity with human values constitute an additional necessary condition for a future to be good, when the goods normally used to defend that continuity are otherwise instantiated?

This route directly supports the limited thesis that **the full historically specific human inheritance is not necessary**. It does not pretend that friendship, creativity or meaning cease to matter when their familiar objects disappear. Instead, it asks whether their human realisations are uniquely necessary.

#### What this fixes

It eliminates the inference from non-instantiability to dispensability. It gives the alien future positive axiological content. It also forces an answer to the individuation problem: which properties must remain invariant for an alien activity to count as a realisation of a value rather than merely an arbitrary goal?

It weakens the more radical interpretation of the project. The conclusion would be that thick human culture is replaceable by alien forms of flourishing, not that thick value as such is unnecessary. That is a cost, but it is probably the strongest defensible version of the fixed thesis.

---

### Alternative route 2: An ontologically generalised original position

Construct a choice situation in which parties know that the future will contain moral patients or agents but do not know whether they will be:

* biological or digital;
* individual or collective;
* embodied or distributed;
* short-lived or temporally extended;
* highly social or weakly relational;
* human-descended or independently evolved;
* phenomenologically familiar or radically alien.

They know that each possible kind of entity may be vulnerable to suffering, domination, irreversible foreclosure, preference manipulation and resource deprivation. They do not know which conception of flourishing they will possess.

Ask what constitutional principles they would choose for the generation and coexistence of future forms of life. Plausible candidates might include protections against extreme suffering, domination, irreversible destruction and comprehensive lock-in, together with procedures for revising inherited norms.

The relevant conclusion would be:

> Impartially situated parties would protect the conditions under which diverse forms of flourishing can arise; they would not require every future form of life to inherit a comprehensive human doctrine.

#### What this fixes

It gives operational content to the thin/open-future commitment. Instead of declaring the thin set uniquely non-parochial, it derives constraints from exposure to possible positions.

It also changes the role of impartiality. Impartiality structures a choice procedure rather than pretending to determine value directly.

#### What it does not fix

The result remains highly sensitive to:

* what the parties know;
* whether they maximise expectation or use maximin;
* whether all possible beings share primary goods;
* whether the parties represent individuals, species or value systems;
* whether creating future beings is covered at all.

The Harsanyi-Rawls dispute would therefore become central rather than decorative. The device could justify constitutional non-imposition, but it would not by itself show that any world generated under those constraints is good.

Of the two alternatives, the matched alien-flourishing route is stronger for Q2. The ontological original position is stronger for the thin-constraint and Q3 material.

## Task 3: The single strongest objection

> You have not derived your conclusion from impartiality. You have stated a principle of equal consideration and then silently substituted a contentious theory of value according to which goods that depend on a particular form of life cease to count once that form of life disappears.
>
> An impartial evaluator can recognise species-specific or practice-dependent goods without privileging the species that instantiates them. I can impartially judge that echolocation matters to bats, that linguistic culture matters to humans, and that some presently unimaginable cognitive good matters to a digital mind. Equal consideration does not require me to redescribe all goods in vocabulary that is independent of their bearers.
>
> Your solitary probe does not show that love, friendship, art or culture are unnecessary. It stipulates away the agents and practices through which those goods could exist, and then asks why their absence should matter. But their inability to find an object in that world may constitute the loss rather than dissolve it.
>
> You therefore owe me the suppressed principle connecting “there is no one here who could instantiate or desire this good” to “this good makes no contribution to the evaluation of the world.” Is that principle person-affecting, desire-satisfactionist, actualist or subjectivist? It is not impartiality.

The author’s likely reply is:

> The claim is not that nothing valuable is lost. It is only that a future can remain good without human goods because alien agents may develop rich goods of their own.

The examiner’s follow-up should be:

> Then the focal thought experiment has ceased to support the conclusion. You have conceded that human goods may be genuinely and impartially valuable, and you have introduced unspecified alien goods whose value makes the resulting world good. Any pluralist can accept that one world can contain valuable things while lacking other valuable things. That establishes only the trivial proposition that a world need not contain every good to contain some good.
>
> Which stronger proposition are you defending: that human goods are replaceable without net loss, that their preservation has no independent value, that alien goods can outweigh their loss, or that humans should refrain from loading them? What feature of impartiality, rather than an unstated substantive axiology, establishes that proposition?

There is no adequate answer available in the current draft.

## Task 4: Missing interlocutors

### 1. Harsanyi on the veil of ignorance

Rawls cannot serve as generic evidence for an impartial lineage without the Harsanyi dispute. Similar ignorance constructions produce utilitarian or maximin conclusions depending on the decision theory and information supplied to the parties. This directly attacks the claim that impartiality has a stable substantive output. ([PhilPapers][9])

Required: Harsanyi, especially “Can the Maximin Principle Serve as a Basis for Morality?”

### 2. Person-affecting versus impersonal axiology

The question “what is lost if no surviving entity misses it?” is a population-axiology question. It cannot be handled merely through Sidgwick, Singer and Parfit as representatives of impartiality.

Required: explicit treatment of person-affecting views, impersonal total views and objective-list approaches. Greaves’s “Population Axiology” is the obvious map; Broome and the post-Parfit literature are then unavoidable. ([PhilPapers][4])

### 3. The agent-neutral/agent-relative reasons literature

The draft currently treats the local or human standpoint largely as a source of bias. The standard objection is that agent-relative reasons and legitimate partiality need not be errors awaiting impartial correction.

Required: Scheffler, *The Rejection of Consequentialism*, and more explicit use of Nagel’s distinction between agent-relative and agent-neutral reasons. Scheffler’s account is specifically concerned with preserving space for the personal standpoint against totalising consequentialist demands. ([PagePlace][10])

### 4. Value pluralism and social forms

The claim that values lack purchase outside their originating form of life touches directly on whether socially constituted goods can nevertheless be objectively valuable.

Required: Raz, *The Morality of Freedom*, especially the relation between values, social forms, autonomy and valuable options. Without this, the chapter risks assuming that dependence on a practice implies merely parochial value. ([scholarship.law.columbia.edu][5])

### 5. Ideal-observer and constructivist theories

“Criterion, not posture” does not remove the need to explain how an impersonal standard is specified and gains authority.

Required: Firth’s ideal-observer theory as the direct historical comparison; Rawls’s Kantian constructivism as the alternative procedural model. Firth matters because specifying an ideal observer’s interests and responses reveals how much substantive content is hidden inside ostensibly formal impartiality. ([JSTOR][2])

### 6. The critical evolutionary-debunking literature

Street and de Lazari-Radek/Singer are not enough. They provide the opposing poles that generate the project’s problem; they do not establish that the desired selective middle is available.

Required at minimum:

* Guy Kahane, “Evolutionary Debunking Arguments”;
* Katia Vavova, “Debunking Evolutionary Debunking”;
* Russ Shafer-Landau on evolutionary debunking and realism;
* William FitzPatrick’s realist response;
* Arnon Levy on whether the evolutionary hypotheses used by debunkers are empirically adequate. ([Wiley Online Library][7])

### 7. A substantive theory of world value

This is not one missing citation. It is the conspicuous missing interlocutor of the entire argument.

The chapter discusses impartiality, autonomy, suffering and alien values, but never commits to an account of what makes a world good. Until it engages at least one of the following families, Q2 has no determinate truth conditions:

* welfare consequentialism;
* objective-list theories;
* perfectionism;
* desire or preference theories;
* person-affecting views;
* impersonal pluralism;
* buck-passing accounts of value.

The eventual chapter cannot remain officially neutral among these. Their disagreement is exactly what determines whether a solitary non-suffering probe, an experienceless optimiser or a culturally alien civilisation counts as good.
