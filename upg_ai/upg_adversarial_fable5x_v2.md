# Task 1 — Independent demolition

**Problem 1. "Arbitrary" has no content, and the draft gives two non-equivalent tests without noticing.**

The criterion is "no arbitrary privileging of person, place, time, or species." Arbitrariness is not a primitive: a privileging is arbitrary only relative to a standard of what counts as a relevant difference, and that standard *is* an axiology. Privileging sentient beings over rocks is arbitrary from a maximally neutral standpoint unless you already hold that welfare matters; privileging welfare-bearers is exactly the kind of move the criterion is supposed to adjudicate, not presuppose. So the criterion is either empty (everything is arbitrary from nowhere, including non-production of suffering, and nothing survives the test — not even your thin set) or it borrows a substantive theory of value, in which case that theory, not the criterion, does all the work, and the theory is undefended in this chapter and deferred to a debunking argument that cannot supply it (debunking is subtractive; it cannot generate the positive account of relevance that "arbitrary" needs).

This is where the criterion/posture move fails to escape Williams rather than relocating him. Williams's deep point — sharpest in "The Human Prejudice," which the draft does not cite — is not that the cosmic standpoint is psychologically unoccupiable but that it is *contentless*: there is no cosmic point of view from which the "speciesism is arbitrary" charge can even be formulated. Converting occupation into criterion-application doesn't answer that; the criterion inherits the emptiness.

The draft also states two different tests as if they were one: "apply a constraint (i.e. no arbitrary privileging…)" versus asking "why that loss is a loss from any point of view but the reader's own." The second quantifies over *occupied* standpoints — does anyone else lose? — which is structurally the occupied-Cosmic-Host evaluation the draft explicitly distinguishes itself from. These come apart: a loss could fail the quantifier test (no other actual perspective registers it) while passing the constraint test (its badness is statable non-indexically), and vice versa. An examiner will ask which is canonical, and each answer creates different problems.

Severity: embarrassing at viva if unaddressed. Tractability: plausibly intractable as scoped — this is Sidgwick's own unsolved problem (see Task 4), and the chapter's architecture assumes it has a solution. Sources an examiner cites: Williams, "The Human Prejudice"; Sidgwick, *Methods* concluding chapter.

**Problem 2. The redescription test equivocates between statability and evaluability, and "good" is threshold-ambiguous — and there is a concrete counterexample.**

"Which candidate losses can still be *stated* once [the human vantage] is set aside" is not a well-formed filter. Every loss has a vantage-free descriptive restatement ("relational states of type R do not obtain in this world" — physics suffices) and every loss's *evaluative* content is exactly as vantage-dependent as the background axiology says it is. Reading (a): descriptive statability — everything passes. Reading (b): evaluative sustainability without a parochial axiology — but then "suffering is bad" faces the identical burden (why does valence matter from no vantage?), and the test filters nothing asymmetrically unless the asymmetry is imported from a theory of value. Predictably, the written chapter will apply reading (b) to love and reading (a)-adjacent charity to suffering. That's the equivocation engine of the whole argument.

Compounding this: "count as good" is never disambiguated between (i) net-positive, (ii) not worse than the thick-value continuation, (iii) among the best achievable. Thesis (i) is nearly uncontroversial — Bostrom and MacAskill can grant that a thick-value-free future *could* clear zero — and too weak to motivate the Q3 commitment to thin-only value-loading. Theses (ii)/(iii) are the interesting claims, and nothing in the eliminative route can establish them, because showing complaints inadmissible never shows a world *good*, let alone as-good-as an alternative.

Counterexample to "fewer such losses survive this test than is commonly assumed": the frustration of the strong, thick-laden preferences of ~100 billion actual humans about the future *is* statable without the human vantage — "a very large number of strong preferences of moral patients are frustrated" is an agent-neutral bad on any desire-satisfaction view, and the criterion's own temporal-impartiality clause forbids discounting present and past preferrers. To exclude this loss you must be a hedonist about welfare. See Problem 3 for why that's fatal.

Severity: substantive shading to embarrassing. Tractability: fixable with a chapter *only if* a reading of "good" is chosen and a positive axiology supplied; as drafted, it collapses when Chapter 2 is written.

**Problem 3. The planned debunking machinery delivers hedonism, and hedonism un-brackets the consciousness question. Internal architectural incoherence.**

De Lazari-Radek & Singer's selective debunking is not a general-purpose tool; its output is specific. What survives their evolutionary filter is the hedonic axiom — pleasure good, pain bad — vindicated precisely because it (allegedly) lacks a fitness-tracking pedigree. If Chapter 5 runs their argument, the surviving axiology is hedonistic. But then the goodness of the probe-world depends *entirely* on whether the probe has valenced experience — and the draft declares: "Nor do I address questions of whether they are conscious or sentient." You cannot bracket sentience and rest the criterion's defence on dLR&S. Under their surviving axiology, an experienceless probe-universe contains literally nothing good; a valenced one is good in proportion to its hedonic states; either way, the bracketed question is the whole question. Note this is distinct from the already-listed "selectivity is attackable" concern: even if selectivity *succeeds perfectly*, the residue it leaves makes your bracketing strategy incoherent.

Severity: embarrassing when Chapter 5 exists. Tractability: plausibly intractable without either (a) swapping debunking sources for ones whose residue isn't hedonic (good luck — Street's argument taken neat spares nothing), or (b) un-bracketing consciousness, which restructures the thesis.

**Problem 4. The argument from silence is self-undermining given the draft's own frame-problem commitments.**

The probe is "chosen to maximize inferential distance." The method section independently asserts that at such distances "language ceases to be a reliable instrument" — that's the stated rationale for the votive object and the Wittgensteinian showing-move. But the central inference of the chapter is: *the reader cannot articulate the loss impartially, therefore fewer losses survive.* At maximal inferential distance, inarticulacy is at least as well explained by the failure of the reader's evaluative competence as by the absence of loss. The chapter's own methodology supplies the debunking of its own null result. The scenario selection and the epistemics are in direct tension: the further the scenario is from human forms of life, the less evidential weight the reader's failure to state a loss can carry. (This is deeper than the editorial note about knob-settings driving acceptance: even at neutral knob-settings, silence is uninformative out there.)

Severity: substantive. Tractability: hard — needs a principled account of when inability-to-state is evidence of absence rather than evidence of distance, and no such account is gestured at.

**Problem 5. The thin set is thick.**

Feinberg's open future is not a thin side-constraint; it is comprehensive post-Enlightenment liberalism — autonomy as the master value, the child as proto-chooser whose future choosing has intrinsic worth. Williams would call it exactly the kind of parochial inheritance the criterion is supposed to screen. "Resource satiability" is not a value at all but a motivational-architecture property — which is precisely the conflation of "distinct axiological, engineering, and constitutive claims" the draft accuses Yudkowsky of. "Non-production of suffering" privileges sentient beings (loops into Problems 1 and 3). And applying Feinberg to the *solitary* probe requires the probe to be an autonomy-bearing patient — un-bracketing patienthood again. The thin/thick line, as populated, is a liberal thick set relabelled.

Severity: substantive. Tractability: chapter-fixable if the thin set is *derived from* the criterion rather than borrowed from liberal theory — but Problem 1 says the criterion may generate nothing.

**Cosmetic, briefly:** Parfit's convergence claim is mischaracterized (it concerns Kantian contractualism and rule-consequentialism converging on the Triple Theory, not "the intuition of impersonality"); Bentham "extended moral status" overstates a footnote; and Rawls does not belong in a cosmic-impartiality lineage — the veil is a device for justice among cooperating members of a closed society, and Rawls explicitly declined to extend the contract to animals, i.e. he *rejected* the generalization the lineage narrative attributes to the tradition. Individually cosmetic; jointly they signal to an examiner that the lineage section is decorative rather than load-bearing.

# Task 2 — Alternative routes

**Route A: constructive, matched-baseline comparison.** Replace the eliminative test ("state the loss impartially") with a positive one: identify a small set of goods that plausibly survive impartial scrutiny — understanding of the universe is the obvious candidate, since it is instantiable without relationality and even dLR&S treat it sympathetically; possibly complexity, achievement — and argue the probe-world instantiates enough of them. Then run the TE as a *two-world comparison*: world A, thick-value human continuation; world B, probe-civilization with stipulated matched totals of the surviving goods. The question becomes whether thick values add value *at matched baselines*, which is your actual thesis (reading (ii) of "good"). This fixes, by construction, the threshold ambiguity (Problem 2), the argument-from-silence (Problem 4 — you no longer rely on inarticulacy), and the burden asymmetry, at the cost of defending a minimal objective list — a cost you are already paying implicitly, since Chapter 5 must deliver *some* surviving axiology.

**Route B: give "arbitrary" formal content via permutation invariance.** Reformulate the criterion social-choice style: an evaluation is admissible iff invariant under permutation of which kind of being occupies which role, among bearers relevantly similar in welfare-relevant capacities. Lineage: Hare's universalizability, anonymity axioms in Rawls/Sen. This answers the emptiness charge (Problem 1) by making the test a concrete symmetry check the reader can actually run, and — importantly — it forces the "relevantly similar" clause into the open, so the sentience question is confronted rather than bracketed. It converts your weakest joint into an explicit premise.

The current eliminative route is *not* the strongest available: it can at best show complaints inadmissible, never show a world good, and so cannot support any reading of Q2 stronger than (i). A hybrid of A and B is strictly dominant.

# Task 3 — The one objection, at full strength

"Mr. examiner speaking. Your criterion forbids 'arbitrary' privileging. Tell me what makes a privileging non-arbitrary without presupposing a theory of value. You cannot: 'arbitrary' means 'not tracking a relevant difference,' and relevance is fixed by an axiology. The only axiology your thesis plan supplies arrives in Chapter 5 via de Lazari-Radek and Singer — whose argument, if it works at all, vindicates hedonism and nothing else. Hedonism makes the goodness of your probe-world turn entirely on whether the probe feels anything, which is the one question you have declared out of scope. So: either your criterion is empty and filters nothing — including your thin set — or it is secretly hedonist and your thesis stands or falls on the consciousness question you've bracketed. Which?"

Your most likely reply: the criterion is formal — a symmetry constraint — and privileging welfare-bearers is non-arbitrary because grounded in the capacity to be benefited or harmed, which is itself perspective-free.

Follow-up: "Then 'capacity for welfare' is carrying the entire argument, and you owe me a theory of welfare. On desire theories, the frustrated future-directed preferences of every human who has lived are impartially statable losses — your test lets thick values back in through the preferences of their bearers. On objective-list theories, love and friendship are canonical list items — thick values are *constituents* of welfare and their absence is a welfare loss statable from anywhere. The only theory of welfare that keeps thick values off the ledger is hedonism, and hedonism needs the probe to feel. You have a trilemma, not a criterion. Pick a horn."

# Task 4 — Missing interlocutors

**Samuel Scheffler, *Death and the Afterlife* and *Why Worry About Future Generations?*** The canonical contemporary treatment of whether human continuation is a condition of things mattering, and of conservatism about existing value. A thesis arguing thick-value discontinuity can be good, which doesn't engage Scheffler, will read as unaware of the main opposing literature.

**G. A. Cohen, "Rescuing Conservatism."** The argument that existing bearers of value merit preservation *as the particular things they are*, even against replacements of equal or greater value — a direct, purportedly non-arbitrary ground for privileging the actual, i.e., a counterexample-generator against your criterion.

**Williams, "The Human Prejudice" specifically.** The draft cites Williams's Sidgwick critique; this essay is the direct engagement with species-privileging and the emptiness of the cosmic standpoint, and it is the text a hostile examiner will have in hand.

**Population axiology.** Q2 evaluates a future containing different beings than the alternative — that is a population-ethics comparison, full stop. Non-identity, the repugnant conclusion, Arrhenius's impossibility results, Greaves's survey: "evaluated impartially across time" walks straight into this literature, and its total absence will be read as not knowing the terrain the thesis stands on.

**Sidgwick's own dualism of practical reason.** Your lineage narrative presents the point of view of the universe as the tradition's achievement; Sidgwick's concluding chapter presents his failure to show it overrides the egoistic standpoint. The founding text contains the objection to your criterion. Omitting this while citing Sidgwick approvingly is the kind of gap examiners enjoy.

**Nagel — misused rather than missing.** *The View from Nowhere* argues agent-relative reasons remain *real* when viewed objectively; Nagel is a rival to your criterion's exclusivity, not a sympathetic precursor. As drafted he's cited on your side of the ledger; an examiner who knows the book will flip him.

One closing note on Part B calibration: your editorial note 3 (knobs) underestimates its own severity — the problem is not just that favourable knob-settings drive acceptance, but that maximal inferential distance makes the reader's silence evidentially worthless (Problem 4 above). That is a different and worse defect, and a sentence pre-empting knob-selection won't touch it.
