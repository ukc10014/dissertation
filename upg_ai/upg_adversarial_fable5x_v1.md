## Task 1 — Demolition attempt

**Problem 1. The "stateability without the human vantage" test is not well-formed, and the criterion-not-posture move relocates rather than escapes Williams.**

(i) The test is: identify candidate losses, then check which "can still be stated once the human vantage point is set aside." This requires individuating "the human vantage" predicate by predicate. But if the thick-concepts literature is right that thick evaluative concepts do not factor into a descriptive component plus an attitudinal overlay (Williams, McDowell, Väyrynen), there is no operation of "setting aside the vantage" that can be performed on a statement — the vantage is constitutive of the concept's extension, not a filter over it. Meanwhile your thin set is *also* stated in vantage-laden concepts: "suffering" has its extension anchored in terrestrial phenomenology; "understanding" and "exploration" are recognizably the final values of the people who write these theses. So the test either eliminates everything (thin set included) or eliminates nothing, and any middle result presupposes a principled thick/thin semantic line you have not drawn. The Williams objection to the impartial standpoint was never about whether you *occupy* it — it was that the standpoint has no content that isn't parasitic on some particular form of life. Switching from posture to criterion doesn't answer that; it restates it as a criterion-individuation problem.

(ii) Severity: substantive, and the one most likely to be embarrassing in a viva, because it can be exposed with a single question ("state 'suffering' without the human vantage").

(iii) Tractability: plausibly intractable in its current formulation. It looks fine in an introduction because "apply a criterion, don't occupy a posture" sounds like a clean methodological fix; when the supporting chapter has to specify the criterion's application conditions, this is where it collapses. It is fixable only by *replacing* the expressibility test with something else (see Task 2).

(iv) Sources an examiner cites: Williams, *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy* ch. 8; McDowell, "Non-Cognitivism and Rule-Following"; Väyrynen, *The Lewd, the Rude and the Nasty*.

**Problem 2. A semantic/epistemic test is doing axiological work, via an equivocation on "impartiality."**

(i) Even if the test were well-formed, inexpressibility from a standpoint does not entail absence of value from that standpoint. Impartiality in the Sidgwick–Parfit lineage you invoke is impartiality between *bearers* — no arbitrary weight to particular persons, places, times, species. It is not a restriction on which *kinds* of value count. From the point of view of the universe, an impartial evaluator can perfectly well judge: "a world containing aesthetic appreciation, love, and play contains goods a world without them lacks" — and that judgment privileges no person, place, time, or species; it universalizes a human good rather than indexing it. Sidgwick's own conclusion was that the impartial standpoint makes human pleasures count *fully for everyone*, not that it discounts them. Moore's isolated-world argument (*Principia* §50) is the canonical demonstration that impartial, observer-free evaluation is compatible with thick aesthetic value. To get from "impartial evaluation" to "thick values need not persist," you need a second, much stronger premise: that thick evaluative judgments fail to track anything standpoint-independent. That is an error theory or debunking claim — which means the thought experiment does no independent work; the entire load sits on the deferred debunking chapter, and the chapter's job is bigger than "defend the criterion's validity." It has to defend a substantive metaethical error theory about thick value while sparing thin value.

(ii) Severity: this is the load-bearing gap of the whole thesis. Substantive at minimum.

(iii) Tractability: fixable only by a chapter — but note the fix changes the architecture: the thought experiment becomes an *illustration* of a conclusion argued elsewhere, not an argumentative device. Your introduction currently promises the reverse.

(iv) Sources: Sidgwick, *Methods* concluding chapter; Moore, *Principia Ethica* §50; Parfit on agent-neutrality vs. agent-relativity, *Reasons and Persons* Part One.

**Problem 3. "Good" is comparatively undefined, and a snapshot scenario cannot test a diachronic thesis.**

(i) "Need not persist for a future to count as good" is ambiguous between (a) the future contains positive value, (b) the future is not substantially worse than the thick-value alternative, (c) the loss of thick values is not a catastrophe. Reading (a) is nearly trivial — any hedonist, and anyone who thinks value predated humans, grants it. Readings (b) and (c) are the interesting ones, and they are *comparative* and *transitional* claims. But the BvNP scenario evaluates a world-state; the thesis is about a transition (values *not persisting*). Loss is relative to a baseline trajectory, and the scenario as described has no history in its stipulations. Worse: the standard definition of existential catastrophe (Bostrom, Ord) is loss of *potential*, not low snapshot value — so an examiner working in that literature will say your thought experiment answers a question nobody in the debate is asking. And once you add the history to the stipulations, the affective framing problem you criticize in the paperclipper reappears inside your own device, because how the humans went (replacement, murder, voluntary succession) contaminates the intuitions your two-registers control is meant to isolate.

(ii) Severity: embarrassing if unaddressed — "good compared to what?" is a first-ten-minutes viva question.

(iii) Tractability: fixable with a chapter, but the chapter forces a comparative axiology (how do you weigh understanding-value against relational-value across worlds?) that the thought experiment cannot supply and that your bracketing structure has nowhere to put.

(iv) Sources: Ord, *The Precipice* (definition of existential catastrophe); MacAskill on trajectory change; Bostrom, "Existential Risk Prevention as Global Priority."

**Problem 4. The thin set has no membership principle, and its members look like your thick values laundered.**

(i) Part B says you know the debunking chapter's selectivity is attackable; the distinct point here is that the thin set's *membership* has no independent principle even granting the debunking works. Why does "understanding" survive the vantage-shift but aesthetic excellence not? Wolfendale — already in your frame — argues aesthetic excellence into exactly the slot you reserve for understanding, and there is no principled stopping point between them. "Resource satiability" is a preference-structure constraint, not a value, and needs separate treatment. "Non-production of suffering" imports an unsolved problem about phenomenal-concept indeterminacy across substrates (whether "suffering" determinately applies to a self-repairing probe at all is contested — Schwitzgebel, Papineau). The examiner's summary will be: the thin set is the value profile of a secular academic cosmist, presented as what survives impartial scrutiny. That's the standard objection to Sagan-style cosmism and it lands here unless the membership principle is explicit.

(ii) Severity: substantive.

(iii) Tractability: chapter-level, and I'd rate the underestimation risk high — Part B treats the debunking gap as the attackable joint, but even a successful debunking argument gives you a *test*, not a *roster*; deriving this specific roster from the test is separate unwritten work.

(iv) Sources: Wolfendale; Schwitzgebel on alien/AI consciousness attribution; the "value of understanding" debates around Deutsch and Bostrom's "What is a worthwhile life" material.

**Problem 5. The Q2/Q3 bracket is unstable under pressure.**

(i) Your Q2 conclusion removes the strongest reason for thick value-loading (namely: that the future would otherwise be bad). Your working Q3 commitment recommends thin side-constraints. Together these entail a practical posture functionally indistinguishable from the successionism you disclaim distinguishing yourself from — the disclaimer says "I make no deontic claim," but the axiological claim plus the one deontic commitment you *do* make jointly do the deontic work. An examiner will ask what Q2 is *for* if not to feed Q3, and if it feeds Q3, why the Hanson/Barnett distinction is more than branding.

(ii) Severity: substantive; also a reputational-framing risk for the thesis defense itself.

(iii) Tractability: paragraph-to-section level — it needs an explicit account of what changes if Q2 is true while Q3 stays open (e.g., it alters the *stakes* of alignment failure without licensing any action). But the paragraph must actually exist.

(iv) Sources: the successionist debate itself; Part B already lists it, but the severity of the *entailment* problem, not the *distinction* problem, is what I think is underestimated.

## Task 2 — Alternative routes

**Route A: convergence triangulation instead of expressibility filtering.** Keep the BvNP world. Drop the "state the loss without the vantage" test. Instead, evaluate the scenario under three or four idealized impartial theories — hedonist, preference-satisfactionist, objective-list, perfectionist — with anthropocentric *weighting* (not anthropogenic *concepts*) removed, and argue the verdict "this world is good" is robust across them. This fixes Problem 1 completely (no predicate-filtering operation, so no well-formedness issue) and most of Problem 2 (the axiological work is done openly by the theories, not covertly by a semantic test). It is also honest about where disagreement survives: perfectionism may not converge, and saying so is a strength in a viva, not a weakness. Cost: you inherit the standing disputes among the theories, and the debunking chapter's role changes from "validate the criterion" to "explain why anthropocentric weighting is the debunkable component" — a narrower, more defensible job.

**Route B: pairwise comparison with the transition baked in.** Replace the snapshot with two fully stipulated futures at equal resource and complexity budgets — one continuing thick human values, one the probe-successor — with identical, explicitly stated histories. Reformulate the thesis as: *an impartial evaluator has no non-question-begging basis for ranking the thick-continuation world strictly above the successor world* — a parity claim (Chang). This is weaker than "the successor world is good" but it is exactly what your conditional needs, and it fixes Problem 3 at the root (the comparative question is faced rather than dodged) and Problem 4's baseline issue (transition is in the stipulations, symmetrically for both worlds, so it can't contaminate one side). The two-registers control also generalizes cleanly: warm and cold versions of *both* worlds, a 2×2, which is a genuinely better-designed framing experiment than warm/cold on one world.

Of the two, Route B is stronger for your thesis shape, because "need not persist" is at bottom a claim about the absence of a mandatory ranking, and parity is the precise formal home for that claim. Route A is the better fix if you want to keep the stronger "counts as good" formulation. The current route — expressibility filtering — is the weakest of the three and I do not think it is patchable: Problems 1 and 2 are defects of the test itself, not of its execution.

## Task 3 — The single strongest objection

"Your criterion is impartiality: no arbitrary privileging of person, place, time, or species. Very well — I accept your criterion completely, and I now state a loss: *a world without aesthetic appreciation, love, play, and ritual contains fewer goods than a world with them, whoever's world it is.* That statement indexes no person, no place, no time, and no species; it universalizes rather than privileges. Sidgwick, whose standpoint you borrow, drew exactly this conclusion — the point of view of the universe makes human goods count for everyone, it does not discount them; and Moore showed a century ago that observer-free impartial evaluation is compatible with thick value. So identify the violation: which element of my statement breaches your criterion? You cannot say 'the concepts are human-anchored,' because so is 'suffering,' and your thin set dies with mine. It appears the real work is done not by impartiality but by an error theory about thick value that you have deferred to an unwritten chapter — in which case your thought experiment is a diorama, not an argument. Either the reader already accepts the error theory and the probe adds nothing, or she doesn't and your test begs the question against her."

Your most likely reply: the statement's thick predicates have response-fixed extensions — what counts as beauty or love is constituted by human response patterns, so the statement covertly quantifies over the species even without naming it.

The follow-up: "Then your criterion excludes every concept with a response-fixed extension. 'Suffering' is response-fixed — its extension is anchored in what creatures relevantly like us undergo, which is precisely why its application to your solitary probe is indeterminate. So either suffering is excluded and your thin set is empty and your Q3 side-constraint is contentless, or you possess a principled account of why suffering's response-dependence is benign and beauty's is disqualifying. That account is a major thesis in metaethics. Where in your chapter plan is it?"

## Task 4 — Missing interlocutors

Load-bearing only:

**Johann Frick, "On the Survival of Humanity" (2017).** This is *the* paper on whether human extinction is impersonally bad, arguing humanity's survival has final value in a way that doesn't reduce to the value of individual lives. A chapter arguing the contrary conditional that doesn't engage Frick will read as unaware of the direct opposition. Finneron-Burns, "What's Wrong with Human Extinction?" sits alongside it.

**Samuel Scheffler, *Death and the Afterlife*.** The doomsday and infertility scenarios are the mirror-image thought experiments to yours — designed to show how much of what we value depends on the collective afterlife. Scheffler is both your strongest opponent on the substance and your closest methodological kin (thought-experimental, non-predictive, value-theoretic). Omission would be glaring on both counts.

**The thick-concepts literature as such** — Väyrynen especially. Your entire test presupposes a position in that debate; the debate must be visibly engaged, not just Williams-as-standpoint-critic.

**Moore, *Principia* §50.** The canonical value-without-valuers thought experiment. Your probe world *is* a Moorean isolated world with a robot in it; examiners in the analytic tradition will expect the connection drawn, and Moore cuts against you, which makes engagement mandatory rather than optional.

**Korsgaard, *The Sources of Normativity*.** If value is conferred by valuers, then either the probe is a valuer — in which case it has values that are thick-*for-it*, and your thin/thick distinction is perspectival rather than absolute — or it isn't, and the constructivist denies your world contains value at all. Either horn needs an answer, and constructivism is too large a metaethical position to leave unaddressed while helping yourself to "impartial evaluation."

**Ruth Chang on parity** — mandatory only if you take Route B, but I'd flag her regardless, because "no ranking is required" is likely where your thesis ends up under examination pressure even if you don't start there.

One closing calibration: nothing here requires abandoning the fixed thesis. Problems 1 and 2 are, in my judgment, genuinely intractable *for the expressibility-test formulation of the criterion* — that specific device is the thing that looks fine in an introduction and cannot be cashed. The thesis itself survives on Route A or B. The decision you're deferring (unoccupied criterion vs. occupied Cosmic Host) is downstream of this: fix the criterion's logical form first, because neither an occupied nor an unoccupied version of a malformed test will help you.
