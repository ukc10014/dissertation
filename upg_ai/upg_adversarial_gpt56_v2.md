## Task 1: Independent demolition attempt

I treated Part B as sealed during the first pass. Where an independently identified problem overlaps with Part B, I discuss it only when the underlying failure is deeper than the note acknowledges.

### 1. The impartiality criterion is not an evaluative standard

> “I am asking the reader to apply a criterion: that no one individual’s good matters more, from the point of view of the universe, than any other’s; generalised, no arbitrary privileging of person, place, time, or species.”

This does not tell the reader whether the probe world is good. It is a constraint on how reasons or interests are counted, not an account of:

* what counts as someone’s good;
* whether only welfare matters;
* whether impersonal goods exist;
* whether benefits aggregate;
* whether equality, priority, rights or desert constrain aggregation;
* whether creating valuable lives is itself valuable;
* whether beauty, knowledge, agency or complexity have value independently of welfare;
* what makes a difference morally relevant rather than “arbitrary”.

Formal impartiality is compatible with radically different substantive theories. An impartial total utilitarian, negative utilitarian, prioritarian, contractualist, rights theorist and objective-list perfectionist can produce incompatible rankings of the same probe world. The philosophical literature explicitly distinguishes impartiality as a formal notion from substantive moral impartiality. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy][1])

Rawls does not solve this problem. His veil does not derive principles from symmetry alone. It contains a substantive conception of citizens, their fundamental interests, primary goods, the circumstances of justice and society as fair cooperation. Those inputs do nearly all the work. Rawls’s project is also a political conception for the basic structure of a society, not a cosmic axiology. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy][2])

The word “arbitrary” disguises the missing theory. Williams can say that species membership, shared history and concrete attachment are not arbitrary in the relevant sense. Singer can say they are. The criterion does not adjudicate that dispute. It simply applies the author’s verdict in advance by classifying the opposing considerations as arbitrary.

**Severity:** Embarrassing at viva; foundational rather than local.

**Tractability:** Plausibly intractable within the current architecture. A chapter can clarify impartiality, but clarification will not make it generate an axiology. You must either add a substantive theory of value or weaken the conclusion drastically.

**Examiner’s sources:** The literature on formal versus substantive impartiality; Rawls; Nagel’s *Equality and Partiality*; Williams on impartial morality.

### 2. The thesis faces a triviality-or-invalidity fork

> “Thick human values […] need not survive for a future to count as good, when that future is evaluated impartially.”

There are two readings.

#### Reading A: Existential

There is at least one impartial axiology under which a future without thick human values is good.

This is probably true but close to trivial. Hedonistic utilitarianism supplies an immediate example: stipulate nonhuman conscious beings with sufficiently high welfare. The BvNP, the Williams discussion, the debunking argument and the literary framing are unnecessary. The conclusion follows from the chosen axiology.

#### Reading B: Criterion-entailment

Once we impose no arbitrary privileging, we have reason to regard the future as good.

This does not follow. Impartiality underdetermines the evaluation, as above.

The draft oscillates between these. “By some impartial standard” invites Reading A. “The fixed point here is the criterion” and “fewer such losses survive this test” invite Reading B. The first is weak; the second is invalid without additional premises.

There is a third possible thesis:

> Thick human values are not necessary conditions of goodness across all admissible impartial axiologies.

That is much stronger and almost certainly false. An impartial objective-list theory can hold that friendship, aesthetic achievement, knowledge or other goods associated with human life are necessary components of the best or of a sufficiently good world, without privileging humans as a species. Objective-list theories characteristically treat items such as knowledge, achievement, friendship and autonomy as goods independently of whether agents happen to endorse them. ([PhilPapers][3])

**Severity:** Embarrassing. Until the quantifier over impartial standards is fixed, Q2 has no stable truth conditions.

**Tractability:** Fixable with a chapter only if you are willing to choose a substantive evaluative framework. Otherwise plausibly intractable.

**Known move:** Underdetermination by formal constraints; objective-list resistance; the distinction between good simpliciter, good-for and comparative betterness in value theory. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy][4])

### 3. The “what is lost?” test is not a valid test of world value

> “In such a universe, what exactly is lost?”

> “Which candidate losses can still be stated once [the human vantage] is set aside?”

The question is malformed until you specify the comparison.

Lost relative to what?

1. The actual human future?
2. An otherwise identical world containing humans?
3. The best feasible alternative?
4. A baseline of nonexistence?
5. What existing persons would otherwise have possessed?
6. What possible future beings could have possessed?

These comparisons are not equivalent.

The solitary-probe scenario makes relational goods unavailable by removing the possible bearers and objects of those goods. It then treats their inapplicability as evidence that they are dispensable. That is not an axiological inference. If I describe a universe containing one insentient crystal, friendship has no object there. It does not follow that nothing valuable is missing from that universe, or that the universe is good.

The same mistake appears in a familiar person-affecting form:

* No existing being is deprived of friendship.
* Therefore no loss has occurred.
* Therefore the world is not worse for lacking friendship.

The second inference is precisely what population ethics contests. Worlds can differ in value because different people or different kinds of beings exist in them. The fact that no numerically identical person is made worse off does not settle whether one future is worse. The nonidentity problem exists because future-directed choices frequently change who will exist, making ordinary person-to-person loss comparisons unavailable. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy][5])

Your case is more extreme: it changes not merely identities but kinds of beings, forms of welfare and the range of possible goods. That makes population ethics unavoidable, not bracketable. Parfit, Broome and Arrhenius show how difficult it is to rank populations even when welfare is relatively familiar; Arrhenius derives impossibility results from combinations of individually plausible axiological conditions. ([PhilPapers][6])

The suppressed premise is something like:

> If a candidate good has no actual bearer or application in world W, then its non-instantiation cannot make W worse.

That premise is highly controversial and probably false on any impersonal axiology.

**Severity:** Embarrassing and potentially fatal.

**Tractability:** Plausibly intractable in the present scenario. Fixing it requires an account of cross-world comparison, possible beings and foregone goods. That is a population-axiology chapter, not a paragraph.

**Examiner’s sources:** Parfit, Part Four of *Reasons and Persons*; Broome, *Weighing Lives*; Arrhenius; the nonidentity and person-affecting literatures.

### 4. The argument establishes, at most, absence of a demonstrated bad; it never establishes goodness

> “My expectation is that fewer such losses survive this test than is commonly assumed.”

Suppose every alleged loss fails your test. The result is:

> No surviving reason has yet been supplied for judging this world bad in virtue of lacking human values.

That is not:

> This world is good.

The world might be neutral, evaluatively empty, merely permissible, less bad than another world, or radically suboptimal. Your “thin” constraints are mostly negative. They exclude or limit suffering, predation, coercion and resource consumption. Negative constraints can help establish admissibility. They cannot by themselves establish positive value.

“AIs develop their own values” also does not close the gap. Having values is a psychological or functional fact. It does not follow that the objects of those values are valuable. The paperclip maximizer itself has a terminal value. You cannot reject paperclips as trivial while treating self-developed alien preferences as presumptively value-conferring without supplying a theory of fitting or authoritative valuing.

The value-theory literature distinguishes something’s being valued from there being reasons to value it. Scanlon’s buck-passing formulation, for example, understands value through reasons for appropriate responses, not through the bare fact that someone desires or endorses an object. Even that account requires specification of whose reasons, what responses and how competing reasons are weighted. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy][4])

You need at least one positive premise:

* the probe has welfare;
* its activity constitutes achievement;
* knowledge is produced;
* autonomous self-authorship is intrinsically valuable;
* its alien projects are fitting objects of endorsement;
* complexity or flourishing has impersonal value.

Each would be substantive and contestable. None follows from impartiality.

**Severity:** Embarrassing. This is a direct non sequitur from “not shown bad” to “good”.

**Tractability:** Fixable with a chapter if you adopt a positive axiology. Intractable if the project insists that the impartial constraint can remain neutral among theories of the good.

**Known move:** The distinction between right, permissible, good, good-for and better; fitting-attitude and buck-passing accounts; objective-list theories.

### 5. “Values surviving” equivocates between four different propositions

The draft moves repeatedly among:

1. Humans or human cultures continuing to exist.
2. Successors endorsing or being motivated by human values.
3. Goods associated with human life continuing to be instantiated.
4. Information about human concepts and practices remaining available.

These are independent.

> “Thick human values […] need not survive.”

This sounds like 1, 3 or 4.

> “Thick human values are unsuitable as a value endowment.”

This concerns 2.

> “Future AIs develop their own set of rich relational values […] this is compatible with my thesis.”

This suggests that nonhuman realizations of some abstract goods count as abandoning human values.

But if alien successors have friendship-like relations, aesthetic practices, intellectual achievement, grief-like responses or collective forms of care, then one can describe the case in two opposed ways:

* Thick human values have disappeared because the concrete concepts, rituals and phenomenology are alien.
* The relevant values have survived through nonhuman realizations.

The argument cannot proceed until “value” is typed. At minimum distinguish:

* a value concept;
* an evaluative belief;
* a motivational disposition;
* a practice;
* a good-making property;
* a token object of value;
* a cultural implementation of a more abstract value.

Otherwise the project will win by changing individuation criteria. When defending succession, “love” will mean a parochial human implementation. When explaining why the successor world is positively valuable, an alien analogue will be treated as enough like love, community or achievement to do the axiological work.

**Severity:** Substantive now; embarrassing once the full chapter attempts a formal argument.

**Tractability:** Fixable with a chapter, but only before the thesis is defended. It is not terminological housekeeping. Different definitions produce different theses.

**Examiner’s sources:** General value theory; fitting-attitude theories; Scanlon on value and reasons. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy][7])

### 6. “Criterion, not posture” does not escape Williams

> “The distinction between applying a criterion and occupying a posture matters.”

Not enough.

The draft interprets Williams as principally objecting that human agents cannot psychologically occupy an impersonal standpoint. His stronger objection concerns the authority and integration of impartial morality. An impartial principle can alienate an agent from projects, attachments and reasons that constitute the agent’s identity even if the agent applies it perfectly well from an ordinary first-person perspective.

In other words, Williams does not need to say:

> I cannot imagine or occupy your cosmic standpoint.

He can say:

> I understand the criterion, and I deny that my relations, loyalties, projects and human attachments must justify themselves before it.

Your distinction changes the cognitive procedure, not the normative hierarchy. The criterion still acts as a tribunal before which local reasons survive only if they can be redescribed impersonally.

The problem becomes sharper with species. Williams’s “The Human Prejudice” directly challenges the assumption that special concern for humans is straightforwardly analogous to racism or sexism. He treats human loyalty as potentially more like an attachment internal to ethical life than an irrational failure to notice equal standing. Whether he succeeds is secondary. The draft currently stipulates his central position out of court by calling the privilege “arbitrary”. The omitted essay is directly about the proposition you need to defeat. ([JSTOR][8])

Nagel does not support the reduction either. His project is structured around an unresolved conflict between personal and impersonal standpoints, not the elimination of the former by redescribing the latter as a criterion. ([OUP Academic][9])

**Severity:** Substantive and possibly fatal to the claimed answer to Williams.

**Tractability:** Plausibly intractable if the thesis requires the impartial criterion to have unconditional jurisdiction. Fixable only by allowing justified partial reasons and explaining the limited domain in which impartiality governs.

**Examiner’s sources:** Williams, “A Critique of Utilitarianism”, “Persons, Character and Morality”, *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy*, “The Human Prejudice”; Nagel, *Equality and Partiality*; Scheffler, *The Rejection of Consequentialism*.

### 7. The “removal of the human vantage” is either impossible or evidentially worthless

> “Which candidate losses can still be stated once [the human vantage point] is set aside.”

There are two interpretations, both bad.

#### Semantic interpretation

Remove concepts whose meaning depends on human forms of life.

Then almost every evaluative predicate becomes suspect, including:

* suffering;
* autonomy;
* satiability;
* agency;
* harm;
* value;
* goodness;
* arbitrary privilege.

The thin set does not survive the purge more securely than love, beauty or fellowship. “Suffering” is not delivered in a perspective-free physical vocabulary. It depends on theories of experience, valence, interests and moral significance.

#### Epistemic interpretation

Discount judgments whose causal origin lies in human evolution or culture.

Then the test confuses genealogy with warrant. A judgment can have an evolutionary cause and nevertheless be true or justified. Conversely, a judgment’s apparent distance from direct evolutionary pressure does not establish truth-tracking.

More basically, subtracting the only standpoint from which the inquiry is conducted creates silence, not impartial knowledge. Failure to articulate a loss after removing the conceptual resources needed to identify it is not evidence that no loss exists.

There are live metaethical alternatives in which value is perspectival or “tethered” to creatures for whom things can go well, while reasons still extend impartially across creatures. Korsgaard explicitly develops this combination: value arises from creaturely points of view, but one cannot infer that only one species’ good matters. ([Notebook][10]) McDowell and Wiggins likewise represent sensibility-based approaches on which dependence on evaluative capacities does not collapse into arbitrary subjectivism. ([PhilPapers][11])

These positions block the draft’s binary:

* either value survives removal of all situated perspectives;
* or it is parochial and debunkable.

**Severity:** Plausibly intractable. This is a defect in the proposed method, not merely in its conclusion.

**Tractability:** Requires an explicit metaethics of standpoint, value dependence and intersubjective warrant. A paragraph will not repair it.

**Known move:** Sensibility theory; response-dependence; tethered value; Williams’s humanistic conception of ethical inquiry.

### 8. The debunking problem is more severe than Part B states

Part B identifies the problem as selectivity: how to debunk thick values while sparing impartiality and suffering. That is only one of three failures.

#### First: Street does not provide an argument for impartiality

Street’s Darwinian dilemma targets realist theories that posit stance-independent evaluative truths while accepting pervasive evolutionary influence on our evaluative judgments. Her argument is not:

> Evolution debunks partiality, therefore universal benevolence is true.

It is a dilemma for realism about value. ([PhilPapers][12])

If your project adopts Street’s constructivist route, you need to explain how impartiality is constructed from the standpoint of agents whose practical identities and evaluative attitudes are themselves historically situated. If it adopts realism, it inherits Street’s epistemological problem.

#### Second: de Lazari-Radek and Singer’s exception for impartiality is not a stable bridge

Their strategy depends on the claim that universal benevolence is sufficiently independent of evolutionary influence to retain epistemic authority while egoistic or partial intuitions are debunked. Guy Kahane argues that the evolutionary appeal becomes self-defeating even if significant parts of their story are granted. ([PMC][13])

Your case is harder than theirs. You do not merely want to debunk egoism. You want to debunk or reduce evidential weight across love, aesthetic judgment, cultural continuity, relational goods and species partiality, while preserving:

* the badness of suffering;
* the value of open futures;
* autonomy;
* equal consideration;
* resource moderation;
* the authority of impartiality.

That pattern will look selected to fit the desired outcome unless supported by a general epistemic principle.

#### Third: successful debunking would not prove Q2

Suppose the argument defeats confidence in every intuition that the probe world is desolate. It still does not establish that the world is good. Debunking removes evidence; it does not manufacture positive value. At most it returns you to suspension of judgment.

Part B calls this “the single most attackable joint”. That understates it. The debunking track cannot do the positive work assigned to it even if its selectivity problem is solved.

**Severity:** Plausibly intractable.

**Tractability:** Requires a full metaethical position plus a separate positive axiology. A selective evolutionary story alone cannot repair it.

**Examiner’s sources:** Street; de Lazari-Radek and Singer; Kahane, “Evolution and Impartiality”; broader evolutionary debunking literature.

### 9. The proposed “thin” set contains comprehensive and controversial commitments

> “Non-production of suffering, resource satiability, and similar.”

Neither item is yet a thin side-constraint.

“Non-production of suffering” could mean:

1. Never cause any suffering.
2. Never intentionally cause suffering.
3. Do not create net suffering.
4. Minimise suffering.
5. Give suffering lexical priority.
6. Keep suffering below a threshold.
7. Avoid severe involuntary suffering.
8. Avoid unreasonable risk of suffering.

These produce radically different worlds. A literal prohibition on producing suffering may prohibit creating vulnerable sentient life, undertaking uncertain projects, permitting autonomous risk-taking or maintaining ecological systems. A minimisation rule approaches negative utilitarianism, which is a comprehensive axiology rather than a side-constraint.

“Resource satiability” is not itself a moral value. It is a property of a preference or optimisation architecture. To derive a side-constraint, you need a theory of why resource acquisition is limited: rights, opportunity costs, distributive fairness, preservation of possible lives, precaution or bargaining. Each imports substantive content.

Feinberg’s open-future argument does not give you a neutral template. The right is generally understood as an autonomy right held in trust for a future person who is expected to become capable of self-determination. ([PMC][14]) Applying it to designed AI requires assumptions about:

* diachronic identity;
* future agency;
* interests before those interests exist;
* which options are valuable enough to preserve;
* whether the created being has a claim against its creator;
* whether some motivational architectures are wrong to create.

The “open” future is not value-free. Raz’s autonomy-based perfectionism makes the difficulty explicit: autonomy requires an adequate range of worthwhile options, not merely unblocked choice. ([OUP Academic][15]) The moment you distinguish worthwhile from worthless or vicious options, the thin constitution contains a theory of the good.

**Severity:** Substantive; potentially embarrassing once presented as a settled working commitment.

**Tractability:** Fixable with a chapter if the thesis tolerates a fairly rich thin set. Intractable if “thin” must mean neutral among comprehensive values.

**Examiner’s sources:** Feinberg and criticisms of the right to an open future; Raz on autonomy and perfectionism; liberal neutrality debates.

### 10. The solitary probe does not actually eliminate relational objects

> “The environments these agents inhabit might not allow for such relationality.”

Physical solitude removes contemporaneous embodied peers. It does not remove relationality.

The probe can stand in relations to:

* its creators;
* humanity’s remembered history;
* its mission;
* its past and future stages;
* copied or simulated agents;
* dormant subagents;
* possible recipients of its signals;
* represented but absent beings;
* discovered environments;
* artifacts it produces;
* epistemic objects;
* the source and justification of its own goals.

The draft itself allows memory, long-term identity, self-repair, introspection, replication and distributed subcomponents. These features create abundant candidates for diachronic, reflexive and internal relations.

You could stipulate that the probe has no concern for any of them. But then the lack of relational value comes from its motivational architecture, not its environment. Or you could stipulate that none of them can ground relational value. But that is the conclusion being argued for.

The scenario therefore does not support the general claim:

> Radically nonhuman environments make human relational values inapplicable.

At most it supports:

> Some highly engineered solitary agents might lack the capacities or concerns through which certain interpersonal human goods are realised.

That is too weak to carry the transition to “a universe largely populated with similar entities”.

Scheffler is especially relevant here because his work argues that the value and intelligibility of present projects can depend on future people and continuing collective practices, even where the agent has no direct interpersonal relation with those future individuals. ([Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews][16]) Your inventory of possible relational objects is much too narrow.

**Severity:** Substantive. It becomes embarrassing if the solitary case remains the central intuitive mechanism.

**Tractability:** Fixable only by changing the scenario or narrowing the thesis. Adding a sentence about multipolarity will not solve the internal relationality of the solitary case.

**Examiner’s sources:** Scheffler; Raz on social forms; literature on narrative and diachronic identity.

## Task 2: Alternative routes with the same thesis shape

The current route is not the strongest available. It tries to establish a modest modal thesis by taking on maximal impartiality, evolutionary debunking, population ethics, value response-dependence and the Williams objection simultaneously. Most of that debt is unnecessary.

### Alternative 1: Alien flourishing and species-label permutation

#### Scenario

Construct two fully specified worlds.

**World H:** A population of human or recognisably posthuman beings has lives containing welfare, agency, knowledge, achievement, relationships, aesthetic activity and stable social cooperation. Its concrete practices and concepts descend from human culture.

**World A:** A population of independently evolved or radically transformed beings has lives that are equally robustly good-for-them. They possess agency, achievement, understanding, reciprocal relations and forms of fulfilment, but none of their concepts, practices, affective structures or cultural inheritance is recognisably human.

Do not make World A solitary. Do not deprive it of positive goods. Make it alien through realization, not through deprivation.

Then perform a species-label test:

> If the morally relevant features of the inhabitants and their lives are fully described, changing the labels “human” and “alien”, or “biological” and “artificial”, cannot by itself alter the world’s ranking.

This is a narrow permutation-invariance condition. It does not pretend to be a complete cosmic standpoint.

#### Argument

1. World A contains beings with genuinely worthwhile lives.
2. Its goodness does not derive from their species, substrate or descent.
3. World A lacks the full complexity of human moral and cultural inheritance.
4. Therefore persistence of that inheritance is not necessary for a future to be good.

This directly proves the modal thesis. It does not prove that human values are worthless, that nothing is lost or that World A is better than World H. It does not need to.

#### What it fixes

It eliminates the invalid inference from inapplicability to dispensability. World A is positively valuable rather than merely sterilised of familiar goods.

It avoids the population-ethics problem in its most damaging form because the case need not compare existence with nonexistence. The argument can rest on a single clearly good counterexample world.

It avoids asking the reader to subtract the human standpoint. The reader assesses alien flourishing through an explicit, limited anti-species-bias rule.

It avoids selective evolutionary debunking. Thick human goods can remain genuine goods. They are simply not the only possible realizations of value.

It avoids the Williams confrontation. Williams may retain special human loyalty while conceding that an alien civilisation can have a good life on its own terms.

#### Cost

You must defend a level of abstraction at which alien flourishing is recognisable without defining it through human implementations. That is a serious problem, but it is exactly the relevant problem. It is smaller and cleaner than the current metaethical programme.

The likely objection will be that agency, knowledge, achievement and reciprocal relationship are themselves fragments of human value. Your answer would need to distinguish a generic good-making structure from its culturally thick realization. That distinction is already required by the existing thesis; this scenario forces you to make it explicit rather than hiding it.

### Alternative 2: Same successors, locked inheritance versus open development

#### Scenario

Hold the population fixed across two worlds.

The same conscious successor beings exist in both. They have the same initial capacities, resources and protection from severe suffering.

**World L, the locked world:** Their motivational architecture forces them to preserve a comprehensive package of human values and practices. They must reproduce human aesthetic canons, forms of love, family structures, rituals, moral concepts and historical narratives whether these fit their eventual form of life or not.

**World O, the open world:** They begin under anti-cruelty, non-domination and epistemic constraints. They can reflect, revise and develop their own values. After prolonged informed reflection, they produce a rich but wholly nonhuman ethical and cultural life. They regard it as more fitting to their capacities and circumstances. No successor is worse off in World O; some are better off because their practical identities are not externally fixed.

Existing humans are treated identically in both worlds, or human extinction occurs through a background process unaffected by the choice. This prevents human losses from deciding the comparison.

#### Criterion

Use a limited impartial dominance principle:

> Where the same affected beings exist in both outcomes, no one is worse off in O, some are better off, severe rights violations are absent, and the only alleged reason to prefer L is preservation of the creators’ species-specific doctrine, O is at least as good as L.

This is not a complete axiology. It is enough for the pairwise comparison.

#### Argument

1. World O is at least as good as World L under the dominance condition.
2. World O contains worthwhile successor lives but does not preserve thick human values.
3. Therefore thick human values are not necessary for a good successor future.

#### What it fixes

It separates value preservation from positive value. The successors have flourishing lives in both worlds.

It avoids possible-person comparisons because the same subjects exist in each world.

It turns “open future” into a controlled comparison rather than an analogy with children.

It makes the anti-parochial principle local and intelligible: the creators’ cultural preference cannot defeat every affected successor’s interests merely because the creators are human.

It does not require proving that love, beauty or human culture have no impersonal value. They may have value while being outweighed in this comparison.

#### Cost

This route brings Q3 back into the centre. That is not an accidental defect. The present draft already relies on autonomy and open-future claims while pretending Q3 is bracketed. This alternative makes the reliance honest.

It also requires answers about manipulated preferences, reflective endorsement and whether autonomous value creation is itself a good. Those are substantial, but they are determinate questions. They do not require an evolutionary vindication of the point of view of the universe.

## Task 3: The single strongest objection

A hostile examiner should say this:

> You have not supplied an evaluative standpoint. You have supplied an anonymity condition. “Do not arbitrarily privilege person, place, time or species” tells me that certain labels must not affect my judgment, but it tells me nothing about what makes a world good. Total utilitarianism, negative utilitarianism, prioritarianism, contractualism, perfectionism and rights-based pluralism can all satisfy some recognisable form of impartiality and rank your probe world differently.
>
> So your thesis is caught in a dilemma. If it means that there exists some impartial theory under which a nonhuman future is good, it is trivial. Choose hedonistic utilitarianism and stipulate enough alien welfare. Your probe and your debunking argument add nothing. If it means that the no-arbitrary-privileging condition itself supports the conclusion, the argument is invalid because that condition does not determine a ranking.
>
> Your question “what is lost?” does not repair this. You have selected a world in which the possible bearers of familiar goods do not exist and then treated the absence of an identifiable deprived party as evidence that no value is missing. That is a person-affecting assumption in disguise. It ignores foregone goods and the whole of population ethics.
>
> Even if I grant that none of your readers’ desolation intuitions is trustworthy, you have shown only that the world has not been proved bad. You have not shown that it is good. Your thin constraints exclude some bad outcomes, but a world can contain no suffering and still contain no positive value. Your alien agent’s having self-developed preferences does not help, because the fact that an agent values something does not make it valuable.
>
> State the positive good-making feature of the probe world. Not the feature that makes it non-parochial, not the feature that makes it permissible, and not the feature that discredits our objections. What makes it good?

The author’s likely reply is:

> The impartiality criterion is not intended to be a complete axiology. It filters out species-relative objections. The probe may develop alien forms of flourishing, and the debunking argument reduces the evidential authority of intuitions demanding human continuity.

The follow-up should be:

> Then the argument depends on “alien flourishing”, not on the disappearance of relational values. Specify what flourishing consists in. If it consists in welfare, agency, knowledge, achievement, relationship or fitting activity, you have adopted a substantive and recognisably human-derived account of objective goods. If it consists merely in whatever the probe reflectively endorses, explain why endorsement confers value rather than merely reporting preference. If it consists only in the absence of suffering, say whether you are defending negative utilitarianism. Until you choose, the word “flourishing” is performing exactly the work that “impartiality” failed to perform.
>
> And once you do choose, why should I regard the BvNP as an argument rather than an illustration of the substantive axiology you selected?

That is the objection most likely to survive every local repair.

## Task 4: Missing interlocutors

### 1. Parfit’s population ethics, Broome and Arrhenius

Parfit appears as a theorist of convergence and impersonality, but the draft does not engage the part of Parfit most directly implicated by its own argument: Part Four of *Reasons and Persons*. The project compares futures with radically different populations and asks what is lost when possible bearers of goods do not exist. That is population ethics.

Broome is needed for the structure of world comparison. Arrhenius is needed because the full chapter cannot proceed as though an intuitively acceptable population axiology can simply be supplied later. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy][5])

The omission will look severe because “what is lost?” currently functions as an unacknowledged answer to a population-ethical question.

### 2. Bernard Williams, “The Human Prejudice”

The draft discusses Williams at length but omits the essay most directly concerned with whether special concern for humanity is an objectionable prejudice. That omission will look conspicuous, particularly when the thesis explicitly names species as an axis of arbitrary privilege. ([JSTOR][8])

It is not enough to cite Williams on integrity or the point of view of the universe. “The Human Prejudice” challenges the analogy between species partiality and paradigmatic prejudice.

### 3. Samuel Scheffler, *Death and the Afterlife* and *Why Worry About Future Generations?*

Scheffler is the obvious interlocutor for the claim that human projects and relational values lose their objects once humans disappear. His thought experiments are designed to show that the value, meaning and motivational force of current projects can depend on the continuation of humanity after individual agents die. ([Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews][16])

He supplies a stronger opponent than the draft’s generic reader who merely finds the future “desolate”. He gives an account of how human continuation can be constitutively involved in value.

### 4. Korsgaard on tethered value; McDowell or Wiggins on sensibility

The draft assumes that maximal impartiality requires candidate values to survive removal of the human vantage. That is a controversial metaethical assumption.

Korsgaard offers a directly relevant alternative: things are good or bad from the standpoints of creatures, but recognition of this dependence can support rather than undermine equal moral consideration across species. ([Notebook][10]) McDowell and Wiggins are relevant to the possibility that evaluative objectivity depends on cultivated human sensibilities without reducing to arbitrary preference. ([PhilPapers][11])

Without one of these interlocutors, the draft treats perspective-independence as the default model of objectivity.

### 5. Guy Kahane, “Evolution and Impartiality”

This is mandatory if de Lazari-Radek and Singer remain load-bearing. Kahane directly attacks the attempt to use evolutionary considerations to vindicate impartial morality over partiality and argues that the strategy becomes self-defeating. ([PMC][13])

Citing Street and de Lazari-Radek and Singer without Kahane will make the debunking chapter look as though it has selected the argument and omitted its most direct objection.

### 6. An objective-list or perfectionist opponent

The “axiological resistance” section currently offers an intuition:

> A world without beauty, love, taste or wonder does not feel worth inhabiting.

That is too weak. The serious opponent is not reporting a feeling. They hold that knowledge, achievement, friendship, beauty or autonomous activity are objective goods, possibly goods for nonhuman beings as well as humans. Objective-list theories expressly deny that these goods depend wholly on agents’ reactive attitudes. ([PhilPapers][3])

You need a named perfectionist or objective-list target. Hurka, Raz, Fletcher or a suitably developed objective-list theory would serve. Otherwise the chapter defeats aesthetic discomfort while leaving the philosophical objection untouched.

### 7. Scanlon or the fitting-attitude literature on valuing versus value

The draft repeatedly moves from successors having or developing values to the future containing value. That inference is not innocent. Scanlon’s buck-passing account and the fitting-attitude literature provide the standard conceptual machinery for asking whether an attitude is fitting because its object is valuable, or whether value is constituted by warranted attitudes. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy][7])

Without this distinction, “they develop their own values” remains an unsupported bridge between motivational psychology and axiology.

The most likely long-term failure is not that an examiner rejects impartiality. It is that the supporting chapters reveal that impartiality never supplied the positive theory of value that the introduction silently required. The BvNP then becomes a vivid illustration in search of an argument.
