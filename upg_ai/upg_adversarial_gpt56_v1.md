# Task 1: Independent demolition attempt

## 1. “Maximal impartiality” is not yet an evaluative criterion

> “The reader is asked not to occupy an impartial standpoint … but to apply a criterion.”

The posture-to-criterion move does not answer the central objection. It changes the grammar of the problem.

A constraint against “arbitrary privileging of person, place, time, or species” can exclude certain grounds for judgment. It cannot determine which remaining considerations count as reasons, how they are weighted, or how whole futures are ranked. Total utilitarianism, prioritarianism, contractualism, rights theories, perfectionism, negative utilitarianism and many forms of value pluralism can all be formulated impersonally. They produce incompatible verdicts about the probe.

Rawls is particularly unhelpful as a shortcut here. Rawls explicitly says that the result of an original-position argument depends on how the parties, their interests, the alternatives and the decision procedure are specified. Different specifications yield different principles. His device concerns principles for the basic structure of association among moral persons under circumstances of justice; it is not a general machine for discovering the value of possible worlds.

Nor does calling the operation a criterion escape Williams. Williams’s objection is not merely that human beings cannot psychologically maintain an elevated posture. It is that ethical theorising itself remains a historically situated practice, and that the supposedly external standpoint is constituted by selections made within ethical life. An evaluator must still decide which characteristics are arbitrary, which abstractions preserve reasons, and which erase them.

This produces a dilemma:

1. If the thesis means that such a future is good under **some** impartial standard, it is close to trivial. Almost any objective function can apply identically across species.
2. If it means that the future is good under **the correct** or best-justified impartial standard, the project needs a substantive axiology, not merely an impartiality constraint.
3. If it means that the future is good under **all sufficiently plausible** impartial standards, it is probably false.

The phrase “by some impartial standard” is therefore much weaker than “under maximal impartiality”. The former is existential; the latter sounds authoritative without identifying an authority.

**Severity:** Substantive; potentially fatal.

**Tractability:** Plausibly intractable in the current architecture. It is fixable only by making the criterion a substantive theory of value, a bounded family of theories, or a sharply limited symmetry principle. That is not a paragraph-level repair.

**Examiner’s sources:** Williams, *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy*; Rawls, *A Theory of Justice*; Nagel, *The View from Nowhere*. Rawls himself warns that there are indefinitely many variations on the initial situation and correspondingly different results.

## 2. The proposed inference is invalid: failure to identify a loss does not establish goodness

> “Identify what is lost in such a world, and test which candidate losses can still be stated once the human vantage point is set aside.”

Suppose every proposed human-specific loss fails this test. The conclusion would be:

> No admissible reason has yet been given for regarding the absence of thick human values as bad.

That does not entail:

> The resulting world is good.

At least four propositions are being allowed to run together:

1. Full human values are not necessary for goodness.
2. The disappearance of full human values is not itself bad.
3. Nothing valuable is lost when full human values disappear.
4. A world lacking full human values is good overall.

Only the first is required by the fixed thesis. The loss test does not establish even the second without further argument, and it plainly does not establish the fourth. A future may contain no objectionable loss of human culture yet remain neutral, empty, futile, dominated by trivial activity, or positively bad for independent reasons.

The project therefore needs a positive account of what makes the probe future good. Candidate answers include welfare, achievement, knowledge, aesthetic value, agency, complexity, relationship, continued life or preference satisfaction. But adopting any of these reintroduces substantive value theory. The thought experiment cannot get goodness merely by subtracting disqualifying losses.

There is also a decomposition problem. The method treats candidate losses as independently removable items. Moore’s principle of organic unities denies that the value of a whole must equal the sum of the values assigned separately to its components. A cultural inheritance might contribute value only in conjunction with particular lives, relationships and historical continuities. Isolating it and finding no independent value would not show that its removal leaves the whole unaffected.

At a deeper level, “the future is good” is itself contested. Geach argues that “good” is normally attributive rather than a free-standing property; Korsgaard argues that value is tethered to creatures for whom things are good or bad. A solitary probe world therefore requires an account of who or what bears the good, unless the project explicitly adopts impersonal intrinsic value.

**Severity:** Substantive.

**Tractability:** Fixable with a chapter if a positive axiology and a comparison class are supplied. Plausibly intractable if the intended method remains exclusively a search for losses.

**Examiner’s sources:** G. E. Moore on isolation and organic unities; Peter Geach on attributive goodness; Christine Korsgaard on tethered value; Parfit on comparing possible populations and futures.

## 3. The solitary probe rigs the test by destroying the conditions under which the disputed values operate

> “A solitary, self-repairing AI on a millennia-long interstellar voyage, chosen to maximize inferential distance from human forms of life.”

This is not merely a distant case. It is an evaluatively desertified case.

The scenario removes or radically attenuates:

* interpersonal dependence;
* descendants and intergenerational projects;
* political association;
* shared historical memory;
* cooperation and conflict;
* recognition by others;
* cultural transmission;
* vulnerability to other agents;
* membership in traditions;
* many forms of embodiment and finitude.

Many thick values are not free-floating objects that remain available after these structures disappear. They are values of relationships, practices and temporally extended forms of life. Eliminating their objects and enabling conditions does not show that the values were parochial. It shows that the scenario has made them uninstantiable.

The suppressed inference is:

> Where a value has no possible object in environment E, its absence in E is not an axiological loss.

That premise is false without major qualification. Destroying the conditions under which music, friendship or scientific inquiry could exist would not establish that nothing was lost because there were subsequently no musicians, friends or investigators present to register the loss.

Scheffler is directly relevant. He argues that human valuing has conservative, non-experiential and non-consequential dimensions; we care that valued things are preserved, and confidence in a collective future helps make present projects matter. The probe scenario cannot simply classify that concern as a perspective-bound residue without arguing against Scheffler’s account of value.

The solitary case can test a genuine but much narrower proposition:

> Can a radically isolated artificial agent’s existence instantiate any value despite lacking human social forms?

It cannot by itself establish:

> Human social and cultural forms are unnecessary to the goodness of successor futures generally.

The former concerns value in minimal environments. The latter concerns substitutability between forms of flourishing.

**Severity:** Substantive. It becomes embarrassing if the absence of objects is explicitly presented as evidence against the value of what formerly related to those objects.

**Tractability:** The solitary BvNP is plausibly intractable as the principal argumentative vehicle. It can remain as a limiting case or application, but the principal proof needs matched comparison worlds or a transition case.

**Examiner’s sources:** Samuel Scheffler, *Death and the Afterlife*; Joseph Raz on socially sustained valuable forms of life; Williams on projects and ethical identity.

## 4. The evolutionary debunking strategy threatens the values the project needs more directly than it threatens many thick values

This issue is already recognised, but its severity is being underestimated. It is not merely an “attackable joint”. It may be structurally incompatible with the proposed conclusion.

Street’s target is not a convenient subset comprising patriotism, filial attachment, human aesthetic judgment and other thick commitments. Her Darwinian dilemma concerns realist evaluative judgments generally. She explicitly extends the pressure to realist accounts of the disvalue of pain.

That is damaging because “non-production of suffering” is supposed to survive as a thin constraint. Our aversion to pain and suffering is among the evaluative dispositions with the most obvious evolutionary history. The project cannot say:

> Evolutionary explanation undermines the authority of thick human values, but suffering remains bad because it strongly appears bad to us.

That would preserve precisely the inferential route being used to debunk the other appearances.

The de Lazari-Radek and Singer response attempts to distinguish impartial benevolence from evolutionarily explicable partiality. But Kahane argues that this use of evolutionary debunking is self-defeating: the premises and reasoning through which impartiality is vindicated are not insulated merely because the final principle is abstract or motivationally difficult.

The project requires an epistemic asymmetry of the following form:

* Thick-value intuitions are explained by fitness-producing genealogy, so their apparent authority is reduced.
* Impartiality and suffering-aversion are connected to normative truth by some independent method, so their authority survives.

Until that independent method is supplied, the selectivity is circular. If it is supplied, the central work is being done by that method rather than by debunking.

There is a further metaethical fork:

* Under robust realism, the project inherits Street’s tracking problem.
* Under constructivism or antirealism, impartiality must be grounded in some evaluative standpoint or procedure. It then becomes difficult to present it as a vantage-independent test capable of disqualifying the thick values from which actual human practical reasoning proceeds.

**Severity:** Substantive; potentially fatal.

**Tractability:** Plausibly intractable if evolutionary debunking is supposed both to remove thick values and positively vindicate the thin set. It may be fixable by reducing the debunking argument to a limited defeater of specific forms of species bias, rather than using it as a general sorting mechanism.

**Examiner’s sources:** Sharon Street, “A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value”; de Lazari-Radek and Singer, *The Point of View of the Universe*; Guy Kahane, “Evolution and Impartiality”; Brad Hooker’s criticisms of their debunking argument.

## 5. The thin set is neither clearly thin nor clearly coherent

> “Thin open-future side-constraints in Feinberg’s sense: non-production of suffering, resource satiability, and similar.”

“Thin” currently appears to mean several different things:

* capable of short formulation;
* not specific to human culture;
* compatible with many conceptions of the good;
* procedural rather than substantive;
* not exhaustively action-guiding;
* intended only as a side-constraint.

These properties do not coincide.

“Do not produce suffering” is extremely substantive if lexical or exceptionless. It could prohibit creating any vulnerable conscious life, undertaking risky projects, allowing transformative autonomy, or preserving systems in which suffering is an unavoidable condition of other goods. If it is non-lexical, the thesis needs a theory of permissible trade-offs.

“Resource satiability” is not obviously a value at all. It is a structural property of goals or agents. A perfectly satiable process could be pointless, oppressive or destructive up to its satiation point.

“Open future” imports still more content. Feinberg’s argument concerns rights held in trust for children who are expected to become autonomous adults. It presupposes a future rights-holder, an account of autonomy, and a distinction between options worth preserving and options that may permissibly be closed. It is not a free-standing principle against giving created agents determinate values.

Applied to a successor AI, the analogy forces unanswered questions:

* Whose future is being kept open?
* Does the future agent have a claim against its creators before it exists?
* Which possibilities count as options for that agent?
* Why is openness valuable if the resulting agent does not value autonomy?
* How much initial structure is necessary for coherent agency?
* Does preserving an “open future” for the successor conflict with preserving an open future for other beings?

The thin set therefore faces two horns:

1. **Genuinely thin:** It fails to exclude painless but worthless, monotonous or systematically rights-violating futures.
2. **Normatively adequate:** It contains a recognisably liberal, welfarist and autonomy-centred doctrine, and is no longer neutral between thick conceptions.

The second horn is not necessarily objectionable. The objection is to claiming that the content is thin and therefore escapes the justificatory burden imposed on human values.

**Severity:** Substantive.

**Tractability:** Fixable with a chapter that replaces “thin/thick” with several explicit axes and separately justifies each proposed constraint. Intractable if thinness is expected to supply neutrality merely by nomenclature.

**Examiner’s sources:** Feinberg on rights in trust; Raz on autonomy and valuable options; Korsgaard on value being relative to the forms of life and good of creatures.

## 6. The modal thesis is unstable between a modest existence claim and a much stronger displacement claim

> “Thick human values … need not persist for a future to count as good.”

Read literally, this is a modal existence claim:

> There is at least one possible good future in which the full complexity of human values does not persist.

That proposition is relatively easy to defend. A rich alien civilisation, or a nonhuman successor civilisation containing welfare, relationships, knowledge, creativity and its own sophisticated values, could provide the witness. Nothing about the claim requires a solitary probe, maximal impartiality or evolutionary debunking.

The project appears to want something stronger:

> Once arbitrary human privilege is removed, the preservation of thick human value is not among the requirements of a good future.

That is a claim about the grounds or constitution of value. It requires showing that human cultural inheritance has no necessary, non-substitutable contribution to goodness. The BvNP does not establish that.

There is a third possible thesis:

> A successor future may compensate for the loss of human values through different goods.

This is more defensible and more interesting. But it requires comparison, substitution and aggregation. It concedes that something may be lost while denying that the loss makes the future bad overall.

The introduction must choose among these. At present, the relatively defensible existential thesis appears to borrow force and novelty from the much stronger thesis, while retreat remaining available to the weaker formulation.

**Severity:** Embarrassing at the level of thesis formulation; substantive once the argumentative burden is considered.

**Tractability:** The quantifier ambiguity is fixable with a paragraph. Defending any non-trivial strengthening requires a chapter.

**Known move:** Standard distinctions between intrinsic value, necessary conditions, contributory value and all-things-considered comparison; Moore and contemporary population axiology are the obvious frameworks. Parfit’s work shows how rapidly apparently simple comparisons between possible futures become dependent on disputed population-ethical assumptions.

# Task 2: Alternative routes with the same thesis shape

## Alternative A: The matched-successor-worlds argument

Replace the solitary probe as the principal test with two richly specified worlds.

### World H

A flourishing human-descended civilisation retains recognisably human moral, aesthetic and cultural traditions. It contains positive welfare, relationships, knowledge, achievement, autonomy, creativity, diversity and low suffering.

### World N

A flourishing nonhuman civilisation has no semantic, historical or genealogical continuity with thick human values. Its forms of relationship, creation, inquiry and practical reasoning are genuinely alien. Nevertheless, stipulate parity between H and N on:

* numbers and kinds of conscious lives;
* welfare and suffering;
* agency and autonomy;
* richness of relationships;
* knowledge and achievement;
* diversity and complexity;
* stability and option value;
* external effects on other beings.

The central question becomes:

> Is H better than N solely because the valuable forms instantiated in H are ours?

The criterion should be narrow: **invariance under species and genealogical relabelling**, not “maximal impartiality”. Where two futures are matched on independently defended value-bearing dimensions, merely replacing “human-descended” with “nonhuman” cannot lower value.

This does not prove that nothing is lost. It supports the exact thesis actually needed:

> Full human cultural and moral continuity is not a necessary condition for a future to be good.

### What this fixes

It fixes three defects that cannot be patched within the solitary-probe structure:

1. It supplies positive goods rather than inferring goodness from the absence of surviving objections.
2. It does not remove the objects of relational, cultural and historical values before assessing them.
3. It isolates human specificity as the variable instead of changing nearly every value-relevant property at once.

Its main weakness is that “parity” will be disputed. That is preferable to the current hidden dispute. The chapter can explicitly test which dimensions an opponent denies are commensurable or substitutable.

This is the strongest route available for the fixed thesis.

## Alternative B: The gradual successor spectrum

Construct a sequence of futures beginning with a recognisably good human civilisation.

At each stage, make a limited transformation:

1. biological to partly synthetic embodiment;
2. ordinary lifespans to extended lifespans;
3. individual memory to partly external or distributed memory;
4. human reproduction to designed successor creation;
5. human affective profiles to novel but welfare-supporting profiles;
6. inherited cultural concepts to successor concepts;
7. human moral practices to alien but stable forms of mutual recognition;
8. eventual disappearance of specifically human thick values.

At every transition, preserve or compensate for the relevant goods: welfare, agency, relationship, knowledge, achievement, diversity and low suffering. The endpoint contains none of the full human inheritance but does contain its own rich evaluative life.

The argumentative challenge to the opponent is:

> At which transition does the future cease to be good, and what non-arbitrary property changes there?

This resembles a Parfitian spectrum argument. It does not claim that every tiny change is value-neutral merely because it is tiny. It claims that an opponent who locates a discontinuity must identify the morally relevant threshold, while an opponent who claims cumulative decline must identify the uncompensated loss.

### What this fixes

It replaces the unexplained leap from human civilisation to isolated probe with a continuity argument. It also distinguishes:

* persistence of the same values;
* continuity of value-bearing capacities;
* replacement by different goods;
* preservation of personal or cultural identity.

Its principal vulnerability is Sorites reasoning. Small permissible or value-neutral steps can aggregate into a large loss. The chapter would therefore need explicit bookkeeping of cumulative losses and gains rather than relying on continuity alone.

The BvNP can remain as the endpoint of one deliberately austere branch of the spectrum. It should not carry the principal inference.

# Task 3: The single strongest objection

> You have not given me an impartial criterion. You have instructed me to delete considerations until only the considerations you already favour remain. “Do not privilege species” tells me not to count the same interest differently merely because its bearer is human. It does not tell me whether cultural continuity, loyalty, beauty, achievement, autonomy, pleasure, suffering or existence itself are interests or values, nor how to compare them. Those are the disputed questions.
>
> Your test then asks what can be “stated” after the human vantage is removed. But the ability to restate a value in impersonal vocabulary is not evidence of its authority. Some genuinely agent-relative reasons can be recognised by an impartial evaluator; conversely, a monstrous principle can be formulated in perfectly universal language. Your procedure confuses universalisability of formulation with correctness of content.
>
> The probe makes the circularity harder to notice because you have removed most of the circumstances in which thick values have application. You then retain suffering and option value as thin constraints, although these are substantive commitments with their own human genealogy. The later debunking argument is supposed to explain why the discarded values were distortions and the retained values were not. Yet the distinction between distortion and insight is precisely what the criterion was meant to establish.
>
> So state the argument without the rhetoric of perspectives. Give me two fully specified possible worlds and a rule that ranks them. Identify every substantive evaluative premise in that rule. Then explain why the premises supporting suffering, autonomy or openness survive the genealogy that defeats historical continuity, loyalty, art and human flourishing.

The author’s most likely reply is that impartiality is only a negative constraint, while the positive thin values will be defended independently.

The follow-up is:

> Then the thesis is not being established by the thought experiment or by maximal impartiality. It is being established by an independently defended welfarist or liberal axiology. Why should I regard the probe as evidence rather than as an illustration selected because your prior axiology already gives the desired verdict? And if your positive theory can do the argumentative work, what indispensable philosophical work remains for the evolutionary debunking chapter?

# Task 4: Missing interlocutors

## 1. Guy Kahane and Brad Hooker on evolutionary impartiality

These are mandatory. It is not enough to cite Street and the de Lazari-Radek/Singer response. Kahane directly argues that their attempt to use evolution to debunk partiality while vindicating impartial benevolence is self-defeating. Hooker attacks their debunking of egoism and common-sense morality. An examiner familiar with the debate will ask why the chapter begins from a contested move without addressing its most direct critics.

## 2. G. E. Moore on isolation and organic unities

The project uses an isolated-world thought experiment and asks what retains value when considered apart from ordinary causal and social contexts. That is recognisably Moorean terrain. Moore is also the obvious source for the objection that the value of a world cannot be obtained by separately testing and summing candidate goods and losses.

## 3. Geach, Thomson or Korsgaard on whether there is goodness simpliciter

At least one serious opponent of impersonal world-goodness is required. Geach attacks predicative “good”; Thomson analyses the different relations hidden by claims that something is simply good; Korsgaard argues that value is tethered to creatures. Without this engagement, “a future counts as good” conceals a major meta-axiological assumption.

## 4. Samuel Scheffler, *Death and the Afterlife*

Scheffler is not optional here. He gives a sustained account of why the continuation of humanity and its practices matters in ways not reducible to the experiences of presently existing individuals. That is one of the strongest direct challenges to the claim that human continuity can be removed as a merely partial concern.

## 5. Parfit on future generations, non-identity and population comparison

A claim about whether one radically different possible future “counts as good” touches population axiology even if the chapter tries to avoid policy or permissibility. The number and identity of future bearers, whether creating good lives benefits anyone, and whether impersonal betterness is coherent cannot simply be bracketed as Q1, Q3 or Q4.

## 6. Joseph Raz on social forms, valuable options and autonomy

Raz is load-bearing for two reasons. First, he argues that autonomy requires an adequate range of valuable options, not mere formal openness. Second, valuable forms of life are socially sustained. This directly challenges both the thin “open future” proposal and the probe’s elimination of social conditions.

## 7. Amartya Sen on open impartiality

If the project wants an impartiality model without an imagined view from nowhere, Sen is closer than Rawls. His “open impartiality” expands the informational and critical perspectives entering judgment rather than stripping all situated viewpoints away. Engagement would force a useful choice: whether impartiality means abstraction from perspectives or criticism through a plurality of perspectives. Those are different procedures and may produce opposite treatment of thick values.

## Bottom line

The current route has three coupled defects:

1. impartiality supplies no world-ranking rule;
2. the probe removes the goods whose necessity is being tested;
3. the debunking strategy lacks a non-circular basis for preserving the thin values needed to call the resulting future good.

These are not independent patch points. Solving the first by adopting substantive axiology intensifies the selectivity problem. Solving the second by enriching the scenario makes the thick values harder to dismiss. Solving the third by independently defending suffering and autonomy reveals that the thought experiment was not doing the principal argumentative work.

The cleanest repair is not to abandon the thesis. It is to narrow the criterion to species-invariant treatment, replace the solitary probe with matched rich futures, and argue only that thick human inheritance is not a necessary condition of goodness. The probe can then function as a severe secondary test rather than as the source of the conclusion.
