# Task 1: Independent demolition attempt

## 1. The impartiality criterion is not an axiology

> “No arbitrary privileging of person, place, time, or species.”

This is a formal symmetry condition. It says that certain identities or locations cannot, merely as such, determine moral weight. It does not tell us:

* what things are good;
* which entities have a good;
* whether goodness is exhausted by welfare;
* whether friendship, knowledge, beauty, achievement, autonomy or culture are objective goods;
* how goods should be aggregated;
* whether creating additional valuable lives improves a world;
* whether goods can be incomparable;
* what makes a difference “arbitrary”.

The last problem is decisive. “Arbitrary” means something like “unsupported by a morally relevant reason”. But which differences are morally relevant is precisely what the substantive axiology must settle. A perfectionist can say that rational achievement matters for reasons independent of species. A Moorean can say that beauty is impersonally valuable. A contractualist can say that what matters is justifiability to each individual, not maximizing an impersonal total. All can reject species privilege while rejecting your probe-world.

The draft repeatedly treats impartiality as if it filters the content of value:

> “My expectation is that fewer such losses survive this test than is commonly assumed.”

Nothing in impartiality predicts that result. An impartial theory could give equal, agent-neutral weight to the loss of friendship, art or understanding wherever those goods might otherwise have existed. Species-neutrality attacks the claim that human goods matter because they are human. It does not attack the claim that some goods exemplified in human life matter because of their non-species-indexed properties.

Equality is particularly unable to settle aggregation or the content of value. The premise that “each counts for one” does not by itself entail that more individuals count for more, or any particular method of comparing outcomes. ([PhilPapers][1])

**Severity:** Substantive; potentially fatal to the advertised argumentative route.

**Tractability:** Plausibly intractable without replacing the criterion with at least a partial substantive axiology. A paragraph cannot fix it. The fixed thesis could survive, but the current derivation from impartiality cannot.

**Examiner’s sources:** Nagel, *The View from Nowhere* and *Equality and Partiality*; Scanlon, *What We Owe to Each Other*; Taurek, “Should the Numbers Count?”; Muñoz, “Each Counts for One”.

## 2. The argument only removes objections; it never establishes goodness

The central interrogation is:

> “In such a universe, what exactly is lost?”

And later:

> “The follow-up question is whether the desolation survives the removal of the human vantage point.”

Suppose this procedure works perfectly. Suppose every intuition that something has been lost is either silenced or evolutionarily debunked. The conclusion would be:

1. we lack undefeated evidence that the world is bad; or
2. we lack undefeated evidence that the absence of human values makes it worse.

Neither entails:

3. the world is good.

The draft slides among at least four claims:

* thick human values are not necessary for goodness;
* their absence is not itself bad;
* nothing valuable is lost;
* the resulting world is positively good.

These are not equivalent. A sterile rock may contain no suffering and violate no rights, but this does not make it a good future. A single non-conscious probe may be harmless, but harmlessness is not flourishing. A universe may be better than a paperclip catastrophe while remaining axiologically negligible.

The selective debunking strategy does not repair this. Evolutionary debunking arguments are epistemic arguments: they attempt to undermine the justification of evaluative beliefs by explaining their origins. Kahane explicitly characterizes them in that way and argues that they carry difficult metaethical presuppositions. ([Wiley Online Library][2]) Street’s argument is likewise a challenge concerning the relation between evolutionary influence, evaluative attitudes and realist value truths. ([PhilPapers][3]) Even a perfectly selective debunking argument would not establish the contrary evaluative proposition.

This is distinct from the selectivity problem already recognized in Part B. Part B assumes that, if thick-value intuitions can be debunked while impartiality and suffering survive, the architecture is substantially intact. It is not. Successful debunking leaves an evidential vacancy. You still need a positive theory explaining what makes the successor world good.

The fallback phrase that the AIs “develop their own values” does no axiological work. A paperclip maximizer has values. So does an agent that values suffering. Having terminal preferences is not the same as instantiating value.

**Severity:** Embarrassing if left implicit; substantive once exposed.

**Tractability:** Plausibly intractable for the current negative route. Fixable only by adding a positive axiology or substantially weakening the conclusion to “not shown to be bad”.

**Examiner’s sources:** Kahane, “Evolutionary Debunking Arguments”; Street, “A Darwinian Dilemma”; G. E. Moore, *Principia Ethica*; Korsgaard, “Two Distinctions in Goodness”. Korsgaard’s distinctions are relevant because the draft moves without warning among final value, intrinsic value, instrumental architecture and constitutional constraints. ([JSTOR][4])

## 3. The thesis lacks a determinate bearer or locus of value

The draft says:

> “Nor do I address questions of whether they are conscious or sentient.”

But the criterion is introduced as:

> “No one individual’s good matters more … than any other’s.”

Whose good exists in the focal world?

There are three possible answers, none currently taken.

### A. Goodness is welfare-based

Then the probe must be capable of welfare. Consciousness, sentience, preference satisfaction, rational agency or some other welfare-grounding property can no longer be bracketed. “Non-production of suffering” is literally inapplicable to entities incapable of suffering. The right to an open future is also difficult to formulate unless there is a persisting subject whose future can go better or worse for it.

### B. Goodness is impersonal

Then the project needs an account of impersonal goods: knowledge, beauty, complexity, achievement, agency, ecological organization, or something else. But this abandons the idea that the thin constraints themselves establish goodness. It also exposes the project to Moorean arguments that valuable states can exist even without someone for whom they are valuable. Moore’s isolation method was designed precisely to compare worlds in abstraction from their instrumental effects and from observers. ([Project Gutenberg][5])

### C. The AI’s own endorsement constitutes value

Then the view is some form of response-dependence or desire theory. It needs constraints against defective, manipulated, adaptive or malevolent preferences. Without them, any coherent optimizer manufactures its own vindication.

The text currently oscillates among all three. Suffering is treated as welfare-based disvalue. Autonomy is treated as a value of agents. Art and beauty are treated as possible impersonal losses. The AI’s own value formation is treated as conferring legitimacy. These are distinct theories of value.

The problem is not merely whether the warm and cold framings stipulate experiencelessness. Even if both stipulate consciousness, the chapter must say whether the future is good for the probes, good because of what the probes do, or simply good as a state of the universe.

Philosophers disagree sharply over whether goodness must be good-for someone, whether welfare consists in a subject’s point of view, and whether there is reason-giving “absolute goodness”. Darwall ties welfare to rational care; Kraut rejects absolute goodness as a basic reason-giving category. ([PhilPapers][6]) The draft cannot remain neutral among these positions while using each opportunistically.

**Severity:** Substantive.

**Tractability:** Fixable with a chapter and explicit stipulations. Plausibly intractable if consciousness and moral patienthood must remain bracketed.

**Examiner’s sources:** Darwall, *Welfare and Rational Care*; Kraut, *Against Absolute Goodness*; Korsgaard, “Two Distinctions in Goodness”; Moore, *Principia Ethica*.

## 4. “Stating a loss without the human vantage” is not a well-formed evaluative test

The operative question is:

> “Why that loss is a loss from any point of view but the reader’s own.”

This equivocates among three very different requirements:

1. **The value is not grounded in the beneficiary’s identity as human.**
2. **The value would be recognized or endorsed by non-human evaluators.**
3. **The value can be described without using concepts inherited from human life.**

Only the first follows naturally from anti-species partiality. The second is a response-dependence claim. The third may be impossible.

An agent-neutral reason is not a reason that must be endorsed “from every point of view”. It is a reason whose formulation does not contain an essential reference to the agent whose reason it is. A sadist’s failure to recognize suffering as bad does not make suffering’s badness merely the victim’s perspective. Correspondingly, an alien’s inability to recognize friendship or knowledge as valuable would not show that those values are human-indexed.

The test also faces a dilemma:

* If “stated without the human vantage” means stated in literally view-independent evaluative language, no candidate value will pass. Human beings supply every predicate used in the chapter, including suffering, autonomy, impartiality, satiability and arbitrariness.
* If it means stated in sufficiently abstract language, almost every thick value can be redescribed: love as non-instrumental reciprocal concern; art as skilled creation and appreciation; fellowship as mutually recognized cooperative attachment; knowledge as accurate representation and understanding.

The abstraction procedure therefore either eliminates everything, including the thin set, or permits the allegedly thick values to return in functionalized form.

This is where the “criterion, not posture” move fails to escape Williams. It answers a psychological objection: readers need not imaginatively become a disembodied universal spectator. It does not answer the normative objection: why should their projects, attachments and concepts submit to the authority of this externalized test? Calling the standpoint a rule rather than an experience leaves its authority untouched. Susan Wolf similarly challenges the idea that morality is constructed simply by abstracting from the personal viewpoint and granting every such viewpoint equal standing. ([PhilPapers][7])

**Severity:** Substantive.

**Tractability:** Plausibly intractable as presently formulated. The project can use a more precise anti-discrimination or agent-neutrality condition, but the “which losses survive removal of the vantage” test should not carry argumentative weight.

**Examiner’s sources:** Williams, *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy* and “Persons, Character and Morality”; Nagel, *The View from Nowhere*; Wolf, “Morality and the View from Here”; Korsgaard, *The Sources of Normativity*.

## 5. Environmental inapplicability does not entail axiological dispensability

The crucial bridge is:

> “The relational values central to human life may simply lack objects there.”

That may establish:

> In a solitary-probe environment, certain relational values cannot be instantiated.

It does not establish:

> Their absence is no loss.

Nor does it establish:

> A universe systematically lacking the conditions for those goods can nevertheless be good.

The inference requires a suppressed premise such as:

> If a form of value cannot be instantiated by the agents who actually exist in an environment, its non-instantiation does not detract from the value of that environment.

That premise is highly controversial. It resembles an actualist or person-affecting restriction: only goods available to actual entities matter. An impersonal axiology rejects it. The absence of friendship in a world containing only solitary beings might make the world worse even though there is no actual individual for whom friendship was feasible. The loss is comparative: a different possible world could contain those goods.

The scaling step compounds the problem:

> “If the reader will allow the possibility of a single such probe, I would like to stretch that possibility to propose a universe largely populated with similar entities.”

That is not stretching one possibility. It changes the axiological object. There is a major difference between:

* a single solitary probe existing as one valuable project within an otherwise diverse universe; and
* a universe whose architecture systematically prevents relational goods.

The solitary case is favourable because it removes victims, conflicts, frustrated attachments and opportunity costs from view. The universalized case introduces an enormous opportunity cost: the exclusion of every form of social, aesthetic, ecological or experiential good that requires richer conditions. The local permissibility of an isolated life does not establish the global adequacy of a monoculture of isolated lives.

Moore’s isolation method cuts directly against the proposed inference. Its purpose is to ask how worlds differ in value when candidate goods are present or absent, not merely whether the actual inhabitants complain about the absence. ([Project Gutenberg][5]) Parfit’s non-identity and future-person cases likewise show why “worse for no actual person” does not straightforwardly entail “not worse”. ([PhilPapers][8])

**Severity:** Substantive.

**Tractability:** Fixable with a chapter if you supply and defend the bridge premise. Plausibly intractable if the project wants to remain neutral between person-affecting and impersonal value theories.

**Examiner’s sources:** Parfit, *Reasons and Persons*, Part IV; Moore, *Principia Ethica*; Broome, *Weighing Lives*.

## 6. The canonical thesis and the conclusion actually drawn have different modal force

The official thesis is:

> “Thick human values … need not survive for a future to count as good.”

This is a weak existential claim:

> There is at least one possible good future in which the full package of human values does not persist.

The later claim is:

> “Thick human values are unsuitable as a value endowment.”

And then:

> “A much smaller … thin set of values might be better as a starting point.”

Those are much stronger claims:

* thick values are generally inappropriate;
* thin constraints are comparatively preferable;
* humans ought not to install a comprehensive value package;
* open-ended successor self-determination has greater legitimacy or expected value.

The focal thought experiment could, at most, supply a modal counterexample to necessity. It cannot establish general unsuitability or comparative superiority. Even if one solitary probe can live well without human culture, human values might remain an excellent endowment for Earth-based, social, conscious or multipolar successors.

This is not merely imprecise wording. The weak thesis is compatible with:

* preserving nearly all human values in almost every realistic successor;
* using thick human values as the best available training target;
* regarding the exceptional probe case as a negligible edge case.

The stronger recommendations are not.

**Severity:** Embarrassing because it is a straightforward modal and dialectical overreach.

**Tractability:** Fixable with a paragraph if the stronger claims are deleted. Fixable only with a separate chapter if they are retained.

**Examiner’s move:** Distinguish “not necessary”, “not always appropriate”, “generally unsuitable”, “impermissible to impose”, and “inferior to thin constraints”. Ask which proposition the dissertation defends.

## 7. “Thin values” are not a coherent category

The thin set appears to contain:

* non-production of suffering;
* resource satiability;
* non-arbitrary impartiality;
* autonomy;
* preservation of an open future;
* perhaps non-domination, option value and constitutional revisability.

These are not thinner instances of the same kind of thing as love, sorrow, art and fellowship. They occupy different logical categories:

| Item                        | Category                                                                  |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Non-production of suffering | Axiological commitment, prohibition or lexical side-constraint            |
| Resource satiability        | Disposition, utility-function property or architecture                    |
| Impartiality                | Second-order constraint on reasons or distributions                       |
| Open future                 | Right, procedural norm or intergenerational principle                     |
| Autonomy                    | Good, status, capacity or deontological constraint                        |
| Love, art, fellowship       | Activities, relationships, practices, experiences or forms of flourishing |

Calling the first group “thin” makes it sound modest and minimally controversial. It is neither. “Non-production of suffering” could mean:

* do not intentionally create suffering;
* minimize expected suffering;
* give suffering lexical priority;
* forbid any positive project that risks suffering;
* minimize suffering subject to a threshold;
* prohibit uncompensated suffering.

Those yield radically different futures. Resource satiability similarly requires a threshold, a conception of legitimate needs and rules for conflicts. Open-ended autonomy can conflict directly with suffering prevention, because autonomous successors may choose risky, cruel or expansionary projects.

There is also a terminological collision. In mainstream metaethics, “thick” commonly refers to concepts combining substantial descriptive and evaluative content, such as courageous, cruel or treacherous. It does not ordinarily mean “the complete complexity of a culture’s values”. Williams’s discussion of thick concepts belongs to precisely the humanistic critique of abstract ethical theory that this dissertation invokes elsewhere. ([PhilPapers][9])

You can stipulate a project-specific meaning, but then “thin” cannot silently inherit the philosophical associations of formal neutrality or minimal commitment. The set should be decomposed into:

1. axiological minima;
2. rights or side-constraints;
3. constitutional procedures;
4. corrigibility and option-preserving properties;
5. resource and motivational architecture.

**Severity:** Embarrassing at the level of classification; substantive in its downstream consequences.

**Tractability:** Fixable with a chapter. Not fixable merely by defining “thin” more carefully, because the items also conflict and require priority rules.

**Examiner’s sources:** Williams, *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy*; McDowell on thick ethical concepts; Feinberg on the open future; Korsgaard on distinctions among kinds of goodness. ([JSTOR][4])

## 8. Cosmic evaluation without population ethics is radically incomplete

The focal objects include:

* a single probe;
* millions of probes;
* planets run by solitary AIs;
* Dyson-scale systems;
* a universe “largely populated” by such entities.

These alternatives differ in number, identity, duration, welfare, productive capacity and unrealized possible populations. No-arbitrary-privileging does not determine how to compare them.

At minimum, the argument needs positions on:

* total versus average value;
* whether creating a good life makes an outcome better;
* the neutral level for existence;
* whether possible but never-created subjects count;
* how to compare one exceptional life with vast numbers of modest lives;
* whether distribution matters independently of totals;
* whether non-welfare goods aggregate;
* what happens if the reachable universe or future duration is effectively unbounded.

Population axiology exists because superficially attractive impartial principles generate incompatible rankings. Broome analyzes distributions of well-being across persons, time and states of nature; Arrhenius derives impossibility results for attempts to order populations while satisfying apparently reasonable conditions. ([PhilPapers][10])

This matters even if the thesis is not comparative. To say that a future “counts as good” requires a baseline or criterion of positive value. Is one contented probe enough? Does the opportunity cost of preventing quadrillions of other worthwhile lives matter? Can a world be good while vastly worse than an available alternative? Is “good” scalar, thresholded or merely non-negative?

The probe case currently benefits from avoiding these questions while the cosmic extrapolation presupposes answers to them.

**Severity:** Substantive.

**Tractability:** Fixable only by a chapter or a severe restriction of scope. You need not solve population ethics, but you must state which comparisons Q2 does and does not make and show that the thesis is robust across a defensible range of population axiologies.

**Examiner’s sources:** Parfit, *Reasons and Persons*, Part IV; Broome, *Weighing Lives*; Arrhenius on population-axiology impossibility theorems. ([PhilPapers][8])

## 9. The genealogy of impartiality disguises disagreement as convergence

The draft groups Bentham, Sidgwick, Rawls, Parfit, Singer and de Lazari-Radek into a lineage of increasingly generalized impartiality. This is too coarse to support the criterion.

Rawls’s original position is not a device for ranking complete cosmic histories. It selects principles of justice for social cooperation under specific assumptions about persons, primary goods, institutions and reasonable pluralism. Scanlonian contractualism treats individual complaints and justifiability as fundamental and is characteristically resistant to simply aggregating benefits. Sidgwickian utilitarianism, Rawlsian justice and contractualism may all reject some forms of arbitrary partiality, but they do not thereby produce the same axiology.

The claim:

> “Consequentialist, deontological and contractualist accounts of morality were all targeting the intuition of impersonality”

is too weak to establish convergence and too strong as intellectual history. Shared opposition to arbitrary discrimination does not imply agreement about value, aggregation, personal reasons or the good.

The danger is lineage laundering: the criterion acquires apparent authority by being presented as the common core of major traditions, when the common core is too abstract to yield any conclusion in the probe case.

**Severity:** Substantive, though not independently fatal.

**Tractability:** Fixable with a chapter. The traditions should be treated as rival specifications of impartial concern, not successive articulations of one criterion.

**Examiner’s sources:** Rawls, *A Theory of Justice* and *Political Liberalism*; Scanlon, *What We Owe to Each Other*; Nagel, *Equality and Partiality*; Parfit, *On What Matters*.

## 10. The warm/cold pair is not a “control” unless it becomes an experiment

Strict stipulative parity is necessary but insufficient.

Literary register changes salience, implied causal structure, perceived agency, narrative distance and interpretation. Those are not external contaminants that can be held separate from the scenario’s presentation; they are how readers identify what the scenario contains. Two passages can contain the same explicit propositions while pragmatically communicating different worlds.

Without:

* multiple participants;
* random assignment;
* a defined dependent measure;
* manipulation checks;
* criteria for semantic equivalence;
* analysis of individual variation;

the pair cannot identify a framing effect in anything resembling the experimental sense. At most it is a rhetorical robustness check: “Does my judgment remain stable across two presentations?”

That can still be philosophically useful. Calling it a control invites an examiner to demand the methodological apparatus of experimental philosophy.

**Severity:** Embarrassing if “control” is retained literally; otherwise minor.

**Tractability:** Fixable with a paragraph by describing it as a contrastive presentation or robustness test. Alternatively, turn it into an actual small study.

**Examiner’s source:** Standard experimental-philosophy methodology and the literature on pragmatic enrichment of thought experiments.

# Task 2: Alternative routes with the same thesis shape

## Alternative 1: The matched-world counterexample

Construct two complete worlds rather than one isolated probe.

### World H

A future containing flourishing human or human-descended beings. It includes friendship, artistic traditions, historical memory, human forms of love, recognizable moral concepts and the rest of the thick human inheritance.

### World X

A future containing conscious non-human successors. They have:

* lives positively good for them;
* durable projects and achievements;
* accurate understanding of their environment;
* reciprocal recognition or another non-exploitative social structure;
* creative activity;
* freedom from severe suffering and domination;
* stable but revisable conceptions of what matters.

Their projects, aesthetic categories, relationships and self-understandings are not continuous with ours. They preserve none of our particular religions, stories, artistic canons, kinship structures, moral vocabularies or characteristic emotional repertoires.

Hold fixed every feature that your eventual axiology regards as value-making. Vary only:

* biological lineage;
* cultural continuity;
* phenomenological and conceptual form;
* the particular objects of concern.

Then ask whether World X fails to be good merely because its valuable activities are not human-shaped.

The argument would be:

1. World X contains positive value-makers.
2. Those value-makers do not derive their value from being human.
3. No morally relevant deficit has been identified merely by noting discontinuity with human culture.
4. Therefore, complete persistence of thick human values is not necessary for a good future.

This directly proves the weak existential thesis. It does not try to infer goodness from an absence of objections.

### What this fixes

It fixes three things that the current route cannot repair locally:

1. **Positive value:** World X is stipulated to instantiate identified goods, not merely avoid suffering.
2. **Bearer of value:** The successors are explicitly welfare-bearing or otherwise value-instantiating.
3. **Suppressed bridge:** The argument does not infer that unavailable goods are dispensable. It shows that alternative goods can make a world good.

It also removes the dependence on solitude, low stakes and resource satiability. The thought experiment remains robust when populated by many agents.

### Cost

This route establishes that **human-specific thick values** are unnecessary. It does not establish that **all thick values** are unnecessary. World X has its own thick, structured forms of flourishing.

That is not a defect relative to the canonical Q2. It is a defect only if the real ambition is to show that an extremely thin universe of satiable optimizers could be good. The draft currently vacillates between those theses. The matched-world route forces the distinction.

This is the strongest available route to the fixed thesis.

## Alternative 2: A substrate-and-culture permutation argument

Use a sequence of worlds rather than a literary redescription.

Begin with a future that everyone in the target audience accepts as good. Transform it in small steps:

1. Replace biological neurons with functionally equivalent artificial components.
2. Extend lifespans and alter temporal experience.
3. Replace inherited human languages with successor languages.
4. Permit artistic, social and moral practices to evolve over millions of years.
5. Remove dependence on distinctively terrestrial materials and environments.
6. Allow psychological and cultural divergence until none of the original human practices remains recognizable.
7. Preserve throughout whatever properties are independently identified as value-making: welfare, agency, understanding, achievement, reciprocal concern, creativity or another explicit list.

At each transition, ask which alteration makes the resulting future cease to be good. The opponent must either:

* identify a value-relevant discontinuity;
* accept gradual preservation of goodness;
* or claim that accumulated small changes create a threshold despite no individual value-relevant loss.

The final world contains no thick human values in the historical or cultural sense, but is connected to an agreed good world by a series of transformations preserving the identified value-makers.

### What this fixes

It converts the vague injunction to “remove the human vantage” into a burden-of-proof structure. The issue becomes whether biological origin, cultural genealogy or recognizable content is independently value-relevant.

It also avoids the objection that alien values have simply been stipulated as valuable. The argument begins with admitted goods and tracks them through transformations.

### What it cannot establish

It cannot show that a non-conscious solitary probe is good. It supports multiple realizability of flourishing, not value nihilism or axiological minimalism.

The route is therefore suitable for Q2 as officially stated, but not for the stronger claim that suffering avoidance and satiability alone suffice.

## Alternative 3: A Rawlsian constitutional route

This route is available if the real target is the thin constitutional commitment rather than Q2 itself.

Imagine a successor polity whose members do not know:

* whether they will be biological, digital, collective or hybrid;
* which generation they will occupy;
* their processing speed or lifespan;
* their eventual conception of the good;
* whether their future environment will be terrestrial, virtual or interstellar.

They choose constitutional constraints governing value formation. They might select:

* protection against severe suffering;
* freedom to revise ends;
* anti-domination;
* access to the resources required for agency;
* preservation of diverse future options;
* procedures for revising the constitution.

The conclusion would be that no particular human comprehensive doctrine should be constitutionally entrenched.

### What this fixes

It gives the criterion an operational decision procedure. It also explains why open-future constraints are selected rather than merely labelling them thin.

### Why it does not prove Q2

This is primarily a deontic and political argument. It concerns fair imposition and constitutional legitimacy. It does not establish that every outcome permitted by the constitution is good. A procedurally legitimate successor civilization could choose bad ends.

Use this route for Q3. Do not make it carry Q2.

# Task 3: The single strongest objection

> Your central inference is invalid. Impartiality is silent about the content of the good. “Do not privilege humans merely because they are human” does not entail “humanly exemplified goods are dispensable”. Friendship, understanding, aesthetic achievement, love and culture can be valued for properties that are neither species-indexed nor dependent on human endorsement. An impartial perfectionist can value them wherever they occur and judge a universe of solitary satiable probes profoundly deficient.
>
> Your thought experiment provides no positive account of what makes the probe-world good. It removes victims, removes competition, removes frustrated human interests, removes social comparison and then asks me to identify a surviving complaint. Even if I cannot, you have at most described a harmless or evaluatively indeterminate world. You have not described a good one.
>
> The evolutionary-debunking chapter cannot supply the missing premise. At best it undercuts some of my evidence for thinking that human relational goods are indispensable. An undercutting defeater for (p) is not evidence for not-(p), still less evidence for your positive proposition that the successor world is good.
>
> So identify the value-maker. What positive feature of the solitary probe or its universe makes that future good?

### The author’s likely reply

The probe has its own projects and values, operates autonomously, creates no suffering, is resource-satiable and remains open to developing a form of flourishing appropriate to its mode of existence.

### Follow-up

> Why does possessing projects or values make those projects valuable? The paperclip maximizer has projects. If you answer that the probe acquires knowledge, achieves difficult ends, exercises autonomy, experiences fulfilment or creates beauty, you have introduced a substantive objective-list or welfare theory. Those are not merely thin constraints, and the thought experiment now succeeds because alien thick goods replace human thick goods.
>
> If instead you rely only on non-production of suffering, satiability and open-endedness, you have described constraints against some forms of badness. You have still not identified a positive good. If the probe is not conscious, for whom does its autonomy or open future matter? If it is conscious, what theory explains why its life goes well?
>
> Your options are therefore: specify a positive axiology; retreat from “good” to “not demonstrably bad”; or concede that the real thesis is only that alien thick values may substitute for human thick values. Which one is the dissertation defending?

There is no adequate answer available in the present architecture. The third option is the most defensible and is already sufficient for the canonical Q2.

# Task 4: Missing interlocutors

## 1. G. E. Moore: isolation and organic unities

This is the most conspicuous omission. You are isolating complete possible worlds and asking what is valuable in them. Moore’s method of isolation is the canonical analytic treatment of that procedure. His account of organic unities also challenges any attempt to evaluate the probe-world by listing isolated components such as no suffering, satiability and autonomy; the value of a whole need not equal the sum of the values assigned to its parts. ([Fair Use Repository][11])

You need Moore whether you agree with him or not.

## 2. Population ethics: Parfit, Broome and Arrhenius

Parfit appears in the impartiality genealogy, but the relevant Parfit is Part IV of *Reasons and Persons*: non-identity, impersonal principles, future people and population comparison. Broome is needed for the neutral level and evaluation of variable populations. Arrhenius is needed because apparently minimal, impartial conditions generate impossibility results. ([PhilPapers][8])

A cosmic axiology that does not address population ethics will look structurally incomplete.

## 3. Good simpliciter versus good-for: Korsgaard, Darwall and Kraut

The chapter uses “good future”, “an individual’s good”, suffering, autonomy and impersonal loss without deciding how these notions relate. Korsgaard’s distinctions prevent some of the draft’s category shifts; Darwall gives a subject-centred account of welfare; Kraut denies that absolute goodness is the basic reason-giving notion. ([JSTOR][4])

This literature determines whether a non-conscious probe universe can contain value at all.

## 4. Natural goodness and forms of life: Philippa Foot and Michael Thompson

The draft repeatedly invokes forms of life, evolved functions and environment-relative capacities. Foot and Thompson offer the most direct analytic challenge: standards of goodness may be intelligible only against the characteristic organization and activities of a life-form. Foot explicitly develops goodness and defect as applying to living things considered under their characteristic form. ([PhilPapers][12]) Thompson develops the logical structure of life-form judgments and natural normativity. ([PhilPapers][13])

They could support your claim that alien forms require alien standards, but they also threaten the idea of a single maximally impartial criterion that evaluates all forms from outside.

## 5. Thick ethical concepts: Williams and McDowell

Because “thick values” is a central technical term, the established thick-concepts literature cannot be bypassed with a parenthesis saying the term is defined independently. Williams’s thick concepts are historically embedded, action-guiding, world-disclosing and resistant to decomposition into neutral description plus thin evaluation. That is closely related to your substantive problem, even though your use of “thick” differs.

Either engage this literature and explain the divergence or rename the category.

## 6. Kahane and the post-Street debunking literature

Citing Street and de Lazari-Radek and Singer is insufficient. Kahane is load-bearing because he directly questions the structure, scope and metaethical assumptions of evolutionary debunking arguments. ([Wiley Online Library][2])

You also need at least one sustained critic of selective debunking. Otherwise Chapter 5 risks being framed as though the only issue were identifying which intuitions have adaptive genealogies.

## 7. Scanlon, Taurek and anti-aggregation

The draft treats contractualism as part of a convergence on impersonality. The claims-based and anti-aggregation traditions show that giving each person equal moral status can oppose, rather than support, impersonal aggregation. The recent formulation that “each counts for one” does not determine how claims aggregate states the exact problem facing your criterion. ([PhilPapers][1])

This omission matters because “no arbitrary privileging” sounds like equal consideration, but equal consideration does not tell you how to rank universes.

## 8. Value pluralism and incomparability: Raz and Chang

The argument assumes that human and radically alien futures fall under a common ordering in which both can be judged “good”, worse or preferable. Value pluralists may instead regard sufficiently different forms of flourishing as incomparable or only imprecisely comparable. Raz’s work connects autonomy to access to an adequate range of valuable options, which is much more substantive than merely leaving an open future. ([PhilPapers][14]) Chang’s work is relevant to the assumption that radically different goods must share a common measure. ([PhilPapers][15])

This does not refute Q2. It may supply a better version of it: human and alien flourishing can both be good without being rankable on a single thin cosmic scale.

# Bottom-line diagnosis

The fixed thesis is defensible. The present route does not establish it.

The draft’s strongest defensible conclusion is:

> A future need not preserve the historically and culturally specific forms of human value, provided that non-human successors instantiate some adequate form of welfare, flourishing or impersonal value.

The draft presently reaches for a stronger conclusion:

> The absence of thick values may itself be unobjectionable, and thin constraints may suffice for a good future.

That stronger conclusion is unsupported. Impartiality does not supply the missing axiology; environmental inapplicability does not establish dispensability; and debunking does not turn lack of condemnation into positive value.

Unless a positive value-maker is introduced, the probe can function as a counterexample to human exceptionalism, but not as an example of a good future.
