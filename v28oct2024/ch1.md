## Introduction

This chapter picks 3 writers in the CCRU lineage that specifically engage with current AGI, in order to identify incoherencies or weaknesses in AI alignment's conception of "itself" (insofar as that is a sensible statement), specifically to gain purchase on the key questions of this project: a) the view-from-nowhere, and b) the limits of rationality in setting out values that are species-neutral and subject-independent

## Nick Land

Nick Land is a controversial figure, both for the things he says, and for the people who seem to gravitate towards his ideas, particularly around contemporary politics.^[See for instance the sources in this [post](https://cybertrophic.wordpress.com/2020/01/04/on-nick-land-the-weird-libertarian/?__readwiseLocation=), which set out his engagement with various online right-wing spaces as well as his views regarding race, as documented in his own writings.] These problematic associations and tendencies, which might have been unclear, inchoate, or not obviously present (and as [Wark 2017] points out, that is not necessarily the most interesting question) in the Land works (circa 1990s CCRU), become more troubling in the current global political context. Land is however a complex character, easy to cancel, but, as the [TU 2024] interview on a left-coded podcast showed, able to present arguments that merit analysis and rebuttal. Following [Wark 2017](https://www.versobooks.com/blogs/news/3284-on-nick-land), I set these later problematic views and associations aside, and focus on the ideas immediately relevant to the intertwined natures of capitalism and AI, largely sticking to Land's own writing, and CCRU-adjacent writers.

In the context of AI, why might he be interesting? I see him as a philosopher of the apocalypse, where the term is taken in its original, Greek $\alpha\pi o\kappa\alpha\lambda\upsilon\psi\iota\sigma$ (apokálypsis), which means “revelation” or “uncovering.”^[*Decide whether to have a small section on apocalyptic traditions across religions - islam, buddhism, hinduism, judaism, zoroastrianism all have this either in linear or cyclical forms, and even shinto has something like it. His teleological vision (which essentially is drawing on the usefulness of technology, as well as the efficacy of markets in resource allocation, as being unstoppable forces^[See below on the "social calculation problem"]), dark as it might be for many, mirrors aspects of the early conversations around AI risk, which would coalesce into the MIRI View and broader alignment discourse. In more contemporary missives, like [Land 2023], he points out seeming anthropocentric blind spots in the entire programme of AI risk. Land's critique goes roughly as follows: 

- The project of AI is to build a human-level machine intelligence or HLMI (quibbles about what this means temporarily set aside, whether it is a "player of games" *par excellence* or an all-knowing oracle, or something else)
- If one accepts something like the Bostrom/Yudkowsky  reasoning, such a thing would, with some non-negligiable probability, more or less quickly lead to a superintelligence (again, suspend questions on what exactly this is, but by definition, it sounds like an entity far beyond our cognitive capabilities) 
- Given such a superintelligence, the AI risk crowd (in Land's telling) purport to constrain the newborn being's volition to a narrow space, roughly corresponding to "preserving human values" or "not killing all humans"^[See Chapter 2 for these two formulations.]
- These formulations seem either incredibly vague or rather easy to misinterpret, or perhaps both. 
- Land's problem is however more fundamental, that the entire Bostrom-inflected framing (which influences subsequent alignment work) has a uniquely Occidental ring of intelligence and volition as distinct things; whereas, in Land's view, they are the same thing or at least very closely related.

Land's argument is problematic, in that although he criticises Bostrom (while cheering Omohundro), his criticisms aren't particularly precise or engaging with current AI research. A deeper problem is that (like for Bostrom, but perhaps not for Omohundro, and subsequent writers around alignment), intelligence, and more awkwardly, superintelligence are significant load-bearing terms in Land's arguments. But people who spend all their time researching this (whether in cognitive science, consciousness studies, or AI) can't seem to agree on what these concepts mean.^[*This begs substantiation*] Moreover, many of the problems he points out are actually hotly debated in the alignment discourse.


### Burning the cosmic endowment

Land's meld of economics, technology, and theory can be approached from many directions, but in context of future arguments around transhumanism and alignment, I consider his case for runaway capitalism as an expression, or perhaps perversion, of Bataille's "solar economy", which rhymes with the idea of "astronomical waste" and "burning the cosmic commons".^[*Check these are what I think they are, citations*]

#### From Bataille to capital

Land's Deleuzo-Guattarian inheritance is well-known; however, he also takes much from Bataille's "solar economy" ([Wark 2017]), which the latter (in *The Accursed Share*) frames as a source of excess—an abundance of energy (enters Earth’s biosphere in massive quantities, fueling all life and organic processes, but with it comes an overflow) that must be expended because it cannot be stored or accumulated indefinitely. Bataille believed that societies, like natural ecosystems, need ways to release this surplus energy. If not spent productively or aesthetically (in art, festivals, or luxury), excess energy leads to destructive outlets: violence, war, or environmental degradation. In the manner of potlachs and other generatively performative sumptuary acts^[*Examples and some sources...maybe the Debt book?*], Bataille saw this expenditure of energy as fundamental to the structure of life itself, a kind of inevitable “squandering” that arises from the abundance provided by the sun - but something that allows for beauty and transcendance.

Land reinterprets the solar economy as a model for understanding capital itself, however he focuses on the endpoint that Bataille made less of: the thermodynamic heat-death of the sun, and probably, the universe.^[*Was this actually an influence on him?*] Land's view leaned into this end, and framed capitalism as the avatar of the cosmic death-drive. He saw capital behaving like an impersonal, inhuman force that consumes and expends resources at an ever-accelerating rate, much like the surplus energy from the sun that Bataille describes. 

In this sense, Land sees capital as considerably more than merely a financial claim upon future resources^[Capital is of course a polysemous term, comprising economic/financial meanings (as stores of value), or as human/social capital (such as knowledge, networks of relationships, shared cultures, all of which enable people to get things done), as well as intellectual capital (a term that shows up more narrowly in contemporary neoliberal-inflected contexts). The Marxist view on capital is quite distinct: it is firstly "value in motion" ([Marx 1867]), and exists in a network of relations between producers and consumers, where production happens through the activation of privately-owned means (factories, mines, etc.) and human labour, with the mix only making economic sense if there is surplus value produced (excess of value over the cost of production which includes wages). This surplus i.e. the capitalist's profit, needs to be re-invested, and leads to accumulation.], as emerging and enabling a set of tendencies that, in practice, lead to its multiplication, accumulation, and separation from any fixed geographic, cultural, or social context (deterritorialisation).^[From his 2018 essay on [Bitcoin](https://sumrevija.si/en/sum10-2-nick-land-crypto-current-an-introduction-to-bitcoin-and-philosophy/): "disequilibrium, 'governed'—or, more strictly, made radically ungovernable—by a fundamental positive-feedback dynamic."] As Land says, this part of his reading is not as novel as it seems, in that he is just restating what Marx saw as the fusion of technology and economics, refracted through Bataille and Deleuze\&Guattari^[see this 2017 [post](https://obsoletecapitalism.blogspot.com/2017/05/nick-land-quick-and-dirty-introduction.html) for Land's definition of (de-)territorialisation and positive feedback loops as inherently nihilistic, and at least in Marx's view (albeit the latter was writing in the context of free trade rather than modern networked technology), the only way forward to achieve revolutionary social change. See also the chapter on Marx's *Fragment on the Machines*, as well as the contributions of Camatte, and Deleuze-and-Guattari in [Mackay 2014]].

As Land discusses in [TU 2024], Marx was writing for a very different world, where workers (at least in theory) owned their capacity for physical labour, and were able to contract for wages. That is, labour (initially physical but increasingly cognitive) was actually worth something. As he points out, in a future with increased automation where AI can do many cognitive or physical tasks, not all workers will have something economically useful to sell.^[This is an area of live debate, as to what the impact of AI will be on economies, and how quickly. See [Acemoglu 2024](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2024-05/The%20Simple%20Macroeconomics%20of%20AI.pdf), and a critique from [Tabarrok 2024](https://www.maximum-progress.com/p/contra-acemoglu-on-ai). A scenario-based approach can be found in [Korinek 2023](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/fandd/issues/2023/12/Scenario-Planning-for-an-AGI-future-Anton-korinek).]

#### Entropy's bridge

Bataille's formulation of the solar excess rhymes, perhaps coincidentally, with an the idea of a "cosmic endowment": a supply of low entropy energy that comes from the sun (and other stars).^[Entropy in this context is measured as the ratio of energy to temperature of the environment, which in context of solar radiation, is roughly commensurate with the solar temperature of ~5778 K, versus temperature at the Earth's surface, around 300 K. This massive difference, plus the fact that solar energy is highly collimated (which means it originates far away on the sun's surface, and still manages to reach us with great intensity), means it is more useful for work than waste heat (from say an engine) which is relatively close to the Earth's ambient temperature.] This energy, being low entropy, is useful (from a thermodynamic perspective) for work, unlike waste heat or other forms of disordered energy. Any intelligent civilization would likely see stellar energy as the most accessible source for sustaining and expanding life, especially as it moves beyond its home planet. From a speculative, stellar perspective, we could think of life, intelligence, and even complex civilizations as “powered” by such cosmic endowments. In this sense, we have a certain endowment of high-quality energy which could be used for planetary or stellar-scale engineering or creative works, the building of complexity, and the (local) reduction or resistance to entropy (which is one way of thinking about "what life is"). This is where Bataille's ideas (though not necessarily Landian nihilism, if that is a reasonable way of describing him) seem to resemble or be echoed in the extropian and transhuman-inflected futurist movements.^[*Check if bataille had any influence, reference max more, freeman dyson, bostrom/kurzweil konstantin tsiolkovsky...maybe go back to moynihan x-risk*]

#### Antecedents: the Austrian school
Part^[*This section should probably be joined with above?*] of Land's conceptual grounding for his view of markets as a type of distributed intelligence comes from economists (in one branch) of the "Austrian School", particularly Friedrich Hayek.^[*Cite at TU 2024 22:00, point out that neither Accelerate nor Fanged Noum actually mention Hayek, so is this lineage backfilled?*] These economists saw market economies as information processing systems that were distributed across many actors (people and firms), which often behaved irrationally and on imperfect information (much of it tacit knowledge as theorised by Michael Polanyi), but somewhow their aggregate activity allowed for efficient prices to be set. 

Hayek (and his collaborator Ludwig von Mises) was making his arguments in part as a counterpoint to the "socialist calculation problem", that is, the difficulty of finding the clearing price (i.e. the relative prices at which goods or services are efficiently allocated) for a given good/service in a complex economy, in a centralised way, because the preferences and constraints of various actors are distributed, are essentially "in their heads". Hayek's specific take was not just one of practicality (how is the central planner to find out these preferences), but epistemological in that these preferences, being tacit in many cases, are inherently incommunicable.^[*Would be good to find sources and get a LLM to check this; also mention Popper?*]

Land seems also to have incorporated ideas from Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk, who developed a more complex theory around types of capital stock that exist at multiple removes from the actual consumption object (e.g. the fishing net, or fishing boat, that is used to catch fish; at a second-order, the machines that produce these nets and boats; these innovations increase time and cost associated with fishing, but potentially increase productivity, and depending on one's view, might be emblematic of a tendency of capital to enable complexity).^[Land's actual written references-by-name to Austrian school economists are thin, other than this quote in *Teleoplexy* [Accelerate 2014]: "'Acceleration' as it is used here describes the time-structure of capital accumulation. It thus references the 'roundaboutness' founding Bohm-Bawerk's model of capitalization, in which saving and technicity are integrated within a single social process-diversion of resources from immediate consumption into the enhancement of productive apparatus. Consequently, as basic co-components of capital, technology and economics have only a limited, formal distinctiveness under historical conditions of ignited capital escalation." However, he explicitly credits Hayek *et al* in [TU 2024], and [Fitchett 2020] digs more into the Hayek connection.]

In a curious aside, the calculation problem in economies, far from being a pure academic dispute, was something that had concrete expression in the Cybersyn project of Stafford Beer (in Chile) and the rather different OGAS experiment in the USSR, championed by Viktor Glushkov. There is of course a broader field of study on the extent to which the ideas around Hayek, Mises, Popper, and others were deeply affected by both the aftermath of World War II and the Cold War's Manichean fight between capitalist (both US-style and social democratic) and Communist systems; however, this falls out of the scope of this project.^[*Maybe Medina's book? ##### Austrians and Austrians

The Hayekian branch of Austrians emphasised decentralised price-discovery and setting mechanisms, and as such, had a basically opposite orientation to the rational agency and game-theoretic origins of modern conceptual thinking about AGI (e.g. the MIRI View, discussed below). Recall that expected utility maximisation (under Von Neumann-Morgenstern) had 4 requirements: agents must have clear, consistent preferences, that can be ranked, the probabilities of which are known, and the agents have the computational ability to assess their choices.^[*Is this discussed in omohundro? Probably should be?*] Hayek's views, on the other hand, were founded upon information being practical or tacit, not being available centrally or in common, high levels of uncertainty, and agents that were often acting irrationally. Notwithstanding, Morgenstern, whose origins were with Mises, eventually (after meeting von Neumann) shed his early reticence about mathematical methods to find equilibrium prices (something Hayek continued to be skeptical of), and embraced what would be known as game theory, as a way to model situations with multiple agents, at least for certain classes of problems.^[*Substantiate*]

### Bostrom and Yudkowsky

#### Contra orthogonality

Land's argument, insofar as it can be translated into ordinary language (stripped of poetic flourishes and appeals to mysticism and arcana), rests approximately equally on markets and intelligence. So what does he think intelligence is?

In ([TU 2024], 17:35) he defined intelligence as "competence in playing games, with a very wide definition of games that encompasses most activity". But more usefully, and a crux of his disagreement with Bostrom is found in [Land 2023], *More Thought* where he refers to Bostrom's optimisation-based definition. 

>'For our purposes, “intelligence” will be roughly taken to correspond to the capacity for instrumental reasoning (more on this later). Intelligent search for instrumentally optimal plans and policies can be performed in the service of any goal.Intelligence and motivation can in this sense be thought of as a pair of orthogonal axes on a graph whose points represent intelligent agents of different paired specifications.'

That is, for Bostrom, intelligence and motivation are orthogonal or on perpendicular axes (metaphorically speaking). Land, in contrast, sees this as a false dichotomy; he understands thinking as being bound up with wanting-to-think, through a term "cybernetic closure". It isn't clear what relevance this has to Bostrom's point (that a highly intelligent being, whether human or machine, can have a unrestricted set of goals), but Land thinks of intelligence as recursive and reflexive, acting upon itself, as a "project" - the optimser is the optimised.^[Echo of Lem of the *Summa* or *Golem XIV*] He writes:

> "That there can be a thought of intelligence optimization, or even merely wanting to think, demonstrates a very different preliminary connection of intellect and volition. AI is concrete social volition, even before it is germinally intelligent, and a ‘program’ is strictly indeterminate between the two sides of this falsely fundamentalized distinction. Intelligence is a project, even when only a self-obscured bio-cognitive capability. This is what the Confucians designate by cultivation. It is a thought — and impulse — strangely alien to the West."

and 

>"Intelligence, to become anything, has to be a value for itself. Intellect and volition are a single complex, only artificially separated, and not in a way that cultivates anything beyond misunderstanding."

It's hard to definitively say what he means by these statements, other than the Bostromian values-competence binary is inadequate and inappropriate; Land seems just to be restating his point that intelligence is itself a fundamental force of nature.^[*Add something on information theory of universe, Popper, etc. and tag to future chapter. Also check his reading of Bostrom is actually correct.*]

Land's Confucian reference is curious in that "cultivation" in ancient Chinese thought is a major concept of how the individual develops themself.^[*Find sources and see if Japanese connection to Kyoto School? See question in Claude convo 'Land/Bostrom'*]

#### The paperclipper as a false is-ought

Land, in the essay *Stupid Monsters* ([Land 2023], pp. 238-239), and also in *Against Orthogonality* (pp. 137-138) reframes Bostrom's orthogonality thesis as a species of Humean is/ought binary ("is" being the cognitive capability, and "ought" the goals or values towards which it is directed). Land (in an echo of the critique from Confucianism above) sees this as a peculiarly Occidental separation beween facts and values, between practical activity and self-reflection (i.e. "thought is an activity" (p. 238)).

In this framing, Bostrom's thought experiment is (according to Land) a contradiction in terms: a superintelligence that pursues such a manifestly pointless goal (manufacturing paperclips) is incoherently discussed as a superintelligence. Even allowing for the fact that Bostrom's example was intentionally oversimplified, Land's critique would seem to hold for many types of simply-stated (in terms of short description length) goals (e.g. some of (my) examples would be "maximising economic growth", "controlling the solar system's resources", etc.) Here I think we are seeing Land's distrust of top-down simple prescriptions (which he discusses in [TU 2024] in an economic context regarding Marx and Hayek) in favour of the complexity and disorder of the world (or nature) as the only worthwhile value or process of note.

In the same essay, Land makes further arguments against Bostrom's example, based on the human evolution under "Darwinian imperatives" of intense selection pressure over billions of years, resulting in a deeply integrated form of intelligence-volition focused on survival and reproduction. Land considers nonsensical the idea that we could engineer an AI with a simpler, more constrained volitional structure than what evolution produced over billions of years. Land's critique is of Bostrom's negative example of the paperclip, but it could also be read as a critique of alignment: what is the project of designing safe AI, if not of building systems more capable than humans -- that nevertheless have more or less restricted volition than humans.^[*This feels like a pretty big point and should be trumpeted / referenced elsewhere*]

In the essay *Pythia Unbound* ([Land 2023], pp. 110-111) he directly takes aim at the project of alignment (read as constraining a more powerful intelligence to prioritise human welfare), in context of Bostrom's "oracle" AI (cast in an earlier essay by Bostrom as the Pythia at Delphi)^[*Cite essay and beef up oracle/genie/etc. section of alignment chapter*]. Land writes:

>"To depict Pythia as vastly smarter than us and yet still hard-slaved to her instincts, *in a way that we're not* - that simply doesn't compute. Intelligence is escape, with a tendency to do its own thing. That's what runaway means, as a virtual mind template. Omohundro explains the basics." (p. 111)

#### Enslaving the Other

The ideas in *Pythia Unbound*, while specifically about AI, recall his analysis of global capitalism as a mechanism that is at once exploitative (of labour from the developing world) and exclusionary (of that same labour when it seeks to freely migrate). >"An enlightenment society wants both to learn and to legislate for all time, to open itself to the other and to consolidate itself from within, to expand indefinitely whilst reproducing itself as the same....The paradox of enlightenment, then, is an attempt to fix a stable relation with what is radically other, since insofar as the other is rigidly positioned within a relation it is no longer fully other." >Gnon is no less than reality, whatever else is believed. Whatever is suspended now, without delay, is Gnon. Whatever cannot be decided yet, even as reality happens, is Gnon. #### Statistical ontology junk

Complicating his views on time and looping, in [Land 2023] he takes time to criticise "statistical ontology junk" and "concentrated EDT", i.e. the anthropic analysis and acausal decision theories that often crop up in discussions of existential risk, suffering risk, thinking about how superintelligences (or maximally rational agents) might behave.^[*Need sources for all of this, and link back to EFAI (maybe)*].

For instance, in *In the Mouth of Madness*, he describes Roko's Basilisk: a thought experiment (dating from a LessWrong post of [2010](https://rationalwiki.org/wiki/Roko%27s_basilisk/Original_post#Comments_.28117.29)) where a future AI, one that is beneficient^[*"Beneficent" in a "coherent extrapolated volition" sense, as formulated by Yudkowsky [Bostrom 2014], pp. 259-266*] with respect to people living in that future and thereafter (e.g. by curing diseases, eliminating want, etc.) discovers in its training data or simply by checking the internet, that there were alignment researchers who knowingly tried to prevent it from coming into being (by slowing down or halted AI research). A straightforward utilitarian calculation (of the value of the future lives towards which the AI was beneficently disposed) would imply that the AI ought to retaliate against those researchers (who blocked its creation) by simulating vast numbers of them and torturing them for infinity. If one accepts that (beneficent) AI could exist and that knowingly not helping future people is bad, and if one is also sensitive to the plight of simulated beings (particularly copies of oneself), then the *very knowledge of this possibility* obliges one to work towards producing such an AI.^[This makes assumptions about discount rates for future lives, as well as whether simulated beings are equivalent to current ones, and about indexical suffering, and is generally thought, both by Yudkowsky at-the-time, and upon reflection by others, to be flawed. It also relies on open questions in decision theory (see [Chapter ...])]

In the Yudkowsky post regarding Roko's Basilisk, Yudkowsky, in his customary overwrought way, banned discussion of the problem on LessWrong (on the grounds that it was an information hazard), which not surprisingly turned it into an online meme.^[The full history can be found on LessWrong [here](https://www.lesswrong.com/tag/rokos-basilisk).] 

As Yudkowsky writes:

>"When Roko posted about the Basilisk, I very foolishly yelled at him, called him an idiot, and then deleted the post. Why I did that is not something you have direct access to, and thus you should be careful about Making Stuff Up, especially when there are Internet trolls who are happy to tell you in a loud authoritative voice what I was thinking, despite having never passed anything even close to an Ideological Turing Test on Eliezer Yudkowsky. 

>"Why I yelled at Roko: Because I was caught flatfooted in surprise, because I was indignant to the point of genuine emotional shock, at the concept that somebody who thought they'd invented a brilliant idea that would cause future AIs to torture people who had the thought, had promptly posted it to the public Internet." (Reddit via [LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/tag/rokos-basilisk))

The reason discussion could be an information hazard is based upon self-reference: a beneficent superintelligence would (in Yudkowsky's formulation) be optimising for humanity's "coherent extrapolated volition" (CEV), meaning that it should incentivise people to work towards its creation by threatening to punish those who were aware of the possibility of its existence but did not do everything in their power to bring it about. Mere discussion of the Basilisk implies an individual and their discussants were aware that a) such as beneficient AI was possible, b) they were considering acting to prevent creation of such AI, and c) the more this idea is discussed, the more it enters training data making it likely that the future AI would be aware of human attempts to forestall its creation. In this sense, the future (even an imagined one from a thought-experiment) might affect the present, in a hyperstitional loop.

Land's problem was less with the idea of the Basilisk, which fit nicely in his world-view ("Wandering through the psych ward, past rows of neurologically-shattered Turing Cops, broken deep in their minds by something unspeakable that came at them out of the near future." [Land 2023] p. 159), and more with Yudkowsky's censorship of the seemingly obvious logical extrapolations of rationalist ideas. That is, Land was again pointing out the inherent contradictions, born of a residual attachment to humanism, at the heart of the rationalist project (at least as documented on LessWrong and adjacent spaces).
